You wake up in a completely different era, with no modern technology around. As you explore your new surroundings, you realize you need to find shelter and something to eat before nightfall, because the forest around you is already growing darker and the air has that sharp, damp chill that means rain is coming.

You’re wearing coarse wool and leather that smells of woodsmoke. No phone, no watch, no plastic, nothing familiar. A dirt path winds between tall oaks toward the sound of voices and a hammer ringing on metal. You follow it into a small village of timber-and-thatch houses. People stare at your clothes and your clean-shaven face, then look away quickly, muttering.

An older woman hanging laundry by a well studies you longer than the others. She speaks a language you almost recognize—something between English and German, full of hard consonants. When you try a few words she understands enough to point you toward a barn at the edge of the settlement. Inside, hay is stacked high and a pot of barley stew sits over a low fire. She leaves you a wooden bowl and a blanket that itches.

That night you lie awake listening to rain on the thatch and the distant howl of something that is definitely not a dog. You realize two things at once: you have no idea how you got here, and if you want to last more than a few days you’ll have to learn how these people live—how they make fire without matches, how they keep food from spoiling, how they stay warm when the real winter comes.

The dream doesn’t end when you close your eyes. It just keeps going, one ordinary, difficult day after another, until the village starts to feel almost like a place you could belong… or until you finally wake up.
