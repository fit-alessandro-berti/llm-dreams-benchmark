build a garden in the ruins of the old library.

The roof is gone, so the rain comes in freely, pooling in the broken reading rooms. The shelves are toppled and the books are swollen with mold, but the soil you haul in buckets from the riverbank is dark and alive. You plant the seeds you’ve been carrying in a tin in your pocket for longer than you can remember — tomatoes, beans, sunflowers impossibly tall.

Every morning you walk the perimeter you made from shopping carts and bent street signs, not to keep people out, but to keep the wild dogs from trampling the sprouts. Every evening you light a small oil lamp and place it in the library’s highest broken window, though you tell yourself no one is left to see it.

On the seventh night, someone does.

She is maybe ten years old, thin as a reed, with a backpack larger than she is and a crow perched solemnly on her shoulder. She stands at your fence of carts and stares at the green shoots pushing up between the fallen encyclopedias.

“Are those real?” she asks.

You nod, and dig your fingers into the earth. A bean sprout has curled overnight into a perfect, impossible spiral. As you watch, it keeps growing — dream-fast — unfurling leaves that sound faintly like turning pages when the wind moves them. The sunflowers along the back wall are already taller than you, their faces turned not toward the ash-gray sky, but toward your lamp.

The girl climbs through the carts without waiting for permission. From her backpack she produces treasures: a dented watering can, a packet of carrot seeds softened by years, half a bottle of clean water, and a book with all its words intact.

Others come after that, drawn by the light. An old man who remembers how to fix pumps. Two brothers who know which rooftops still catch rain that is safe to drink. A woman who sings while she works, and whose singing makes the tomatoes ripen redder.

You trade seeds for stories. You trade stories for shelter. The library fills again — not with silence, but with the scrape of shovels, the bubbling of soup made from your first thin harvest, and children reading aloud by lamplight among the tomato vines.

One night, the crow speaks, as crows do in dreams. “It’s enough now,” it says.

The garden is glowing softly. Every leaf, every fruit, every sunflower is giving off a warm, golden light that pushes back the ruined dark for miles. You can see roads reforming in that light. You can see other gardens, other lamps, answering from distant hills.

You climb the broken stairs to the window and look out over the world you decided not to abandon.

And as the first real sun in years rises over the wasteland, you feel the dream begin to loosen — but your hands are still warm, and still smell of soil.
