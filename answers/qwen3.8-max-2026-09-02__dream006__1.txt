At first you freeze, certain everyone will be horrified. But the crowd falls completely silent, watching the moths spiral upward like tiny lanterns. Each one lands gently on someone’s shoulder, and wherever it touches, that person smiles as if remembering something precious they had forgotten.

You look down at your notes and realize the paper is now covered in moving drawings: your childhood home, a door you never opened, a friend you lost touch with, a version of yourself standing calmly in the center of it all.

When you look back up, the audience is no longer an audience. They are standing in rows that stretch endlessly into a night sky, and every face is someone you have ever known—or will know someday.

You understand, without being told, that the speech was never meant to be spoken. It was meant to be released.

So you open your mouth again, and this time a whole flock of moths pours out, filling the air with light. The crowd begins to applaud—not with their hands, but with their hearts, each beat sounding like distant thunder.

As the sound grows louder, the stage beneath your feet turns into water, and you sink slowly into it, not afraid, because the water is warm and full of stars.

Then you wake up, still hearing applause somewhere far away, and for a moment you feel certain that, in the dream, you finally said exactly what you needed to say.
