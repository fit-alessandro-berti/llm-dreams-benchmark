You feel a storm approaching and the air grows heavy, charged with a static hum that vibrates in your chest. The dappled sunlight filtering through the canopy vanishes, replaced by bruised, slate-gray clouds that swallow the sky. The birds fall silent. In their place, a low, resonant rush begins—not just wind, but something deeper, like the forest itself drawing a slow, steady breath.

A single drop falls, striking your palm with a sound like a struck bell. The sky cracks open.

But instead of soaking rain, what pours down are thousands of pale, luminous seeds, drifting like slow fireflies through the suddenly violent wind. The trees do not bend or break; their bark ripples like water, their leaves turning to glass and chiming in the gale. The storm isn't tearing through the woods—it's walking through it.

You step forward as the ground softens into moss that pulses faintly with each footfall. The wind tugs at you, but it doesn't push; it guides. Around you, the trees lean inward to part a path, their branches interlocking like a natural cathedral arch. Lightning flashes, not as jagged white splits, but as broad washes of violet light that briefly illuminate a clearing ahead.

In the center of the clearing stands a pool of perfectly still water, reflecting a sky that no longer matches the one above you. In the reflection, the storm is already gone. Soft light spills across a field of wildflowers, and for a moment, the scent of petrichor and distant rain-soaked earth wraps around you like a memory you've always known.

You don't run from the weather. You step into it. The wind carries you across the threshold between the woods and the water, and the dream gently folds in on itself, leaving only the quiet hum of a world that has shifted, just enough, to make room for you.
