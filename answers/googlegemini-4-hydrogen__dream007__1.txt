You wrench the front door open, expecting the familiar chill of the evening air, only to step out onto a narrow wooden pier stretching across a pitch-black, mirror-still lake. Behind you, the door clicks shut, and when you turn around, the house is gone—replaced by a towering pile of old, mismatched suitcases.

Your heart is still hammering in your ribs, your throat tight with unspoken rebuttals. You look down and realize you’re holding your shoes in your hands, and both of them are brimming with cold rainwater. 

Across the dark water, a faint amber glow flickers. Your friend is standing on a floating wooden dock fifty yards away, shouting something back at you. Their voice doesn’t travel across the water; instead, it crackles quietly from a brass intercom bolted to a lamppost right beside you. You suddenly realize the argument isn't about what you thought it was—it's about who forgot to wind the brass key that keeps Tuesday from happening twice. 

Panic flares up in your chest. You need to fix this, to explain that you didn't mean it, so you try to run down the pier toward them. But your legs move as if you're wading through waist-deep honey. Every frantic step barely shifts you an inch forward, and the wooden planks beneath your bare feet begin to soften, turning into damp, packed sand. 

You reach into your pocket to call them, pulling out your phone, only to find the screen is an analog clock with no hands. Frustrated, you look back up—and your friend is suddenly standing three feet in front of you. 

The anger has completely evaporated from both of you, replaced by a heavy, inexplicable exhaustion. You're both wearing thick winter coats, though the air feels like a humid July night. Neither of you says a word. They simply reach out and hand you a warm ceramic mug. You take a sip, and it tastes faintly of salt and freshly cut grass. 

Far off in the fog, a low, resonant train whistle blows, even though there are no tracks anywhere in sight. You look down at the mug, and when you glance back up, your friend is gone—only the faint echo of their laugh remains, drifting slowly out across the quiet water as the sky begins to turn a blinding, papery white.
