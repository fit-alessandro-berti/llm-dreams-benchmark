You are in a world where colors and shapes are constantly changing. As you explore this surreal landscape, you come across a blank canvas and a single brush lying in the grass, which is not grass exactly but a shifting field of green triangles that ripple like water when you step through them.

The canvas is the only still thing here. Everything else — the sky cycling from violet to amber, the hills folding themselves into cones and then softening into spheres — moves and breathes, but the canvas stays white and silent, like a held breath. You understand, the way you understand things in dreams without being told, that the canvas is why the world cannot settle. It is waiting to be painted, and until it is, everything must keep auditioning: every color trying itself out, every shape offering a possibility.

You pick up the brush. It has no bristles, only a small point of warmth, like the memory of holding someone's hand.

You touch it to the canvas and the whole landscape leans in. The hills stop mid-fold. The sky pauses on a shade of blue-gray you have seen exactly once before, through a window, on an afternoon you had forgotten until now.

You paint a line. It isn't a line of color — the brush pulls color out of the world instead of putting it on. Where you paint, the chaos around you quiets. The stroke on the canvas becomes a horizon, and at once the real horizon steadies itself to match. You paint a small circle, and the sun, which had been wandering the sky like a lost coin, settles into place. You paint a crooked little house, and behind you, you hear a door creak open on hinges that did not exist a moment ago.

With every stroke the world grows calmer and the canvas grows fuller, and you begin to feel the strange weight of it: you are not decorating this place, you are deciding it. The triangles of grass wait to see if you will let them stay triangles. Part of you wants to fix everything — nail down every hill, name every color, finish the painting so completely that nothing can ever shift again.

You lift the brush and stop.

Because you realize you would miss it. The restlessness. The sky trying on amber like a coat it might not buy. The mountains practicing being mountains.

So you leave one corner of the canvas blank. A small white square, no bigger than your palm, in the lower left.

The world exhales. Through that unpainted corner all the unfinished changes pour like light through a keyhole — and everything settles into a gentle, breathing balance: mostly steady, but shimmering at the edges, the way summer air shimmers over a road.

The house behind you is fully there now. Someone inside is setting a table; you can hear the soft knock of cups. You lean the brush against the canvas, and the canvas accepts it, absorbing it slowly like water absorbing a drop of ink, until the brush is only a painted brush, leaning against a painted easel, inside the painting of the world you are standing in.

You look down. Your own hands are brushstrokes. They always were.

Somewhere the unfinished corner glimmers, and you walk toward the house, changing slightly with every step, and glad of it.
