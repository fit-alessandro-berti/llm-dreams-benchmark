You are feeling lost and alone in a bustling city. Suddenly, a group of kind strangers offers to help you find your way, and you accept before you even know what you are looking for.

There are five of them, or maybe seven — the number keeps shifting the way numbers do in dreams, and it doesn't seem to matter. One is an old woman with a coat full of pockets, and each pocket holds a small brass key. One is a boy carrying an umbrella that drips upward, the rain rising off it in slow silver beads. The tallest of them, a man whose face you can never quite look at directly, says, "You dropped your street. We saw it happen. Come on, we know where they keep the lost ones."

You walk with them and the city rearranges itself politely to let you pass. Buildings lean back like commuters making room. The traffic sounds soften into something almost choral. You try to explain that you don't remember your address, that you're not even sure this is your city, and the old woman pats your arm and says, "Nobody remembers their address. That's what the rest of us are for."

They lead you down an avenue that narrows into a lane, then into a corridor that smells like your grandmother's kitchen, though you can't say which grandmother, or whether you ever stood in that kitchen at all. The walls are papered with maps — not of the city, but of conversations, with little dotted lines showing where people meant to say one thing and said another. The boy with the umbrella traces one of the dotted lines with his finger and says, "This one's yours. See? You got lost right here, ages ago. Everything since has just been walking."

You want to cry, but in the dream it comes out as laughter, and the strangers laugh with you, kindly, as if this is the correct exchange rate.

At the end of the corridor there is a door with your name on it, except the name is spelled the way you spelled it as a child, with a letter backwards. The old woman tries her keys one by one. None of them fit, and she seems delighted by this. "Good," she says. "It was never locked." She pushes the door open with one finger.

Inside is not a room but an evening: a small square with a fountain, string lights sagging between lampposts, a table set for exactly the number of people present, including you. The bustle of the city is still there, but now it surrounds the square like a warm blanket instead of a wall. You recognize the place with your whole body before your mind catches up — not because you have been here, but because you have been homesick for it your entire life without knowing.

"You weren't lost," says the tall man, pulling out a chair for you. "You were early. We've all been waiting for you to arrive."

You sit. Someone pours something warm into your cup. The boy folds his impossible umbrella and the rain it collected drifts up past the string lights and becomes, unremarkably, stars.

You are about to ask them their names when you realize you already know them — they are everyone who was ever briefly kind to you and then gone: the woman who gave you directions once, the man who held a door, a classmate whose face you'd forgotten. They have all been here the whole time, keeping your seat.

The old woman raises her cup. "To being found," she says.

And just as you raise yours, the fountain's sound becomes your alarm, or your breathing, or the morning traffic outside a window — and you wake with the strange, certain comfort of someone who knows the way back.
