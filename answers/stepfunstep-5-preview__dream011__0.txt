…written in your own handwriting.

Not the careful, blockish print you learned in third grade, but the slanted, hurried scrawl you only use when taking notes at two in the morning. The ink is still wet. It smells like ozone and old library books.

The first question asks: *What is the exact weight of the lie you told your mother in 1998?*

You read it twice. Your pencil feels enormous in your fingers, heavy as a crowbar. When you look down again, the question has rearranged itself: now it reads *Define the color of your brother’s voice on the day he left.* Beneath it, the multiple-choice options are not letters but flavors—vanilla, copper, Tuesday.

You lift your hand to ask the proctor for a fresh exam booklet. But there is no proctor at the front of the room. There is only a mirror the size of a chalkboard, and in it you see yourself, older, wearing the outfit you will wear on the day you finally give up. Your reflection isn’t taking the exam. It is watching you with pity.

The desk is breathing. You can feel it against your forearms, a slow, papery respiration.

Question 3: *Draw a map of every place you have ever been lost.* You start to sketch your childhood block, but the graphite line keeps growing, snaking past the margins, down your wrist, into your sleeve. By the time you look up, the classroom walls have become floor-to-ceiling windows, and outside it is not a campus but the interior of a vast, humming machine, all gears spinning backward.

The final question is printed in red at the bottom of the page, and it is very simple.

*Describe your future career.*

There is no answer key. There is only a blank blue book in the corner of your desk that smells like rain, and the sudden, suffocating realization that you have been preparing for this your whole life—and are only just now beginning to understand the questions.
