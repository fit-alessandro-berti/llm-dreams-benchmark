...find the clockmaker before the bells stop ringing.

I don't know how I know this. It's simply *true*, the way things are true in dreams: settled and unquestionable, sitting in my chest like a swallowed stone.

The street beneath me is packed earth and straw, still damp from a rain I don't remember. Timber-framed houses lean toward each other overhead, their upper floors nearly touching, so the sky is only a thin blue ribbon. The air smells of woodsmoke, horses, and baking bread, and something sharper underneath, like iron or old coins.

I reach for my pocket out of habit, for my phone. Instead my fingers close around a brass key, warm as if someone just handed it to me. Its teeth are shaped like tiny birds.

A bell tolls somewhere above the rooftops. *One.*

A woman in a gray wool dress passes with a basket of eggs, and I ask her where the clockmaker lives. She looks at me with pity, the way you'd look at someone asking directions to their own house.

"You've been there already," she says. "You just haven't gone yet."

Then she's gone, and the eggs she dropped have hatched into brown sparrows that scatter up the lane. I follow them because it seems like the right thing to do.

*Two.*

The lane twists in ways that shouldn't be possible. I pass a blacksmith hammering a horseshoe that glows blue instead of orange. I pass a fountain where the water flows upward and disappears into the ribbon of sky. I pass a boy selling newspapers with no words on them, only drawings of my face at different ages: seven, fifteen, twenty-three, and one I don't recognize, older, with a scar along the jaw.

"Latest edition!" he calls. "Read all about you!"

*Three.*

The sparrows lead me to a narrow door painted the deep green of pine needles. There's no sign, just a keyhole shaped exactly like my key. The ticking coming through the wood is layered and enormous, hundreds of clocks all slightly out of step, like rain on a tin roof.

*Four.*

Inside, the workshop is bigger than the building could possibly hold. Clocks cover every surface and climb the walls to a ceiling I can't see: grandfather clocks, cuckoo clocks, pocket watches dangling like fruit from wire branches, a sundial that somehow works indoors. At a long workbench sits an old man with magnifying lenses stacked over one eye, gently tightening a gear no bigger than a grain of rice.

He doesn't look up. "You're late. Or early. It's hard to tell with you."

"Why am I here?" I ask. "What era *is* this?"

He finally sets down his tweezers and turns. With a small shock I realize he has my eyes, and the scar along the jaw.

"It isn't an era," he says. "It's the moment before you decided something. You've been circling it for a long time. I built this whole town so you'd have somewhere to walk while you made up your mind."

*Five.*

"Decided what?"

He points to a clock on the bench. It's plain and wooden, and it has no hands at all.

"That's yours. The key winds it. Once it runs, you go back. Everything you left will be there: the noise, the screens, the hurry. But you'll carry this with you." He taps his chest. "The part of you that knows how to walk slowly down a crooked street and follow sparrows."

*Six.*

I look at the key, then the clock. Outside, the bells are slowing, the gaps between the tolls stretching like held breath.

"And if I don't wind it?"

He smiles, and it's the kindest, saddest smile I've ever seen. "Then you stay and learn to fix clocks. It's not a bad life. I should know."

*Seven.*

I fit the key into the clock. It turns easily, as though it had been waiting for me. Inside, something catches, and a soft steady ticking begins, slower than a heartbeat, patient.

Hands bloom across the clock face like frost on a window.

*Eight*—

I wake up. My alarm is going off, and my phone is buzzing on the nightstand with the usual flood of notifications. But for a long moment I just lie there, listening to rain on the window, not reaching for anything at all.

On my pillow, impossibly, is a single brown feather.
