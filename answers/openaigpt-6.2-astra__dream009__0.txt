The blacksmith has one tucked behind his ear. A woman selling plums wears hers on a ribbon around her neck. Even the baby asleep in the handcart has a little wooden spoon tied to its wrist.

“Have you lost yours?” the plum seller asks.

“I don’t think I brought one.”

“Oh,” she says, with a tenderness that frightens you. “You’ve come a long way, then.”

She gives you hers.

At noon the bells ring, and everyone gathers beside a dry well. One by one, they lean over it and tap their spoons against the stones. Each tap brings up a small sound: rain on a roof, a horse nickering, somebody laughing so hard they can’t finish a sentence.

When your turn comes, you strike the rim.

From far below, you hear your father in the kitchen of your childhood home. He is singing the wrong words to a song, as he always did. Then comes the scrape of a chair and the soft click of the stove turning off.

You had forgotten that last sound. Of all things, it undoes you.

The plum seller waits beside you until you can speak. “Can I go down?” you ask.

“No. But you can send something back.”

You bend close. For a moment you try to think of something important enough to say across all those years. In the end you just call, “Save me a pancake.”

Far below, a plate settles on the table.

The well goes quiet. People drift back to their stalls. You offer the spoon to the plum seller, but she closes your fingers around it.

“Keep it,” she says. “I live here.”

You wake in your own bed, your hand curled around nothing. It’s still early. In the kitchen you find the old mixing bowl, blue around the rim, and for the first time in years you make too much batter on purpose.
