You don’t know how you know this. The knowledge is simply there, like a song whose tune you remember but whose words you’ve forgotten.

The city around you is built of sun-baked brick. Merchants lead donkeys through narrow streets, water sellers carry clay jars on their shoulders, and smoke rises from courtyards where bread bakes over open fires. Everyone speaks a language you have never learned, yet you understand every word.

At the market, a boy notices your strange clothes. Instead of staring, he takes your hand.

“She’s been waiting,” he says.

He leads you through a maze of alleys to a door painted the deep blue of twilight. Inside, an old woman sits at a loom, weaving a length of black cloth scattered with tiny points of light. One corner remains unfinished.

“There you are,” she says, as though you are merely late for supper. “Your thread came loose.”

She places the shuttle in your hand. On the cloth, among the shimmering lights, you recognize constellations—and then whole landscapes: forests, oceans, cities, faces. Somehow, the fabric contains more than could possibly exist.

“Every age is woven from what the age before it managed to remember,” she explains. “To go home, you must add one thing your world cannot afford to forget.”

You think of its great inventions, its towers, its machines that cross oceans and speak across continents. But none of those feel right.

Instead, you remember a small kitchen at night: rain ticking against the window, two cups of tea going cold, and someone you love laughing so hard they cannot finish their story.

You weave that.

The cloth trembles. A new point of light appears, faint but steady. Outside, the city begins to dissolve into gold.

“Will I remember this?” you ask.

“Only as much as you need.”

You wake in your own bed, morning pressed against the curtains. For several minutes, you lie still, afraid to move and disturb the last traces of woodsmoke and warm bread.

Around your wrist is a single blue thread.

When you touch it, it vanishes—but somewhere, very faintly, you hear a loom begin again.
