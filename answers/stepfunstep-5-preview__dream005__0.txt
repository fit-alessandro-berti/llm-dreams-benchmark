You clutch a glass of champagne that is somehow warm and tastes like static. The room is a townhouse that keeps rearranging itself when you aren’t looking—hallways elongating into ballrooms, staircases folding upward into darkness. You only know one person here. Elena. You need to find her before the evening turns on its hinge and becomes something else entirely.

In the entryway, a circle of people is laughing at a joke no one has told yet. They wear name tags that are blank. As you pass, they part like a tide, and one of them—smiling too widely—says, “She went upstairs to find you,” in a voice that sounds like a door closing.

You climb. But the stairs deliver you to a dining room where a dozen guests are arguing passionately over a painting on the wall. The canvas is blank. “It’s clearly a different blue than yesterday,” someone insists, pointing at nothing. Another guest looks up, and for a moment their face is yours. They turn away before you can speak.

Behind a beaded curtain, you find a group sitting on the floor in a circle, all of them whispering secrets into their palms. They are cupping the words like birds, and when you ask if they’ve seen Elena, they open their hands in unison. Nothing flies out. Only dust, catching the light. “Check the garden,” they chorus.

There is a garden now, indoors, impossible: a lawn of moss growing through marble tiles, the ceiling lost to a violet sky with too many stars. People lie in the grass with their eyes closed, dressed for a gala, listening to music you can’t hear. One of them opens an eye and says, “She was just here,” but their mouth doesn’t move.

You keep walking. Rooms fold into other rooms. A kitchen where no one is cooking, only watching a pot boil. A library where everyone reads the same book, and the words rearrange themselves when you try to glimpse the pages. There is a small door you hadn’t noticed before, barely ajar, the width of a breath. Inside is a narrow room that smells like rain on hot pavement and Elena’s shampoo. She is sitting on a windowsill, feet dangling into the violet sky, swirling a drink that matches yours.

“You’re late,” she says, but she’s smiling.

“I don’t know anyone here,” you say, the admission tumbling out.

“Neither do I,” Elena says. “I just keep pretending they’re all people I knew in childhood.”

She pats the windowsill beside her. You sit. Outside, the glass frames the entire party like a diorama: the blank painting, the secret-whisperers, the frozen dancers. From this distance, the noise is muffled, underwater, meaningless.

“I didn’t want to be alone,” you say.

Elena hands you her glass. The static is gone. It tastes like the first day of summer vacation. “Then don’t be.”

You both look out at the gathering. Already the stars are beginning to dissolve into morning light, and the walls are loosening their geometry. The party is thinning, becoming a rumor. Elena’s shoulder presses against yours, warm and solid, the only anchor in the drifting house.

“Ready?” she asks.

You nod, and together you lean back into the window, into the sky, while the room behind you folds quietly away, like a note slipped back into a pocket.
