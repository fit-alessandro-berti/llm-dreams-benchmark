In the opening round, you're handed a clock that runs backward and told to assemble a jigsaw puzzle of a horizon you've never seen. Every time you fit a piece into place, it dissolves into a small, glowing bird that flies off into the rafters of a stadium made entirely of glass. You soon realize the puzzle isn't meant to be finished; it's meant to be released. When you stop trying to control the pieces and simply watch them fly, the birds circle back and form the picture in the air—a shifting, luminous map of somewhere you've always wanted to go.

The second challenge drops you into an elevator with no buttons, only mirrors. As it plunges downward, the reflections begin to argue with you. One version of you is frantic, clutching a stack of rejection letters; another is confident, wearing a suit made of solid gold. The doors open onto a tightrope strung between two skyscrapers labeled *Expectation* and *Reality*. You don't walk the line. Instead, you sit down on the edge, dangle your legs into the void, and invite your reflections to join you. The tightrope widens into a hammock, and the arguing falls silent.

By the semifinals, the challenges have grown strangely intimate. You're given a microphone in a room made of soundproof foam and asked to give a presentation to an audience of your own past mistakes. They are small, awkward-looking creatures with giant, attentive ears. Instead of hiding or apologizing, you start telling them what you learned from each one. They lean in, mesmerized, and begin to shrink until they fit comfortably in the palm of your hand, warm and harmless.

Then comes the final round. The arena falls silent. The other competitors—a mosaic of shifting shapes and bright, nervous ideas—fade into mist. A single desk appears, piled high with contracts, titles, and golden badges. The Head Judge, a towering figure wrapped in a cloak of shifting deadlines, hands you a pen that writes in invisible ink.

"Sign here," they say, their voice like the rustle of paper, "and the job is yours forever."

You look at the contract. The invisible ink begins to glow, revealing not terms and conditions, but a single, handwritten question: *What would you do if no one was watching?*

You smile. You set the pen down, walk around the desk, and simply begin to do the thing you love—humming a off-key tune, sketching on the back of the paper, completely losing track of time. The cloak of deadlines unravels into ribbons. The desk melts into a warm, sunlit workspace with a slightly crooked chair. 

And as the dream begins to blur at the edges, warm and heavy like morning sleep, you realize the grand prize was never the job itself. It was the feeling of being exactly where you're meant to be, doing the work that makes the hours disappear.
