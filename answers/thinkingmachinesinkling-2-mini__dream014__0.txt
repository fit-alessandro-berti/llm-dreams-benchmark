...and you follow them, not sure if you’re stepping into a real street or a remembered one. Their group flows around the corners like a small, living current—some holding umbrellas that blink faintly in neon, some carrying groceries that smell like cinnamon and rain. You grip the collar of your coat and try to explain that you’re lost, but the words feel soft, like they’re made of fog.

The strangers make room for you anyway. One points toward a blue doorway, another sketches a route in chalk on the pavement, and a third asks one simple question: “Where do you want to feel brave?” You don’t know. Then you realize you want to be somewhere your body feels smaller and steadier—somewhere where being lost isn’t proof that you failed, but only evidence that you were curious enough to look.

As you walk, the crowd seems to thin, not because danger is near but because the city begins to turn into something kinder. The chrome signs soften. The noise becomes a rhythm. You and the strangers reach a rooftop, or perhaps the ledge of an old bridge, overlooking a tangle of gold lights and mist. Together you say the names of places you once hoped to be, and then, at the edge of the dream, one of them offers you a paper map that is actually blank except for a single small mark.

You don’t recognize the mark at first—then you do. It’s your own street. The version of home where you are still a stranger to yourself, but not alone. When you open your eyes, the city is still loud, but it has changed: the crowd moves around you with the same quiet courtesy you felt in the dream, as if everyone remembers the doorway, the chalk line, the scent of bread and rain, and wants you to find your way back.
