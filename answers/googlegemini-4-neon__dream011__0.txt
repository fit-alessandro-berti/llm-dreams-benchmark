**Question 1:** *Calculate the exact weight of the color blue on a Tuesday. Show all your work.*

Your stomach drops. You definitely missed this lecture. In fact, as a cold wave of dread washes over you, a terrible realization hits: you haven't attended a single class since September. You didn't even know where the building was until five minutes ago. 

Desperate for a question you can bluff your way through, you flip to page two, only to find the paper has turned into thick, damp felt. **Question 2** isn't a question at all—it’s just a faded photograph of the hallway in your childhood home, with a single blank line underneath that reads: *What did you forget to lock?*

Around you, the lecture hall has stretched impossibly wide, its rows curving up into a shadowy ceiling like a massive wooden amphitheater. The room is dead silent except for the deafening, synchronized *scratch-scratch-scratch* of hundreds of other students writing effortlessly. You glance sideways at the person next to you—it’s your fifth-grade teacher, wearing a tuxedo—and they are already on page fourteen, nodding with quiet satisfaction.

"Five minutes remaining," the proctor announces. Her voice echoes as if she is speaking from the bottom of an empty swimming pool. You look up at the wall clock, which has no numbers—only three brass hands spinning lazily backward.

Panic rises in your throat. You decide you just need to put *something* down on the page, anything for partial credit. You grip your pencil tightly and press it to the felt, only to realize you are holding a piece of cold, bendy licorice. It smears a useless brown streak across the paper and snaps in half.

Before you can ask for another one, your desk shrinks to the size of a postage stamp. Your exam slides off onto the floor, which you suddenly notice is ankle-deep in lukewarm rainwater. You drop to your knees, scrambling to scoop up the soggy pages before your entire future floats away down the aisle. 

A shadow falls over you. You look up to see the proctor standing right beside your chair, tapping a wooden yardstick against her palm. Her face is slightly blurry, like an out-of-focus photograph.

"Time is up," she says gently, holding out a wire basket. "Please hand in your shoes and your left eye."

You open your mouth to argue—to tell her there’s been a horrible mistake, that you studied the wrong textbook, that you aren't even enrolled in this department—when the classroom bell erupts. It rings too loud, too shrill, vibrating through the floor until the walls fracture into blinding strips of morning sunlight.
