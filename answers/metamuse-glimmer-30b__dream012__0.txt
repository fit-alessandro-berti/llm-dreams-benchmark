You are the captain of a ship in the middle of a turbulent sea. Your crew looks to you for guidance as the storm worsens, and you stand at the wheel in your dress uniform, throat dry, because you can’t remember if you ever learned how to read a compass.

The deck is tilting violently under your boots. Rain hammers the sails until they sound like drums. Your crew — men and women with your own face, or your mother’s, or your third-grade teacher’s — stare up at you, mouths open, waiting for a command. You raise your hand to steady them, but the wheel in your hands is warm and rubbery, more like the steering wheel of your first car. It turns, but the ship doesn’t.

A wave the size of a house breaks over the bow and the world goes white and cold. When the water clears, the sea is gone. You are still on the deck, but now you’re in your childhood kitchen. The storm is the sound of the refrigerator humming too loud. The crew is still there, sitting at the table with wet coats on, holding mugs of tea that never stop steaming.

You try to speak. You want to say “All hands on deck” or “Batten down the hatches” or something competent and captain-like. What comes out is a long, embarrassing squeak, like a kettle. The crew nods politely, as if that was the plan all along.

You laugh, suddenly, because it’s so obviously a dream, and in that instant the ship lurches again — not with the sea, but with your own body shifting in bed. Your alarm hasn’t gone off yet.
