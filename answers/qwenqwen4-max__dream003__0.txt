...and a faint warmth, as though something inside has been breathing against the wood.

The patterns are not carved. They are *growing* — hairline seams of brass spreading through the grain like frost across a window, and when you tilt the box toward the light they resolve into a street map of a city you have never visited but could walk blindfolded. There is the square with the dry fountain. There is the alley where the dog sleeps under the blue door. You know the name of the woman who lives above the bakery, and knowing it makes your teeth ache.

You look for a keyhole. There isn't one. You turn the box over and over, and each time you set it down it is slightly heavier, the way a sleeping child gets heavier in your arms.

The room you're standing in has become your grandmother's kitchen, though the window looks out onto the train platform at dusk, and a train is always just leaving. Someone is behind you. You don't turn around because in the dream you understand that the person behind you is also you, at nine years old, waiting to be told whether it's safe to look.

You press your ear to the lid.

Inside, someone is pressing their ear to the lid.

You knock twice. They knock twice — not an echo, an answer, arriving a half-second early. And the horror of it is mild and domestic, like finding a light already on in a room you thought was empty. You realize the lock is on the inside. You realize you have been very patient.

You say, *I'm sorry it took so long,* and the box opens the way an eyelid opens.

Inside there is no treasure, no letter, no small folded proof of anything. There is a key. Brass, worn smooth in one spot by a thumb. Your thumb fits the worn spot exactly, the way a word fits a mouth.

And you remember — the way you remember things only here — that you lock it every night. That you have opened it eleven thousand times and each morning you set the remembering down somewhere and cannot find it again. That the box is not hiding anything from you. It is *keeping* something for you, the way a jar keeps summer, and the keeping requires the lock.

The nine-year-old behind you takes your hand.

You turn the key.

You wake with the taste of brass under your tongue and your fist closed around nothing, and the light in the room is already on.
