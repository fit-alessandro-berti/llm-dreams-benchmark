They close around you like a warm current—an old woman with a map drawn on her palm, a teenager carrying a paper crane, a man whose laugh sounds like a bus braking gently. They don’t ask where you’re going; they ask what you’re hungry for. You say, “Home,” and they nod as if that’s an address.

The city rearranges itself: stoplights become fireflies, skyscrapers lean down to listen, and every street sign turns into a word you almost recognize. At the end of a narrow alley, they stop before a door you’ve never seen and yet know. One by one, they hug you goodbye, and their kindness leaves a trace of light on your skin.

You open the door. Inside, it isn’t a room but a morning—your morning—and you step into it, no longer alone. Then the dream softens, and you wake with the feeling that somewhere, in some bustling city, strangers are still waiting to be kind.
