You are given a mysterious locked box with no key in sight. As you examine it closely, you notice intricate patterns and grooves cut into the lid, thin as hairlines, and you realize after a moment that they are not decoration. They are a map. Not of any place you have been, but the shape of it is familiar in the way a word is familiar when it sits on the edge of your tongue.

The box is warm. Not sun-warm, but body-warm, as though someone has just set it down after carrying it for a long time against their chest. You turn it over. The underside is plain wood, except for a single line of writing, and the writing is in your own hand. You do not remember writing it. It says: you already opened this.

You look for a keyhole and there isn't one. There is no seam where the lid meets the body. You run your thumb along the edge and feel a faint pulse under the wood, slow, like something asleep.

The room you are in is your grandmother's kitchen, though you know that house was sold years ago, and the light coming through the window is the wrong color for any hour of the day. It is the light that happens inside a shell. Someone is moving in the next room. You can hear a drawer open and close, open and close, patiently, as if they are looking for something they are in no hurry to find.

You set the box on the table and the patterns on the lid begin to move. Very slowly, the hairline grooves slide against each other, the way the streets of a city might rearrange if no one were watching. You understand that the map is redrawing itself to show where you are now. A small mark appears near the center. You know without being told that the mark is you, and the mark is also the box, and there is no distance between them.

The person in the next room stops opening drawers. Footsteps. You do not turn around. You have the feeling that if you turned around you would see someone you love who is not supposed to be here anymore, and that seeing them would be fine, would be a relief even, but that the box would close forever if you did. So you keep your eyes on it.

"You have to remember the key," the voice says from behind you. It is kind. It is a little tired. "You put it somewhere clever. You always do."

You think: I don't have a key. And then you think: my mouth. You reach up and there is something small and cold under your tongue, and it has been there so long that you had stopped feeling it. You take it out. It is not a key. It is a tooth. One of your own, a baby tooth, with a tiny hole drilled clean through it.

You press the tooth against the center mark on the lid, the place that is you.

The box does not open the way boxes open. Instead the room opens. The walls of the kitchen fold outward like petals, unhurried, and behind them there is not the outside of the house but the inside of the box. You are standing in it. It is enormous. The wood grain arches overhead like the ribs of a whale, and the light in here is the same shell-light, only deeper, and the air smells like cedar and rain and the back of a closet you hid in once as a child.

The floor is covered with things. Hundreds of them, small and ordinary, laid out carefully in rows as if by a museum with a very strange curator. A green button. A bus ticket, soft as cloth from being folded and refolded. A hair elastic with one strand still caught in it. A phone number written on a napkin, in handwriting you almost recognize. A pebble that is exactly the pebble, you know it instantly, from a beach where nothing happened except that you were happy for one whole afternoon and then forgot.

You understand what the box is for.

You walk between the rows. Each thing you look at flares briefly, and you feel it, the whole day around it, the temperature, what you were wearing, who said what. Then it settles back into being just an object. You keep expecting to find the one thing that hurts, the one you have been locking away all this time, and you brace for it, and you keep walking, and it isn't here.

At the far end of the box, where the wood curves down to meet the floor, there is a second box. Much smaller. It fits in your hand. It has no lock at all. It is just a lid on a body, and the lid lifts easily, and inside it is empty, and the empty inside is warm.

You realize you are supposed to put something in it.

Behind you, very far away now, the voice says, "Take your time," and you hear the drawer open and close again, open and close, and you understand that they are not looking for anything. They are just keeping you company while you decide.

You stand there a long time holding the small box, and the map on the lid of the great box overhead keeps slowly redrawing, and the mark that is you does not move, because you are already exactly where it says.

Then you know what to put in. You take the tooth, the one with the hole through it, and set it in the small box, and close the lid.

Everything folds back. The whale-ribs become the kitchen. The kitchen becomes an ordinary morning. The box sits on the table, plain and locked and cool now, no writing on the bottom, no pulse, and you are not sure anymore whether it was ever anything but a box.

But your tongue keeps finding the place where the tooth was, and there is a small, clean gap there, and it does not hurt at all.
