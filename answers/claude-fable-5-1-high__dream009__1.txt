You wake up in a completely different era, with no modern technology around. As you explore your new surroundings, you realize you need to find water before you need anything else, because your mouth tastes of iron and straw, and the sun is already a hand's width above the roofline.

The room you have woken in is a loft. The floor is planks laid loose over beams, and through the gaps you can see down into a stable where a dun-colored horse is breathing slowly, its ribs lifting and falling like a bellows. There is no glass in the window. There is only a wooden shutter, and beyond the shutter a town that smells of smoke, dung, wet wool, and bread.

You climb down a ladder that has been worn smooth in the middle of every rung. Nobody stops you. The horse turns one eye toward you and decides you are not interesting.

The street outside is not a street so much as an agreement between houses. The houses lean toward each other over your head as if they are gossiping. Somewhere a bell is ringing, not to tell the hour but to tell something else, some fact everyone here already knows. You do not know it. This is the first time you understand that you are the only person in this whole era who does not know what the bell means.

You walk downhill because water is always downhill.

People pass you. A woman with a yoke across her shoulders and two buckets swinging. A boy driving three geese with a switch, the geese complaining in a language that has not changed in a thousand years. A man in a leather apron who looks at your shoes for a long moment and then looks at your face and then looks away, quickly, as though he has seen something he will have to tell a priest about later. You look down at your shoes too. They are the only thing you brought with you. The soles have a pattern of tiny hexagons. Here, that pattern is the most advanced object within a hundred miles.

At the bottom of the hill there is a well.

Of course there is a well. There is a stone lip around it and a windlass with a rope and a bucket, and the rope is wet, and the bucket is wet, and three women are standing beside it talking. When you come close they stop. Not rudely. They simply wait to see what you are.

"I need to drink," you say, and the words come out wrong. Not wrong exactly. They come out in the right shape but with the wrong music, the way a song sounds when someone hums it who has only read the sheet. The oldest woman tilts her head. Then she lowers the bucket for you herself, hand over hand, and the sound of it hitting the water far below is the most beautiful sound you have ever heard.

The water is cold enough to hurt your teeth. You drink from the bucket like the horse would. Nobody laughs.

"Where is your master?" the oldest woman asks, and you understand that this is the real question, the one underneath all the others. Who do you belong to. Where do you fit. Without that answer you are not a person here; you are weather.

"I am looking for him," you say, which is a lie and also, in the strange logic of the dream, completely true.

She nods as though this is a common problem. She points up a different street, one that climbs toward a squat tower. "Ask at the house with the blue door."

You go. You realize as you go that you have now been given a task, and that the task is a kind of shelter. As long as you are walking to the house with the blue door, you are someone with a reason to be in the street.

The blue door is not blue. It was blue once, and now it is the color of a bruise healing. You knock, and a small window in the door opens before your knuckles have finished, and an eye looks at you. Just one eye, and a strip of forehead, and a strip of gray eyebrow.

"You are late," says the eye.

"I only just arrived," you say.

"That is what late means."

The door opens. Inside it is dark and warm and full of paper. You have never seen so much paper in one place without a screen anywhere. Sheets are pinned to the walls, stacked on trestles, rolled and tied with string and stuffed into clay jars like flowers. A man with ink up to his wrists is standing in the middle of it all, and he is the owner of the eye, and he is looking at you now with both eyes, which is worse.

"Can you write?" he asks.

You say yes.

He hands you a quill. You have never held one. Your fingers know exactly what to do with a pen and nothing about what to do with a feather that has been cut into a knife. He points at a sheet. There is a line of text on it, written in a hand so cramped and looping that it looks like a hedge in winter, and he wants you to copy it below.

You dip the quill. Too deep. A drop falls and blooms into a black sun on the paper. He does not sigh. He simply takes the sheet, turns it over, and gestures: again.

So you write. Slowly at first, and then less slowly. The letters are almost letters you know. Some of them have tails that curl the wrong way. Some of them are not letters at all but a kind of shorthand, a hook that means "and," a little roof that means "the." You copy them without understanding them, and then, around the fourth line, you begin to understand them, the way you begin to understand a conversation in a room you have been sitting in for an hour.

The text is a list. Names, and beside each name a number, and beside each number a word. Wheat. Wool. Salt. Wheat. It is a ledger. It is the most ordinary document in the world, and you are copying it out in a room that smells of oak gall and soot, and you feel a happiness so sharp it is nearly grief.

"Good," says the ink-wristed man, from somewhere behind you, and that is all, and it is enough.

You work until the light through the doorway goes orange. Your hand aches in a new place. He gives you bread and a piece of hard pale cheese and a cup of something sour that you drink anyway, and he tells you that you will sleep in the loft above the stable, which is where you woke, which means you have already been here longer than you have been here.

Walking back through the town in the dusk, you keep noticing what is missing. Not the obvious things. You do not miss the glow of anything. What you miss is smaller than that. You miss the hum. There has always been a hum under everything, the sound of machines agreeing with each other, and it is gone, and in its absence you can hear a dog two streets away, and a shutter creaking, and your own blood.

The horse is asleep when you climb the ladder. You lie on the straw and look up at the roof, where the thatch has a small hole in it, and through the hole there is a single star. You think: if I stay here long enough I will learn what the bell means. You think: I already know the word for "and."

And then, at the very edge of sleep, you hear it. Very faint. Very far away. Coming from no direction at all.

The hum.

You sit up. The horse stirs below. The star in the hole in the thatch is still there, but it has moved, or you have. You wait, and the hum grows, and then you understand that it is not the machines coming to find you.

It is the sound the bell makes just before it rings.
