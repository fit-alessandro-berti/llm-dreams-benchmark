...written in a language you've never learned but somehow understand. The first question asks you to calculate the exact weight of every regret you've ever carried, rounded to the nearest heartbeat.

You look up. The exam room is your childhood kitchen now, but the ceiling has become the sky, and clouds drift lazily through the fluorescent light fixtures. Your proctor is your third-grade teacher, but she speaks with your father's voice and keeps insisting you use the blue pencil. You don't have a blue pencil. You reach into your pocket and pull out an endless cascade of silver spoons.

The clock on the wall has no hands, but you can hear it ticking backward. Someone in the row behind you is weeping. You turn—it's you, age seven, taking this same exam, and that version of you seems to know all the answers without reading any of the questions.

Question two reads: *Draw the smell of rain on the day you were happiest.* You suddenly can't remember how to hold a pen. Your hands have become your grandmother's hands—arthritis-swollen, familiar—and they know exactly what to do, but they're writing a letter to someone you've never met about a door you've never opened.

The student beside you leans over and whispers, "The answer is in the fish tank." There is no fish tank. There has always been a fish tank. Inside it, a goldfish is swimming through the very room you're sitting in, as if the fish tank contains *you*.

You realize you're running out of time. You check the clock. It now has too many hands, all spinning in different directions, and each one belongs to someone whose name you're forgetting.

The final question is always the same in every dream you've ever had:

*"What have you forgotten?"*

You feel the answer forming on your tongue like the name of someone you loved in a life you haven't lived yet. It's right there. You open your mouth to write it down—

—and you wake, with the taste of rain and spoons and your grandmother's kitchen, and the terrible, nagging certainty that you left something important unfinished.
