You wake up in a completely different era, with no modern technology around. As you explore your new surroundings, you realize you need to find the way home before the town forgets you were ever there.

The air smells of woodsmoke and damp earth. You’re standing on a cobbled street under a low, grey sky. The buildings are tall and narrow, brick and timber, with laundry lines strung between them and a church steeple rising at the end of the lane. No wires, no screens, no hum. Only the clop of horses, the clang of a blacksmith’s hammer, and a woman calling in a language you almost understand.

You don’t know your name here. When you try to speak to a boy with soot on his cheek, he looks through you like you’re mist. Your clothes are wrong — too clean, too soft — and your shoes make no sound on the stones. A bell tolls, distant and slow, and everyone stops to look up. You feel the pull of it in your chest.

You realize you need to find the place you woke up. You backtrack past the market stalls, past the well where women lower buckets, past the mural of a strange red bird painted on a wall. The street narrows, then opens into a small square where an old man is winding a clock. The clock has no hands, only a single bronze ring.

He sees you this time. He points past the steeple, to a narrow archway half-hidden by ivy. “You’re late,” he says, and his voice sounds like your own. “The dream only holds while you’re looking for it.”

You run. The cobbles blur. The sky lightens impossibly fast, and for a moment you see the faint outline of your own bedroom window superimposed over the church roof. You reach the arch just as the bell tolls again, and you step through.

You wake up.
