I grip the wheel tighter, knuckles white as bone, and feel the wood groan beneath my palms—not like timber, but like the ribs of some great sleeping beast stirring beneath us. The crew's faces are pale in the lightning's strobe, their eyes wide as moons, but I cannot look away from the horizon, where the sky has torn open not into rain, but into a spiraling vortex of silver light that seems to beckon rather than destroy.

"Steady!" My voice comes out as thunder, though my lips barely move. The ship pitches, and for a moment we are flying, suspended above waves that have become glass mountains crashing around us like shattered cathedrals. I see the fear in their eyes, but also something else—wonder, as if they know, as if we all know, that this storm is not an ending but a threshold.

I reach into my coat and pull out the compass my grandmother gave me before she dissolved into sea foam, its needle spinning wildly, pointing not north, but downward, into the churning deep where something vast is waking up to greet us. "All hands," I command, and my voice carries across the deck like a bell tolling across centuries. "We are not sailing through this storm. We are sailing *into* the dream itself."

The mainsail tears—not with a rip, but with a sigh, revealing not canvas but a doorway, and through it I see the shore we left behind, green and impossible, waiting. I step forward, one foot on the rail, and the wind doesn't push me back. It lifts me. It welcomes me.

Behind me, I hear the crew begin to sing—a shanty in a language that hasn't been spoken since the ocean was young. And as the vortex swallows us whole, I realize with perfect, dreaming clarity that I have been captain of this ship not for days, but for years, centuries perhaps, and the storm has been waiting for me all along to remember my name.
