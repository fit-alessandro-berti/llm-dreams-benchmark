**The Inventory**

You wake because your hands are cold, and for a moment you don't know whose they are. They're too rough — cracked at the knuckles, dirt ground deep into the nail beds, the nails themselves gone yellow and brittle. You raise them in front of your face and the light through the hole in the roof is the color of weak tea.

The room is a room in a house that stopped being a house some years ago. The refrigerator stands in the corner with its door hanging open on a hinge that gives up a long, patient sigh now and then, as if still waiting for the moment someone closes it. Inside: a bottle of vinegar with an inch left in it. Two olives. A twist of something in foil that might once have been cheese.

You know the inventory before you look, because you've been keeping it in your head the way other people keep prayers — constantly, silently, with a small panic every time it changes.

Outside, the world is the color of a stove's ash.

There used to be a highway. You remember from photographs, from before: a ribbon of black and white paint, a yellow line going off toward a city that glowed at night like a fallen constellation. Now the highway is a scar of weeds and cracked concrete, and the city is a low smear of gray on the horizon that people say is still burning, though no one has been able to say what.

The resources are the first danger. Water tastes like pennies. You boil everything. You have a cloth filter made of a shirt and a jar with a hole in the lid, and when the wind shifts wrong the water comes out of the cloth brown and you drink it anyway because the brown is *something*. There's a standpipe nine blocks east that runs four minutes a day, and you time it with the sun, and if you're two minutes late someone else has been there — and if someone else has been there, there's a new tally mark on the wall of the gas station where you sleep, scratched by someone else's knife.

The second danger is quieter.

Dogs. Not wild — abandoned, and worse for it, because abandoned means hungry in the specific way that a hand reaching for a can is hungry. They don't bark much. They watch from the roof of the school and they do the math on you: how tired you are, how far the next wall is. You've learned to throw food. You don't like what that teaches you about yourself, but you're not going to pretend you haven't learned it.

The third danger has no name and doesn't need one. It's just the world, wearing down.

**The Decision**

And then, on the eighth day — or the ninth, you've lost count, the days are all the same shade of gray — you find something.

Behind the wall of the pharmacy, where the collapse opened up the back rooms and the shelves have spilled out like snow, in a drawer that stuck when you pulled it: a tin. Inside the tin, in a small plastic bag, seeds.Beans. Heirloom, the packet says, the printing faded but legible, and there's a picture of a green thing so vivid it looks like a hole cut in the paper, a window onto a world where things grow upward on purpose.

You sit down in the aisle between the shelves. Above you, the ceiling fan doesn't turn, but the light moves anyway, because the sun does that through the hole in the roof, and for a moment the dust in the air looks like it's falling upward.

The arithmetic is not favorable. You know it before you do it.

There's no water for this. The last of the good dirt is at the nursery on the north side, and the north side is where the dogs are, and the dogs are getting bolder, and the last person who went north didn't come back, though people say a lot of things that turn out to be half-true. The seeds need sun, and there's a season coming you can't name. You have eleven days of food if you're careful. You have a knife with a loose handle and a bad feeling about the men in the trucks you hear at night, the ones with the engine noise and no headlights, who come and go and leave the town slightly emptier each time.

And you could not do it. You could go east, toward the coast, toward the boats, toward whatever the boats mean. You could go south. You could do nothing, and you could live a long careful life of boiled water and thrown food and tally marks and sleep, and you would not die of anything except the slow business of not having done a single thing with your hands that mattered.

You think about the beans. You think about the picture of the green.

Here's what you decide, and the deciding feels less like a decision and more like a floor giving out beneath you, a way of falling that you choose because the alternative is standing still forever.

You decide to plant them.

Not because you believe you'll eat them. That's the part that breaks your heart a little, and you let it break, because you haven't let anything break in a long time. You decide to plant them because the alternative is that the last green thing in the world dies out, and because a person who lets the last green thing die has agreed to a thing about the world that they will be forced to agree to every single day for the rest of their life.

So you make the trip north. You take the long way, through the parking structure, because the parking structure has three exits and no sightlines, and you go in the morning when the dogs are still asleep, and you carry the tin inside your shirt against your ribs where you can feel it, a small hard square that beats like a second heart.

You get to the nursery at noon. It's mostly glass and gravel, the greenhouse frames bent, the plastic gone to lace. But in the corner, under an overhang where the roof held, there's soil — actual soil, dark and crumbly and full of worms, the worms so improbable that you sit down and cry for a while, which you hadn't planned.

You plant eleven beans, one in each spot where the soil is best, and you cover them, and you tamp the dirt down with your bare hands until your yellow broken nails are black to the wrist.

**The Dream Logic**

That night you sleep in the greenhouse with the tin under your head like a pillow, and you dream — because of course you do, because you're here, because this whole thing is a dream you're having, and the dream knows it, and so do you.

You dream the beans come up overnight. You dream them coming up green and enormous, leaves like hands, the whole wall of them climbing the greenhouse frame and pushing through the plastic and going up into the gray sky where they flower so bright that the dogs stop and stare. You dream the men in the trucks come and stop, and get out, and stand there with their hands at their sides, and one of them says *we didn't know anyone was still doing this.*

You dream the coastline. You dream the boats.

You dream a woman you've never met says to you, *I had a garden once,* and you say, *what did you grow?* and she says, *tomatoes,* and then she laughs, and the laugh is the sound of someone remembering the word *tomato* correctly.

And then you dream that you wake up. You dream that you open your eyes and you're in your bed and the ceiling fan is turning and the refrigerator is humming and there's a whole world out there, functioning, with its own problems, and that you feel the most enormous grief — not for the world, but for the dirt under your fingernails, which is still there, which you rub and rub and won't wash off, and which is the only thing in your life that has ever been worth carrying.

**But**

Except.

Except you don't wake up. Except there's a light in the room, and it's the wrong color, tea-colored, coming through the hole in the roof. Except your hands are rough and yellow and cracked, and when you raise them, the dust in the air is falling *upward*.

And there's a tin under your head. And somewhere north of here, under an overhang where the roof held, the soil is dark and full of worms, and eleven small things are pushing.

You get up. You have eleven days of food. You have a knife with a loose handle.

You have dirt under your nails, and you don't wash it off.
