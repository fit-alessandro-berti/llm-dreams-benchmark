In this dream, survival is not just about bread and water, but about remembering what it felt like to look at something fragile and want it to live. 

Every night, while the dust storms paint the horizon in bruised shades of violet and ash, you climb thousands of cracked concrete stairs. You carry soil cupped in your hands like gold, gathered from the damp crevices of the underground subways. You sweep away the rusted girders and replace the blown-out skylights with shards of colored stained glass scavenged from fallen churches.

The dangers of the wasteland—the prowling, scavenger drones with their blind red eyes, the wandering shadows that forgotten men have become—follow you to the base of the tower. But dream-logic protects you; the higher you climb, the quieter their footsteps become, muffled by the rising wind.

At the summit, beneath a sky thick with static and silent auroras, you press the last dried seed into the earth. You don't know what it will be. It is a relic from a world that had names for things like *orchids* and *willows*. 

You pour the last of your clean canteen water over the mound. 

Instantly, time folds in on itself. A pale green shoot breaks the dry crust, unfurling not with leaves, but with luminous, glowing fronds that hum like a cello. The light spreads, pulsing through the cracked glass dome, casting kaleidoscopic emeralds and ambers across the ruined skyline below. 

Down in the dark, the dangers pause. The mechanical prowlers stop their clicking; the scavengers look up from their barricades. For the first time in an age, the ruins are illuminated not by fire, but by growth. 

You lean against the warm glass, watching the light reach the edge of the world, feeling the cold weight of the apocalypse dissolve into the quiet, blooming certainty that morning is finally coming.
