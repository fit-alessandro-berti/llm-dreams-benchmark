You are participating in a competition where the grand prize is your dream job. As the competition progresses, you face various challenges and

the challenges keep changing shape before you can finish them. The first one is a written exam, ordinary enough, except the questions are printed in an alphabet you almost remember, the letters swimming a little when you look at them directly. You answer anyway. Your hand knows what to write even though you don't, and when you glance down at the page you see you have been drawing floor plans of your childhood home, over and over, each one slightly wrong — a door where the pantry should be, a staircase that never existed.

The proctor collects the papers without looking at them and says, "Excellent. You've advanced."

The second challenge takes place in an elevator. There are eleven other contestants crowded in with you, all wearing lanyards with your name on them. The task, a voice explains through the ceiling speaker, is to reach the top floor. But the buttons are labeled with years instead of numbers, and everyone keeps pressing the years they were happiest, so the elevator only goes down. You press 2031, a year that hasn't happened yet, and the other contestants turn to look at you with something like grief. The doors open onto a rooftop anyway. You have advanced again. You are the only one who gets out.

On the rooftop, a woman in an interview suit sits behind a folding table with your résumé in front of her. Except it isn't a résumé — it's a stack of photographs. You recognize some of them: you at seven holding a paper boat, you asleep on a train, you standing in a kitchen you've never seen, laughing at someone just out of frame. "Walk me through this gap," she says, tapping a photograph that is entirely white. You open your mouth to explain and instead you hear yourself say, "That was the year I was the lake."

She nods as if this is the correct answer and writes something down with a pen that leaves no marks.

"Final challenge," she says, and the rooftop is suddenly an auditorium, and you are on the stage, and the seats are filled with everyone who has ever interviewed you, hired you, rejected you, taught you, doubted you. Your dream job, the voice through the speakers announces, is behind one of three doors at the back of the stage. But when you turn around there are no doors — just three windows, and through each one you can see yourself already working. In the first window you are important and exhausted. In the second you are content and small. Through the third window the office is empty, the chair still warm, a coat over the back of it, as if you just stepped out.

The audience leans forward. Somewhere a timer is counting down in a language made of bells.

You walk to the third window and instead of choosing it you open it, and climb through, and you are in the empty office, and you understand with the smooth certainty of dreams that the job was never behind any of the doors — the job is being the one who left. On the desk there is a note in your own handwriting. It says: "Gone to the lake. Back never. Started Monday."

The applause from the auditorium reaches you faintly, like rain on a roof several houses away. You put on the coat. It fits the way only things in dreams fit — as though it had been waiting, as though it had been worn soft in advance by all the versions of you who got here first.

You are still wearing it when you wake, or at least your shoulders think so, for a moment, in the dark.
