Open your mouth to shout an order—but instead of words, rain falls out of you. Small drops at first, then a downpour.

You grip the wheel, which is warm now, like the flank of an animal. It turns willingly one direction and refuses the other, exactly like fear does. Around you the waves rise up straight, tall as cliffs, holding their positions like soldiers deciding whether to let you pass.

Your first mate seizes your arm. In this light—though there is no light, only the gray shimmer of the storm—you see it's your grandmother, wearing her Sunday coat, perfectly calm amid the chaos. "The compass," she says. "Don't watch where it points. Watch what it avoids."

You don't understand until you look down: the needle spins wildly, except for one sliver of direction it always skips past, a gap in its circle like a missing tooth. So you steer toward the absence.

Below deck, singing drifts up through the floorboards—your whole crew, gathered around something warm that casts no shadows. They sound happy. This frightens you more than the storm, because in dreams you know these things: whatever is too calm is about to change shape.

Climbing onto the bow, you remember suddenly that the sea feeds on fear—it drinks yours and grows another inch taller. So you laugh instead, the strange rusty laugh of someone who realizes mid-scream that they're dreaming, and the sea flinches like a dog that's been struck before.

The waves part. Not dramatically—not all at once—but politely, the way a crowd parts for a procession. Through the gap you glimpse still water stretching away in every direction, and hanging low over it, an enormous slow moon shaped exactly like an eye opening.

Then you wake—or something wakes—with salt on your lips and your hands still curled around a wheel that isn't there, turning, turning.
