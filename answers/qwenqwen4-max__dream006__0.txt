…speak, and the first word out of your mouth is *water*.

You didn't mean to say it. Your notes were about something else — quarterly figures, or the migration of birds, you can't remember now — but the pages in your hands have gone soft as wet cloth, and the ink is running down your wrists in thin blue veins.

The audience doesn't seem to mind. They lean forward together, a single organism with a thousand faces, and you recognize them: your third-grade teacher, the man who sold you a peach last summer, someone you haven't thought about in twenty years and whose name arrives in your mouth like a stone. They are all wearing your coat.

*Water*, you say again, and this time it's true. You are talking about the reservoir behind your grandmother's house, how the drowned town is still down there, the church steeple furred with algae, the doors that still swing in the current. You've never known this before. You're learning it as you say it. Your voice comes from somewhere behind the back rows and reaches you a half-second late, so that you are always hearing yourself just after you've spoken, always slightly surprised.

The stage has become a dock. The wood is warm under your bare feet — you were wearing shoes, you're certain of it — and the auditorium seats stretch out over the lake in neat red rows, and everyone is very patient, and the water is at the level of the second row now, lapping.

You understand that you have to finish before it reaches the podium. You understand there's a last line that will make everything clear, that they've been waiting for it, that it's a simple line and you almost have it.

You open your mouth.

A small brown bird flies out.

The audience stands, delighted, and you think: *oh, of course, that's the ending, that was always the ending* — and you're climbing a staircase that wasn't there, looking for the room where you left your shoes, and the speech is still going on below you without you, and it's going very well.
