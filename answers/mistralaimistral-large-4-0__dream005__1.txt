…the rooms begin to breathe.

First, you pass a cluster of people standing in a perfect circle in the center of the ballroom. They are all wearing identical silver shoes—polished to a mirror shine—and they are not talking. They are only breathing, in perfect synchronization, their chests rising and falling like the tide. When you try to ask one of them where to go, their mouths open, but instead of words, a puff of lavender smoke escapes, smelling of your grandmother’s house and something else, something metallic.

You step back. The floor beneath your feet is warm, almost feverish, though the chandeliers above cast pools of glacial blue light. The architecture has begun to forget its own rules: doorways lead to staircases that spiral upward into clouds, and through the windows you can see not the city outside, but a different version of the same party, happening in a parallel darkness.

You encounter a long banquet table where guests are eating luminous fruit—apples that glow faintly, oranges that pulse like heartbeats. They do not look up as you weave between their chairs. One of them, a woman with hair the color of autumn leaves, reaches out and touches your wrist. Her fingers are cold as winter water. She whispers a name—*not* the name of the person you seek, but close enough to make your teeth ache*—and when you turn to ask her what she means, the chair beside her is empty, and the fruit in her hand has turned to ash.

The music shifts. It is a song you have never heard, yet you have been humming it your entire life. It comes from everywhere and nowhere, played on instruments that look like brass but sound like string quartets played backward.

You find yourself in a corridor that stretches impossibly long, lined with doors that are slightly ajar. Behind each one, you hear laughter—familiar laughter, but distorted, as if heard through water. You open one. Inside, a living room from your childhood, but the furniture is upside down, suspended from the ceiling. A television flickers with static that forms faces, then dissolves.

You are getting closer. You can feel it in the way the air thickens, sweet and heavy, like honey left in the sun. The guests here are beginning to look like the person you’re searching for—the same slope of shoulders, the same way of holding a champagne flute—but when you reach them, their faces are blank, smooth as eggs, or they are turned away, always turned away, showing only the backs of their heads, their hair identical to the hair of the one you seek.

Then you see them.

They are standing by the terrace doors, which open not to a balcony, but to an ocean at midnight. They are not looking at the water. They are looking at you. But their face is blurred, as if the dream itself refuses to let you see them clearly, protecting something, or perhaps punishing you for looking too hard.

You walk toward them. The distance does not close. The other guests part like curtains, their conversations fading into the roar of the tide that isn’t there.

You reach out your hand. Your fingers brush their sleeve.

And then—

The alarm clock, or the sun, or the sudden, terrible clarity that you were never at a party at all, but standing alone in your own kitchen, holding a coffee cup that has grown cold hours ago, wondering why your heart is beating so fast, and why you can still smell the lavender smoke, and why, when you look in the mirror, your shoes are silver, and your breath is perfectly, impossibly synchronized with the rhythm of the room.
