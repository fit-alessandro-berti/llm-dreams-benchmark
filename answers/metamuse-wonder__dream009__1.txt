...find water before anyone realizes you don't belong here.

The silence wakes you first. No hum of a refrigerator, no distant traffic, no phone buzzing on a nightstand. Just birds, wind in tall grass, and somewhere far away, a bell tolling slow and uneven, like it was rung by hand.

You sit up. You aren't in your bed. You're in a hayloft above a stable, straw sticking to your hoodie, sunlight falling in dusty shafts through cracks in the wooden walls. Below you, a horse stamps and snorts. The air smells of woodsmoke, warm animal, and baked earth — a smell so clean and so strong it almost hurts.

You pat your pockets automatically. Empty. No phone, no keys, no wallet. Just you, in jeans and sneakers, in what is very clearly not your century.

Outside, the village confirms it. Dirt roads worn into ruts by cart wheels. Cottages with thatched roofs and crooked chimneys. A blacksmith hammering something glowing orange, each strike ringing like a note. Women carry water in wooden buckets from a stone well. Men lead oxen. Nobody has glasses. Nobody has a zipper.

Except you.

Dream-logic settles over you, calm and absolute: *If they see the zipper, they'll know.* Not who you are, exactly, but that you're wrong. Out of place. And in this time, wrong can be dangerous.

So your first mission isn't grand. It's not to change history or find a portal. It's to get a drink, get different clothes, and learn how to exist for one day without being noticed.

You wait until the stablehand leaves, then borrow — steal, really — a rough brown tunic hanging over a stall door. It scratches your skin and smells like sweat and wool, but it covers your hoodie. You smear a little dust on your sneakers and hope they pass for strange leather shoes. Your mouth is so dry your tongue sticks.

At the well, an old woman is drawing water. She has your grandmother's face. Not resembles her. *Is* her, eighty years old and somehow also a thousand years removed from you, her hands strong around the rope.

She looks straight at you and, without surprise, says your name.

"You took long enough," she says, and offers you the bucket. The water is cold enough to make your teeth ache. It tastes like stone and iron, better than anything you have ever drunk.

"I don't know what I'm supposed to do here," you tell her.

She smiles like that's the most normal thing in the world. "You need to earn your supper. Same as everyone."

For the rest of the dream-day, you do. You stack firewood until your shoulders burn. You learn to carry two buckets on a yoke without spilling most of it down your back. You fail at kneading bread and succeed at sweeping flour. Your hands, soft modern hands, blister. People speak and somehow you understand them, though their words have an old music to them, and when you answer, they understand you too — as long as you don't say words like *okay* or *weekend*.

No one asks where you're from. In dream fashion, they just make room for you at the long table when the bell rings again at sunset.

The bread your grandmother-not-grandmother baked is on the table. Torn, steaming, smeared with butter. You are suddenly, overwhelmingly homesick for a place that hasn't been invented yet, and also strangely at peace here, doing simple work with your body until you were tired enough to belong.

"You can go back when you remember," she tells you quietly, while the others laugh and pass a clay jug around.

"Remember what?"

She taps your chest, right over the hidden zipper. "That you don't need to carry everything with you to survive."

The bell tolls a third time, but now it's morphing, thinning, becoming familiar. The firelight flickers into morning light through blinds. The smell of bread lingers for one impossible second in your apartment.

You wake up on top of your covers, fully dressed, thirsty.

And for the whole day afterward, every time you reach automatically for your phone, you hear water being drawn from a stone well, and you pause — just long enough to get a drink first.
