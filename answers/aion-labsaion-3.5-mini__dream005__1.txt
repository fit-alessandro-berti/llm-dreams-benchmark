...their conversations seem to bend around me like water around a stone.

A group in formal wear laughs at something I apparently said years ago. "You haven't changed a bit," says a woman with silver eyes. I want to ask her name, but my voice comes out as a low hum, like a refrigerator.

The room keeps rearranging itself. Hallways that were behind me are suddenly ahead. The ceiling lowers and lowers until everyone is crouching, still chatting pleasantly as if nothing is wrong. I crawl under a chandelier and emerge in an enormous kitchen — industrial pots of soup stirring themselves. Someone's grandmother is there, not mine, but she hands me a bowl anyway and says, "You look thin."

I ask if she's seen my friend. She points with a wooden spoon toward a door that clearly leads outside, but when I open it, I'm back in the ballroom. The music has become the sound of a dial tone.

Then I remember: the one person I know here is Alex, my childhood friend. I climb a spiral staircase made of folded napkins, and with every step I lose a detail of Alex's face — first the freckles, then the shape of the eyebrows, then whether they're tall or short. By the top, I know with total certainty that I know them, but I can't remember who they are.

At the top of the stairs, a phone booth stands alone in the middle of the crowd. It's ringing. Everyone shushes everyone else. I pick up the receiver, and it's my own voice on the line: "You're already late."

I wake with the heavy feeling of having forgotten something important, and a faint taste of soup.
