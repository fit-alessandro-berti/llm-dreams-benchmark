Three knocks. A pause. Then three more.

“Who’s down there?” you ask.

Your first mate counts the crew, pointing at each face with a wet finger. “Nobody, Captain.”

You lift the hatch. Instead of the hold, there’s a kitchen. Rain taps at its little square window. Your mother is at the table, peeling an apple in one long strip.

“Shut the door,” she says. “You’re letting the weather in.”

You haven’t heard her voice in twenty years. You nearly go down.

Then a wave hits the ship broadside, and your first mate catches you by the belt. You slam the hatch and tell the crew to lash themselves to the rail. You have to say it twice. Your voice doesn’t sound much like a captain’s, but the sailor nearest you ties her line, then reaches over to help the man beside her.

By dawn, the storm has passed. The crew sleep where they sit, still tied to the ship. You open the hatch again. There are barrels below, a coil of rope, an inch of seawater.

On the top step rests an apple, peeled all the way around except for the little patch where a thumb held it steady.
