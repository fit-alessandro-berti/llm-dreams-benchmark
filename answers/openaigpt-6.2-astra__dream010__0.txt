each one takes place in a room from a job you’ve had before. In the bakery, you have to ice a cake on the last day of summer; the buttercream keeps sliding off in one soft sheet. At the call center, you have to put a woman through to the person she meant to call twenty years ago. You manage it. You stay on the line just long enough to hear her say, “Is that really you?”

By the final round, only you and a woman named June are left. The judge sets a brass alarm clock on the table between you.

“Wake up the last contestant,” he says.

You hadn’t realized there was a third. Then June puts her head down on her folded arms. At the same instant your own eyelids grow heavy, and you understand: whoever stays awake gets to wake the other.

The trouble is, June has begun to dream about the sea. You can hear it through her sleeve. You haven’t been swimming in years.

You put your head down too.

The water is cold enough to hurt your teeth. June is twelve, and so are you, and for a while you do handstands in the shallows, judging each other solely on how straight your legs stick out. Eventually something brass bumps against your ankle. The clock. Its hands are both pointing to a little door in the cliff.

Behind the door is an office with two desks. On one sits a stack of applications for dreams: *One more afternoon with the dog.* *A house I know my way around.* *Flying again, please, but lower this time.*

June takes the one about flying. You take the dog.

There’s a box on the form marked BREED, but nobody’s filled it in. You’re worrying about this when you hear claws skitter on the floor outside, followed by the solid thump of a tail against the door.

From somewhere far above, the judge calls, “Well? Did anyone win?”

“Hang on,” you call back. You’re already on your feet. “He needs letting in.”
