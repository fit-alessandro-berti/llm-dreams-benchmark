You find yourself in a post-apocalyptic world, with resources scarce and dangers everywhere. Despite the hardships, you decide to **kneel beside the cracked concrete slab where a single, stubborn sunflower pushes through the ash—its yellow face tilted toward the weak morning light—and carefully dig a shallow hollow with your bare hands, not for food, not for shelter, but to catch the first few drops of dew that cling to the petals after last night’s unexpected drizzle. You place the hollow under the flower’s base, then sit back on your heels, quiet for a full minute, listening: the wind sighing through broken rebar, a distant bird that shouldn’t be alive, the soft *plink* of water finding the earth. When you rise, you don’t drink it right away. Instead, you walk three slow steps east to the half-buried garden box—just stones and soil, no plants now—and press the dew-filled hollow gently into the dry earth beside it. You whisper, not a plan or a prayer, just: *“Stay.”* Then you turn and walk away, not toward safety, not toward supplies, but simply forward, one foot after the other, leaving the flower, the hollow, and the promise in the dirt. The world is still broken. The dangers still watch. But for this one small act—catching light, naming a hope, placing it where nothing grows—you are no longer just surviving. You are tending. And that, in the ash, feels like the only revolution left worth dreaming.  

*(The dream lingers like the faint scent of wet earth and pollen as you keep walking.)*
