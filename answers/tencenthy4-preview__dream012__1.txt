Here’s one way the dream could continue:

You are the captain of a ship in the middle of a turbulent sea. Your crew looks to you for guidance as the storm worsens, and you feel a strange calm settle in your chest, as though the storm has been waiting for you to stop fighting it.

You loosen your grip on the wheel.

“Let her ride!” you shout, and the words are not English but an older language of salt and rope and wind. The crew hesitate—then they obey. The ship stops trying to conquer the waves and begins to move with them. Masts bend like bowing reeds. Rain stitches sea to sky until there is no horizon, only motion.

In the eye of the gale, a light appears. It is not a lighthouse. It is a window, warm and yellow, floating impossibly above the water. Through the glass you glimpse a small bedroom: a sleeping child, tangled in blankets, cheeks flushed with fever.

You understand then, the way one understands things in dreams, without needing to be told. The ship is a blanket. The crew are stuffed animals arranged at the foot of the bed—the bear, the rabbit, the one-eyed owl. The storm is the fever breaking.

You give the order.

“Steady as she goes.”

Your loyal, button-eyed crew salutes. The ship surges toward the window, and just before it reaches the glass, the sea smooths into a great silver silence.

You wake.

The sheets are damp and twisted. Real rain taps gently against the window. At the foot of the bed, a stuffed bear sits upright, one plastic eye catching the early light, as if to say:

*We made it, Captain.*
