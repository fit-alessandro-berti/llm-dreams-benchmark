The competition shifts from tests of skill to tests of vision. First, you’re handed a broken clock—literally, gears scattered across a table—while the judges watch in silence. You could fix it quickly, but you realize the real challenge is that the clock is running backward, and they want to see if you ask *why* anyone would build something that undoes its own time. You choose to rebuild it not as it was, but as a compass, and the judges nod.

Then comes the collaboration round: you’re paired with a rival who has been trying to sabotage your prototype. Instead of defending it, you hand them your best idea, and together you merge both approaches. The prize isn’t just the job; it’s the moment you realize the dream role was never about beating others, but about making something that needed both of you to exist.

In the final challenge, they ask you to present not a portfolio, but a promise—what you would build in your first year if the job were truly yours. You speak not about achievements, but about the problem that keeps you awake at night, the one only this role could help solve. The room goes quiet.

When the curtain falls, you don’t just win. You understand that the competition was a mirror, and every challenge was asking the same question: *Do you want this enough to become it?*

You wake—still in the room, heart beating—but now you know exactly what the next step is: not to wait for permission, but to build the first piece of it tomorrow.
