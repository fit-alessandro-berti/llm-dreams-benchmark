The street smells of woodsmoke, wet wool, and something earthy you can't name. Cobblestones press cold through thin shoes you don't remember putting on. Around you: timber-framed houses leaning toward each other like conspirators, a cart heaped with turnips, a man in a pointed hood leading a stubborn mule.

Nobody seems to notice you don't belong. Or maybe they do — a woman churning butter at her window pauses to watch you pass, eyes narrowing at your too-clean hands.

You find the well. Of course you do. In dreams, you always arrive places as if you'd always known the way. The water tastes of iron and cold stone, and in the bucket's dark surface you catch your reflection: your face, but older. Or younger. It keeps shifting, the way reflections do in dreams.

A church bell tolls once.

And now you know — again, that knowledge arriving fully formed from nowhere — that you're here for a reason. Something you left behind in this century. Something important. You just can't remember what.

You walk toward the bell tower, and the street stretches to meet you, doors and faces blurring past like pages flipped too fast. A baker, a blacksmith, children chasing a wooden hoop — each of them glances up as you pass, and each seems about to speak, about to tell you the thing you've forgotten.

The bell tolls a second time.

At the church door, an old man is waiting. He holds out a small brass object, worn smooth by centuries of hands. You reach for it, and as your fingers close around it, you realize it's your phone. Except it isn't — and in the dream this makes perfect sense. It has been here all along, waiting in 1348, and if you can just carry it back through the door—

The bell tolls a third time.

You wake with your arm still outstretched, fingers curled around nothing but the edge of your blanket. The phantom weight of brass lingers, warm in your palm. For one more moment you can almost hear well water sloshing, smell the woodsmoke.

Then it's gone — the way dreams always leave, all at once, like a tide going out.
