You duck into a subway station. The map shows lines leading to stops called *Palindrome Street* and *The Place You Forgot*. A train arrives, and when its doors slide open, they reveal your childhood bedroom — glow-in-the-dark stars still stuck to the ceiling. You step back. The doors close. The train sighs away into the dark.

A hand touches your shoulder. It's your third-grade teacher, though she has your boss's voice and your mother's eyes. "You're late," she says, or maybe she says "You're early" — the words keep flip-flopping. She hands you an umbrella, which feels deeply important, even though the sky above the canyon of buildings is impossibly blue.

You break into a run. Your legs move through honey. Each block stretches longer than the last, and somewhere behind you the meeting has already begun — you can hear the murmur of voices, the scrape of chairs. In a storefront window you catch your reflection: one shoe, a bathrobe thrown over a suit.

Then, impossibly, the building is right in front of you. Of course it is. It was always here. The elevator has no buttons, only a keyhole shaped like a question mark. The doors open directly onto the meeting room.

Everyone turns. There are more people than chairs, and some have the faces of people you haven't thought about in decades. Someone slides a document across the table. You look down — it's blank, no, it's written in that same swimming language, no — wait. As you tilt your head, you realize it's your own handwriting.

And suddenly you remember, completely, exactly why you're here —

just as the alarm drags you upward through layers of sleep like a diver rising through darker and darker water.

You wake. For three disorienting seconds, you can't remember your real address. Then your own ceiling comes into focus, ordinary and still, and the city dissolves like sugar in warm water.

Somewhere, you suspect, the meeting is going on without you.
