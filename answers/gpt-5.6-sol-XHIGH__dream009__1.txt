You wake up in a completely different era, with no modern technology around. As you explore your new surroundings, you realize you need to learn what year it is before anyone discovers that you do not belong in it.

You are lying in a field of silver grass beneath a sky with too many stars. There are no aircraft lights moving among them, no distant pulse of a radio tower, no electric haze above the horizon. The darkness is enormous and clean. When you sit up, dew has soaked through clothes you do not remember putting on: a rough linen tunic, a belt of braided cord, sandals tied around your ankles. Your pockets are gone. So is your watch.

Beyond the field, a city burns with the soft, unsteady light of oil lamps. Its walls are the color of dried blood. Towers stand along them at uneven intervals, and on every tower a bronze disk turns slowly in the wind, though there is no wind where you are sitting.

You follow a road toward the gates. It is deeply grooved by wheels, and the dust between the ruts is warm under your feet. People pass you carrying baskets, amphorae, sleeping children. Their faces are tired and strangely familiar, as if you have seen each of them for a second through a train window. You prepare to ask where you are, but the first words spoken near you sound like water poured over stones.

Then, without warning, you understand them.

“Before sunrise,” an old woman tells a boy beside her, “the king will ask for the missing hour.”

The boy laughs. “Hours don’t go missing.”

The woman grips his wrist. “This one did.”

At the gate, soldiers inspect everyone’s hands. Those with ink stains are sent left. Those with calluses are sent right. Your hands have neither, and the soldiers stare at them with suspicion. One of them turns your palm toward the torchlight. In its center is a small black mark shaped like a square.

“A recorder,” he says, suddenly respectful.

Before you can object, they place a clay tablet in your arms and usher you through the gate.

Inside, the streets twist between windowless houses. Donkeys bray from courtyards. Water runs in narrow channels under your sandals, reflecting strips of flame. High overhead, ropes stretch from rooftop to rooftop, hung with hundreds of tiny bells. No one touches them, yet as you pass beneath, they ring one after another, following you deeper into the city.

A girl in a yellow veil is waiting at the foot of a staircase. She cannot be more than twelve. She bows to you and says, “You are late.”

“For what?”

“For yesterday.”

She climbs, expecting you to follow. The staircase winds up the outside of a vast stepped temple. With each level, the city below seems to age. New roofs blacken and cave in; walls crumble; trees rise through abandoned courtyards. By the time you reach the top, centuries appear to have passed beneath you, but the girl is not even out of breath.

On the highest platform stands a room with a blue door. Inside is a table, two stools, and a bowl filled with clear water. An old man sits on one stool. His beard is braided with gold wire, and his eyelids have been painted with small open eyes.

“At last,” he says. “The one who remembers the future.”

You tell him he is mistaken.

“Naturally.” He gestures to the other stool. “Sit.”

The girl places your clay tablet on the table. Its surface, blank at the gate, is now covered in wedge-shaped marks. You cannot read them, but you know they describe your bedroom: the glass of water beside the bed, the blue digits of an alarm clock, the hairline crack in the ceiling. At the bottom is a picture of you asleep.

The old man dips one finger into the bowl. The reflected stars shiver.

“Every night,” he says, “the world passes through this room. Every morning, one hour is returned to the gods. That is why sleepers wake unable to account for where they have been.”

“I want to go home.”

“You have said that before.”

He turns the tablet over. On the back are dozens of versions of the same sentence, pressed in different alphabets, scratched with steel, inked in languages not yet born. Some are in your handwriting.

The room tilts. For a moment, the blue door opens not onto the temple but onto your apartment hallway. You hear the refrigerator humming. You lunge toward it, but the girl shuts the door with one hand.

“Not until you replace the hour you took,” she says.

The old man pushes the bowl toward you. Its water has become dark and deep. Far below its surface, something glows: a small white hour turning like a coin as it falls.

You reach in. The bowl is deeper than your arm, deeper than the table, deeper than the temple. Cold closes around your shoulder. Your fingers brush rooftops, tree branches, the mast of a ship. You hear newborns crying and soldiers shouting in centuries that have not happened. The old man urges you to reach farther.

At last you catch the hour.

It struggles in your fist like a trapped moth. When you pull your hand free, you are holding an ordinary brass key.

All the bells in the city begin to ring.

The old man recoils. The painted eyes on his lids blink separately from his real ones. “That is not the hour,” he whispers.

The girl lifts her veil. She has your face.

“No,” she says. “It is the way out.”

The blue door vanishes. In its place is a narrow keyhole suspended in the air. Through it you can see daylight, impossibly bright. The old man overturns the bowl, and night spills across the floor in a shining sheet. Stars scatter around your feet. From the staircase comes the thunder of soldiers climbing.

You fit the key into the floating keyhole.

The entire sky turns with it.

For one dizzying instant, the city hangs above you and the stars lie below. Houses, temples, people, and walls loosen from the earth and begin falling upward into the dark. The girl seizes your wrist. The old man catches her veil. You catch the table. Everything is linked in a swaying chain while the missing hour, freed from the bowl, rises around you as a pale ring.

“Let go!” the girl shouts.

You do not know whether she means the table, the key, or the life you came from.

So you let go of everything.

You fall through the keyhole into a corridor made of morning. Doors race past on both sides. Behind one, you are an old person waking in the same field. Behind another, you are a soldier at the gate examining your own empty hand. Behind a third, the city is a glass-and-steel ruin buried beneath sand, and archaeologists are lifting a clay tablet from the earth. You try to call to them, but their flashbulbs burst like silent lightning, and each burst sends you farther away.

The last door is your bedroom door.

You crash through it and wake in bed just before the alarm sounds.

For a while you lie still, listening to the electrical murmur of the room. The ceiling crack is there. The glass of water is there. The clock is there, but its display is blank. Outside, the first train of the morning passes, filling the walls with a familiar tremor.

You convince yourself it was only a dream until you open your hand.

The brass key lies in your palm. Beneath it, pressed into your skin, is a small black square.

Then every clock in the city begins ringing at once, and the hour hand on each of them turns backward toward yesterday.
