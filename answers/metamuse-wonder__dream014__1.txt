and you, despite the small voice in your head telling you to stay wary, nod and take the hand that's offered to you.

It's warm. Surprisingly solid for a dream.

The strangers don't ask where you're going — not exactly. The old woman in the yellow coat asks, "What does home sound like?" The tall man with the umbrella, even though it isn't raining, asks, "What color was being safe?" A little kid, no older than six, tugging a red balloon that floats a little too still in the wind, just says, "Don't worry. We get lost here every night."

They don't lead you down one street. The city seems to lead itself around you. A crosswalk stretches into a bridge over a canal that wasn't there a second ago. A subway staircase goes up instead of down, opening onto a quiet rooftop garden lit with string lights. Shop windows flicker past showing not clothes or food, but brief moments: a kitchen table from your childhood, a friend laughing on a train, a door you used to know by heart.

You try to explain that you don't recognize any of this, but the strangers just smile like that's the point.

"We're not taking you to a place," the woman says, unfolding a map. It's not of streets at all, but of constellations. "We're taking you back to a feeling. The place will follow."

The bustle of the city softens as you walk. The honking becomes distant music. The crowds part without pushing. Your chest, which had felt tight and hollow since the dream began, starts to loosen. You realize your feet know the way your mind has forgotten — left at the bakery that smells like rain and cinnamon, straight past the fountain where the water flows upward for just a moment, right when you hear someone humming a song you almost remember.

At last you stop in front of a small, unremarkable door tucked between two towering buildings. There's no number on it. Just light, warm and gold, spilling from underneath.

You know, without being told, that if you open it you will wake up.

The kid ties the red balloon gently around your wrist. "So you can find us again," he says.

You turn the handle, grateful and a little sad to leave them, and step through —
