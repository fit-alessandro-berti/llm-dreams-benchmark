You start to run.

The streets rearrange themselves as soon as you enter them. A narrow alley widens into a boulevard lined with silent cafés; a flight of stairs descends toward the sky; street signs flicker between languages you almost understand. Every clock shows a different time, yet all of them are moving too quickly.

You stop a woman carrying a red umbrella, though there is no rain.

“Excuse me,” you say. “Do you know where this meeting is?”

She looks at you as though the question is perfectly reasonable.

“Of course,” she replies. “It’s wherever they’re expecting you.”

Before you can ask who *they* are, she steps onto a passing tram and disappears behind its fogged windows.

Your phone is in your hand, but it has no maps, no messages, and no names. There is only a countdown on the screen:

**47:12**

Beneath it, a single instruction appears:

**Follow what you recognize.**

You look around. Nothing is familiar—until you hear a melody drifting through the traffic. It is an old tune you have not heard since childhood, played badly on a piano somewhere nearby. You follow it through a market where vendors sell bottled thunderstorms, unfinished conversations, and keys without locks.

The music leads you to a hotel with revolving doors that spin too fast. Inside, the lobby stretches upward for hundreds of floors, full of elevators labeled not with numbers, but with words:

**REGRET**

**ALMOST**

**HOME**

**LATER**

At the end of the row is one marked **NOW**.

You enter.

There are no buttons inside. The doors close, and the elevator begins moving sideways. Through its glass wall, you see rooms passing by: a classroom where everyone has your face, a kitchen from a home you no longer visit, a train station where someone is still waiting for you. You want to stop, but the elevator continues until the countdown on your phone reaches:

**12:03**

The doors open onto the roof of a skyscraper.

A long table has been set beneath the open sky. Dozens of chairs surround it, but only one person is sitting there, facing away from you. Papers flutter across the tabletop, covered in handwriting that looks like yours.

“You’re late,” the person says.

“I still have twelve minutes.”

“No,” they answer. “You’ve had years.”

They turn around.

It is you—but older, calmer, and somehow more tired. There is no anger in their expression. Only patience.

You sit across from them. The entire city is visible below, its streets shifting like veins of light.

“What is this meeting about?” you ask.

Your older self slides a blank piece of paper toward you.

“The decision.”

“What decision?”

“The one you keep waiting to feel ready for.”

The countdown reaches **00:59**.

You stare at the empty page. Every possibility seems to appear on it for a moment—a journey, a confession, a door left unopened, a version of your life that begins the instant you stop asking permission.

“What am I supposed to write?”

“Anything,” your older self says. “But it has to be true.”

The final seconds begin to vanish.

You take the pen.

Just before the screen reaches zero, you write one sentence.

The city becomes completely silent.

Then every clock strikes at once.

The buildings dissolve into birds. The streets lift from the ground like ribbons. The table, the chairs, and your older self scatter into bright fragments, but the paper remains in your hand.

As you begin to wake, you try desperately to read what you wrote.

The words blur—except for the final three:

**Begin from here.**
