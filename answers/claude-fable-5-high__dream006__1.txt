You are about to give a speech to a large audience. As you step onto the stage, you see the crowd looking expectantly at you, and you begin to reach into your jacket for your notes — but your jacket has no pockets. It has never had pockets. You realize you are holding, instead of index cards, a single smooth stone, still warm, as if someone had been keeping it in their hand for years before passing it to you.

You place the stone on the podium and it makes a sound like a bell, and the audience leans forward in unison, a single motion, like wheat bending under wind.

You open your mouth to apologize, to explain that the speech has been misplaced, but what comes out is not an apology. It is the first line of something you have always known and never said. You hear yourself say it before you understand it, the way you sometimes hear rain before you know it is raining.

The microphone is unnecessary. Your voice arrives everywhere at once, intimate, as if you were speaking to each person alone in a small kitchen at night.

As you speak, you notice the audience is made of everyone. Your third-grade teacher is in the fourth row, still holding the chalk. Your grandmother is near the aisle, younger than you ever knew her, laughing at a joke you have not told yet. There are strangers, too, thousands of them, but each face, when you look at it directly, resolves into someone you once passed on a street and never forgot — the man with the umbrella, the girl on the train reading a book whose title you tried and failed to see.

The hall itself is growing. The walls slide quietly backward like ships leaving harbor, and the ceiling opens by degrees onto a sky the color of old paper. Nobody finds this alarming. You least of all.

You keep speaking. You are not sure of the words even as you say them, but you are sure of their weight. Each sentence leaves your mouth and becomes slightly visible, a faint silver thread, and the threads drift out over the audience and settle on their shoulders like the beginning of snow. People are catching them. A child stands on her seat and gathers a whole handful.

At some point you understand what the speech is about. It is about the stone. It was always going to be about the stone. You lift it from the podium and hold it up, and it is heavier now, because everyone is looking at it, and attention has weight — this is a fact you know only here.

"This was given to me," you say, and the crowd nods, because it was given to them too, all of them, each one privately, long ago, and they had each assumed they were the only one.

The applause, when it comes, is not applause. It is the sound of ten thousand people exhaling at the same time, a soft roar like surf, and the silver threads lift off their shoulders and rise through the open ceiling into the paper-colored sky, where they braid themselves into something enormous and slow.

You step back from the podium. Your legs feel like a child's legs at the end of a long day of swimming. Someone is guiding you offstage — it is you, an older you, patient, unsurprised — and as you pass into the wings the stage lights dim behind you one by one, like windows in a town where everyone is finally going to sleep.

In the darkness backstage there is a small table, and on the table is a glass of water someone left for you before you were born. You drink it. It tastes like the middle of a lake.

Far away you can still hear the audience, no longer clapping, just talking among themselves in low warm voices, the way people talk after something true has happened, deciding without any hurry who among them will be next to speak.

You put the stone in your pocket. The jacket has pockets now.

You wake with your hand closed, holding nothing, still warm.
