They move slowly, the way a sleeping animal does. The stone is warm under your palm, and the puzzles carved into it rearrange themselves when you look away. A lock that needed a key now needs a color. A riddle you solved three corridors back reappears, but this time the answer is your grandmother's kitchen, and somehow that's correct. The door swings open.

Deeper in, the logic changes. You come to a chessboard where every piece is a small brass bird. You understand, with the total certainty that only dreams allow, that you must not move them but listen to them. Each one hums a note. When you arrange the notes in the right order, the floor becomes a staircase spiraling down through water that you can breathe.

At the bottom is a door with no handle, no keyhole, and no inscription. There is only a mirror set into its surface, and the reflection isn't quite yours. It's you at seven years old, holding a puzzle box you'd forgotten you ever owned. The child looks up and says, "You've been solving them all wrong. They're not locks. They're questions."

You realize then that every corridor, every turn, every breathing wall has traced the shape of a single thought, one you've been circling for years. The maze isn't something you're inside. It's something you're thinking.

The child holds the puzzle box out through the glass. You reach for it and your fingers close around it. It's lighter than you remember. Inside, something shifts, like a marble or a seed or a very small bell.

You begin to open it, and the walls, the water, and the brass birds all go quiet, waiting to see what you'll find.
