You are walking through a peaceful forest when suddenly the weather changes dramatically. You feel a storm approaching and the light goes green, the way it does underwater. The birds stop mid-note, as if someone lifted the needle from a record, and the trees begin leaning all in one direction — not away from the wind, but toward it, curious.

The first raindrops fall upward. You watch them lift off the ferns and rise in slow silver strings toward a sky that has become a ceiling of dark cloth, sagging low enough to touch. You reach up and touch it. It is warm, and it breathes.

Thunder arrives, but it isn't sound; it's a feeling in your teeth, like remembering a name at the moment someone says it. Each rumble makes the forest rearrange itself slightly — a birch now where an oak was, the path folding back on itself so that the way forward is also, comfortingly, the way home.

You find a door standing alone between two pines. No walls, only the door, white with a brass knob shaped like an acorn. Rain hammers hard on the other side of it — you can hear the true storm there, the one this forest is only dreaming about. You knock. The knock is answered by your own knock, half a second late.

When you open the door, you find your grandmother's kitchen, dry and yellow-lit, the storm streaming down the window above her sink. She isn't there, but the kettle is on, and two cups wait on the table, and you understand one of them has always been yours. Outside the window, the forest you were just standing in tosses and shines in the downpour, and you watch a small figure — you, still out there — look up at the window and wave.

You wave back. The kettle sings. Lightning turns everything white and tender for a moment, and in that whiteness you can smell pine and bergamot and wet earth all at once.

The storm settles in like an old animal by the door. You sit down. You pour. The rain agrees to fall downward again, out of politeness, and the thunder softens into something like a heartbeat heard through a wall — steady, patient, willing to wait until you finish your tea before the dream lets you wake.
