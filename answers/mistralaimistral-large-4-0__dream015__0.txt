Despite the hardships, you decide to **plant a garden in the cracked concrete of the abandoned city center.**

You kneel beside a rusted-out fountain where the water has long since turned to dust, and your fingers dig into soil that shouldn't exist here—dark, impossibly fertile, smelling of rain and copper pennies. In this dream, the apocalypse has left behind not just ruins, but *echoes*, and the seeds you press into the earth are silver and humming, like small moons wrapped in soil.

You don't know what will grow. Tomatoes, perhaps, or something that has no name yet—flowers that bloom in reverse, opening their petals to swallow the ash that falls like gray snow. Other figures move in the periphery of your vision: survivors with faces blurred like watercolors, watching you with expressions you can't quite read—hope, or warning, or simply recognition.

The sun here is a dying ember, bleeding orange across the skeletal remains of skyscrapers. Yet when you water the first seedling with water from a dented canteen, it doesn't just grow—it *reaches*, stretching toward you with tendrils that feel almost like fingers brushing your wrist.

You understand, then, that survival isn't just about finding food or avoiding the things that hunt in the dark. It's about this: refusing to let the world end where the concrete ends. You are building a green defiance against the gray.

And as the dream begins to fade, you realize the garden is already taller than you are—leaves whispering secrets in a language older than the bombs, older than the silence. You wake with dirt under your fingernails and the taste of green growing on your tongue.
