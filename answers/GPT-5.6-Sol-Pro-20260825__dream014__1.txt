The strangers are an unusual group: an elderly woman carrying a bright red umbrella, a bicycle courier with a silver bell, a child holding a paper map, and a quiet man whose coat pockets are filled with tiny glowing lanterns. None of them asks where you came from. They only ask, “Where do you feel you belong?”

You try to name a place, but you cannot remember your address. Instead, you describe a feeling: warm light through a window, the sound of someone making tea, and the certainty that your arrival matters.

“That’s enough,” says the woman with the umbrella.

They lead you through crowded markets, across bridges suspended between skyscrapers, and down a narrow alley invisible to everyone else. With every step, the noise of the city grows softer. You notice that each stranger disappears after helping you past a difficult crossing. The courier leaves at the bridge, the child vanishes when the map is no longer needed, and the man gives you his final lantern before fading into the evening mist.

At last, only the elderly woman remains. She stops before a small blue door beneath a flowering tree.

“Is this my home?” you ask.

She smiles. “Not exactly. It is the place where you begin finding it.”

You open the door and discover a quiet room filled with people you have helped at different moments in your life—some you remember, many you do not. They turn toward you with warm, familiar expressions, as though they have been waiting.

Then you understand: the strangers were not guiding you toward an address. They were reminding you that being lost does not mean being forgotten, and being alone does not mean you are unloved.

As you step inside, the city outside becomes silent. Just before you wake, you hear the woman say:

“Whenever you lose your way, look for kindness. It always knows the road home.”
