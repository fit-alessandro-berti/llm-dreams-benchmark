...strange, impossible trials that seem to rearrange themselves whenever I look away.

The first challenge takes place in a hallway of mirrors, where every reflection is slightly late. I raise my hand, and my reflection raises its hand a moment after, as if it needed to think about it first. A judge with no face hands me a brass key and says, "Open the door you're standing in front of." I'm standing in the middle of the hallway, but when I turn around, there is a door behind me that wasn't there before. I open it, and it leads to a kitchen where my grandmother is making soup. She doesn't look up. "You're going to be late," she says, and somehow I know she means for the competition. I pass the test by sitting down and tasting the soup, which is perfect, and the mirrors all stop lagging at once.

The second challenge is a race across a library where the floors are made of water. The books on the shelves whisper their titles as I pass, and some of them are books I've always meant to write. I try to run, but the water holds me like thick honey. A competitor I don't recognize, wearing a coat made of newspaper, floats past me on a single page. "You don't swim in this," she says. "You read your way across." I start reading the nearest book aloud, and the words become stepping stones. I reach the far side soaked to the knees, holding a pen I didn't have a moment ago.

The third challenge is the hardest. The judges, now a committee of tall herons in dark suits, present me with a closed box and ask me to describe what's inside without opening it. I feel the weight of it, and it's warm, like something alive. Every instinct wants to guess, to impress them, but I say only, "I don't know yet." The herons exchange glances, then slowly bow their long necks. The box opens by itself, and inside is nothing but a small, bright window showing a room I recognize: my own desk, my own unfinished work, and a version of me sitting there, looking tired but content. The herons say, "This is the job. It was always the job."

Then the competition dissolves like sugar in tea. The prize is not a title or a corner office but a quiet room with a window that overlooks a harbor, where I am told I'll spend my days helping people find the thing they're trying to build. I feel relief so vast it makes me weep, and somewhere a clock begins to chime, far too early, the way clocks do when you're about to wake.

I reach for the window, and the harbor blurs into morning light.
