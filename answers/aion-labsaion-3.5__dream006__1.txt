...speak, but no words come out. Your mouth opens, and instead of a greeting, a flock of white doves bursts from your throat and scatters over the audience. The crowd doesn't seem surprised. They lift their arms, and the doves land on their fingertips like they've been waiting for this moment their whole lives.

You look down at your notes, but the pages are blank—except for a single sentence written in handwriting you recognize as your grandmother's, even though she's been gone for years: *"Everyone here is dreaming too. Tell them what they want to remember."*

So you set the notes aside. You lean into the microphone and begin describing things you've never told anyone: the summer you were nine and the creek behind your house froze in July, the way your father's hands smelled like cedar and coffee, the name of the girl who moved away before you could say anything.

As you speak, the audience begins to nod, and you realize with a start that they're nodding because they remember too. They remember *your* memories. A woman in the third row is crying softly. A man near the back raises his hand and when you call on him, he says, "Don't stop. If you stop, we all wake up."

The stage lights grow warmer, like sunlight through honey. Somewhere behind you, someone is playing a piano, though you never saw one there before. The music is a song you know but can't place—one your mother hummed while cooking, maybe, or something from a road trip you half-remember.

You keep talking. The theater seats begin to empty one by one, but not in a bad way—the people leaving are smiling, walking out through doors that open onto beaches and childhood kitchens and autumn streets. They're going wherever the speech is taking them.

Finally, it's just you on the stage, the spotlight soft around your shoulders. The last thing you say into the microphone, to no one at all, is: "Thank you for coming."

And the curtains close like eyes.
