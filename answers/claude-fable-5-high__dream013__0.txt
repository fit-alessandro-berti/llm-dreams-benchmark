You are in a maze filled with complex puzzles. Each puzzle requires intense concentration and logical thinking to solve, and as you move deeper into the maze, you begin to notice that the puzzles are changing you.

At first it is subtle. You solve a wall of sliding tiles that must be arranged into a picture of a door, and when the tiles click into place the door appears — but you realize the picture on the tiles was assembled from memories you didn't know you had. A kitchen window from a house you may have lived in. A hand, someone's hand, resting on a railing. You walk through the door and try to recall the window again, and it is gone, spent, used up as currency.

The maze accepts memories as payment. That is the rule no one told you.

The corridors are lined with a soft grey material like felt, and the light comes from the puzzles themselves: each one glows faintly until it is solved, then dims. Behind you the maze goes dark, room by room, so there is no going back — not because the way is blocked, but because there is nothing left to see there.

The second puzzle is a table of brass gears that must be arranged so that turning one turns all the others. You work at it for what feels like hours, though the hours don't pass — you can feel them queuing up politely behind you, waiting. When the last gear meshes and the whole table spins in one smooth motion, you feel a small clean joy, and you also feel the name of your first teacher slide out of your head like a coin through a slot. You hear it drop somewhere below the floor. You almost remember hearing it drop.

Deeper in, the puzzles stop pretending to be objects. One room is simply a question written on the wall in your own handwriting: WHAT WERE YOU DOING BEFORE THE MAZE? The room will not let you leave until you answer, and you stand there a long time, because you understand — with the certain, unargued logic of dreams — that any answer you give will become true, and whatever was actually true will be paid into the wall.

You write: I was resting.

The wall accepts this. A corridor opens. And now it is true; before the maze, you were resting. You feel lighter, and you distrust the lightness.

The maze grows warmer as you go. The felt walls give way to wood, then to something like the inside of a lived-in coat. The puzzles get easier, which frightens you more than if they had gotten harder. A child's shape-sorter. A knot with only one loop. You realize the maze is not testing your intelligence anymore. It is testing whether you'll keep solving. Whether you'll keep paying.

You stop in front of a puzzle that is just two buttons, one marked CONTINUE and one marked UNDERSTAND, and you know you can only press one.

You stand there with your finger raised, and the maze goes very quiet, the way a room goes quiet when it is listening. And you understand — pressing UNDERSTAND would count as pressing it — that the maze is not a place. It is the act of figuring things out, walled and roofed and stretched into corridors. You have been inside it much longer than this dream. You built parts of it. Some of the puzzles are ones you left for yourself, years ago, baited with your own curiosity.

You press CONTINUE, of course. You were always going to. That is what the maze knew about you from the start, the one thing it never needed to take as payment because you give it freely every time: the next room, the next glowing problem, the next small clean click of something falling into place.

The corridor ahead lights up. Somewhere behind you, gently, a door you will never need again goes dark.

You walk on, smiling, unable to remember what you were doing before the maze, certain it was restful, certain it can wait.
