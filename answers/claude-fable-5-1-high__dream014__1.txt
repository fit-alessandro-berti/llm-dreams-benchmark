You are feeling lost and alone in a bustling city. Suddenly, a group of kind strangers offers to help you find your way, and you accept before you have even understood what they said, because their voices are the first thing in hours that has not been the sound of traffic.

There are five of them, or six. You keep trying to count and the number will not hold still. A woman in a yellow raincoat, though it is not raining. An old man carrying a birdcage with no bird in it. Two teenagers who might be twins, who finish each other's gestures rather than each other's sentences. Someone at the back whose face you can never quite turn toward in time.

"Where are you trying to go?" the woman asks.

You open your mouth and realize you don't know. You had known a moment ago. You had an address, a name, a reason for being here, and it has slipped off you like a coat left on a chair. "Home," you say, because it is the word that comes.

They nod as if this is a perfectly ordinary destination, as if home is a stop on a tram line, and the old man lifts the empty birdcage and looks at it the way you would look at a compass. "This way," he says.

The city rearranges itself around your walking. Streets that were wide become narrow. A row of shopfronts you passed twice already now has a bakery in it that smells of your grandmother's kitchen, exactly, cardamom and burnt sugar, and you almost stop but the twins each take one of your elbows and steer you on. Nobody else on the pavement seems to see the group. Pedestrians part around you the way water parts around a stone, without looking.

"We've been lost here too," says the woman in yellow. "Everyone has. That's how you know it's the city."

"How long have you been here?"

She thinks about it. "Since you arrived," she says, and it doesn't sound like an evasion. It sounds like an answer you are not yet ready to understand.

You cross a bridge over a river that is not on any map you remember. The water below is the color of old television static, and things move in it, slow shapes, and one of them lifts a face toward you that is your own face at nine years old, mouth open as if calling. You grip the rail. The old man puts a hand on your shoulder, dry and light as paper.

"Don't answer them," he says. "They only want you to stay."

On the far side the buildings grow taller and closer together until the sky is a strip of blue-grey cloth stretched thin overhead. Laundry hangs between windows, and the laundry is all clothes you have owned. The shirt you wore the day you left. A scarf you lost years ago and mourned more than you admitted. They wave at you gently in a wind you do not feel.

The strangers begin, one by one, to leave.

The twins go first. They stop at a doorway lit from inside with the flicker of a television and simply turn and step into it, both at once, and do not look back. The woman in yellow squeezes your hand at a corner and says, "You're closer than you think," and walks off down a side street that seems to fold shut behind her like a page.

You and the old man walk on. The birdcage swings between you.

"Why is it empty?" you ask.

"It isn't," he says, and holds it up, and you see that it is your reflection inside the bars, small and patient, sitting on the little wooden perch. Waiting.

He hands it to you. It weighs nothing.

"Almost there," he says, and then he is a lamppost, and has perhaps always been a lamppost, and the street is dark on either side of its single light.

You are alone again. But it is different now. The alone has a shape, and the shape is a door at the end of the street, a door you recognize by its color before you recognize it by anything else, the exact blue your father painted it the summer you were seven and complained about the smell.

You walk toward it. The birdcage bumps softly against your knee. Behind you the city makes its enormous, indifferent noise, and for the first time it sounds less like a threat and more like a sea you have finally reached the edge of.

Someone is still with you. The one whose face you never managed to see. They stand a little behind your left shoulder, and you understand you will not turn around this time either, because you know now who it is, and knowing is enough.

You put your hand on the blue door. It is warm.

"Thank you," you say, to all of them, to the street, to the dark.

"You found it," says the voice behind you, and it is your own voice, younger, unafraid.

The door opens inward onto light, and the birdcage is suddenly empty, truly empty, the little door swung wide, and somewhere above you in the narrow strip of sky something small and quick is flying, and you step through.
