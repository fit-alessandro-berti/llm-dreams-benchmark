...written in a language I almost recognize. The letters look like English, but when I try to read them, they slide around — the words rearrange themselves like ants scattering when you lift a stone.

I look up. The exam hall is enormous, stretching back farther than any building should, rows and rows of desks fading into a gray haze. Every other seat is empty. Somewhere far away, a clock is ticking, but the ticks are coming too slowly, each one stretched out like taffy.

I pick up my pen. It's the pen from my third-grade classroom, the one with the chewed cap, and my hand feels like it belongs to someone else. I write my name at the top of the page — or at least I try to. The letters come out backwards. Then upside down. Then they turn into small insects and walk off the edge of the desk.

The first question finally holds still long enough for me to read it:

*"Explain everything you have ever failed to learn."*

Below it are three lines for the answer, and the lines are moving — rippling like the surface of water. I realize I've been sitting here for hours. Or maybe years. My coffee cup beside me is full again, though I don't remember refilling it, and it's steaming, and it's also the clock, ticking.

I hear footsteps. The professor is walking down the aisle toward me, but their face keeps changing — first my mother, then a stranger, then my twelve-year-old self, then no face at all, just a smooth blank oval like an egg.

"You have four minutes left," they say. But their mouth doesn't move, and the voice is coming from inside my own chest.

I look down. My answer sheet is covered in writing I don't remember doing — hundreds of lines of my own handwriting, saying things like *I'm sorry* and *I tried* and *the answer is the door, the answer was always the door.*

I turn around. There's a door in the wall behind me that wasn't there before. It's slightly open, and warm yellow light is spilling through the crack, and I can hear — faintly — someone calling my name. Not angry. Just calling, the way you call someone home for dinner.

The clock ticks one final time, a sound like a bell, like a slam, like —

I wake up. My heart is pounding. The ceiling of my bedroom is gray in the early light. For a moment I can still feel the pen in my hand, and I lie there wondering what grade I got, and whether any of it counted, and why I feel — strangely, impossibly — like I passed.
