You start to run, though you're not sure toward what. The streets here obey some older, stranger geometry—every corner you turn deposits you back where you began, except the buildings have subtly rearranged themselves, like furniture moved by someone while you slept.

A woman selling flowers from a cart calls out to you by name. You've never seen her before, but she says, "You're going the wrong way," and points down an alley that definitely wasn't there a moment ago. Her face keeps almost resolving into someone you know—your third-grade teacher? Your sister?—but never quite lands.

In the alley, the pavement goes soft and warm beneath your feet, like walking on bread dough. You check your phone: the screen shows only a photograph of a door, and beneath it a countdown reading 43 minutes, then 42, then 4 hours, then 41. Time is misbehaving again.

You find the meeting eventually—it's inside a laundromat, behind the dryers, up a staircase made of filing cabinets. Everyone is already seated, and they all turn to look when you enter. They are all versions of yourself at different ages. The oldest one, near the window, shakes his head slowly—not angry, just tired, the way you feel when you've arrived somewhere important and can no longer remember why it mattered.

Someone hands you papers to present. The text stays legible only for as long as you aren't looking directly at it. You begin speaking anyway, and your voice comes out as someone else's—deeper, calmer, certain. And the words make sense. That's the strangest part. In the dream, everything finally makes sense, and you understand that the meeting was never about anything at all. It was simply the place you were meant to arrive.

Outside the window, the city begins folding itself up like a map, streets creasing over streets, until there's only the table, the faces, and a light coming from nowhere in particular.

The oldest version of you leans forward and says, quietly, "You know you're dreaming, right?"

And you do know. But knowing doesn't lift you out. Instead the dream accepts the fact and continues, unbothered—the way water accepts a stone—and you realize the meeting has ended, though no one left, and the hour you were worried about never actually passed, because it was never really an hour. It was just the shape your worry took so it would have somewhere to sit.

Somewhere far above the folding city, an alarm clock is ringing in a bedroom you'll remember only as a feeling: the particular relief of being late to nothing.
