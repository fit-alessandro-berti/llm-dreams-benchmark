You start to run, but the city keeps rearranging itself around you—streets fold like maps, alleys breathe open and shut, and every clock you pass shows a different time. You stop a man in a bright yellow coat and ask where the meeting is. He hands you a business card with no name and an address that keeps changing as you read it. “Take the tram,” he says. “The one that looks like it’s already leaving.”

You chase the tram and catch it just as the doors sigh shut. The conductor smiles like she knows you. “You’re late,” she says, “for the meeting you’re supposed to have with yourself.” The tram glides through the unfamiliar city, past buildings made of glass and rain, past crowds of people all carrying the same crumpled note. Finally, it stops in front of a quiet tower that reflects you back at yourself, slightly older, slightly kinder.

Inside, there is a long table. Sitting around it are versions of you: the child, the stranger, the one who is tired, the one who is brave. They wait patiently. No one has an agenda. You sit down and say, “I’m sorry—I forgot where I was going.” The oldest version of you nods. “That’s all right,” they say. “We were waiting to decide what matters.”

You open your mouth to answer, and the room fills with morning light. You wake just before you say the words, but for the rest of the day you carry the strange address with you: not a place, exactly, but a direction.
