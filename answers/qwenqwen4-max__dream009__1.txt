…find water before I find out what century I'm in.

That's the first thing the dream tells me, and I obey it the way you obey everything in dreams — completely, without asking why.

The road is dirt packed hard as fired clay, and it runs between two fields of something grain-like that leans all one direction though there's no wind. My shoes are wrong. Canvas sneakers, fluorescent laces, the loudest objects in the world. I keep walking on the grass verge to keep them quiet.

A woman is drawing water from a well with a rope over her shoulder, and she doesn't look up, and she says a name. It isn't my name. But it fits me the way a key fits — I feel the tumblers turn — and I answer to it, and my throat makes a sound in a language I don't know, and the sound means *yes, I'm back, sorry it took so long.*

She hands me the bucket. It's heavier than a bucket should be. Inside, the water is very still and shows a sky with too many birds in it.

I understand, then, in the way you understand things in dreams — not by learning it but by having always known it — that I have a job here. There is a thing that must be carried to the top of the hill before the light goes orange, and if I don't carry it, something enormous and unnameable will be lost. Not my life. Something worse than my life. Something like *the possibility of ever having been born.*

So I climb.

The hill has stairs cut into it, worn concave in the middle by feet, thousands of years of feet, and I think: *I am one of the feet.* Halfway up there's a doorway with no house around it, just a frame standing in the grass, and through it I can see the same hill but in winter. I don't go through. In dreams you always know which doors are for later.

In my pocket there is a phone. Of course there is. It's warm, it's at 3%, and the screen shows a photograph of the woman at the well, except she's wearing a hospital gown and the well is a parking lot. I try to show it to a boy herding goats, but the screen has become a small brown moth, and it sits on my palm with its wings closed, and the boy says something gentle and unsurprised, and I put the moth in my shirt pocket where it stays very still, like a heartbeat that's waiting.

At the top of the hill there's a stone basin, dry, cracked, older than the stairs.

I pour the bucket in. The water doesn't fill it. The water goes *down*, endlessly, a thin silver thread unspooling into the dark, and I realize the basin isn't a basin, it's a throat, and the hill is drinking, and far below in the valley the grain begins to lean the other way.

The light goes orange. I made it.

And the woman's voice comes from directly behind my ear, though she is a mile below, and she says the name again — the not-my-name — but this time she says it the way you'd say it to someone who has to wake up now.

The moth in my pocket starts to vibrate.

It's my alarm.

I'm coming up out of the hill, out of the orange, out of the water going down forever, and for about four seconds I'm in both places, and I can feel the bucket handle grooved into my palm —

and then it's just the ceiling, and the room, and the small bright rectangle in my hand, buzzing.

3%.

I go to the kitchen and drink a glass of water standing up, and it tastes faintly of stone.
