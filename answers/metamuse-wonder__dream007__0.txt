The words you'd been about to throw hang in the air between you — literally. In the strange logic of the dream, every sharp thing you've both said is floating there now, fragile as glass ornaments. One wrong move and they'll all shatter.

Your friend is standing in your kitchen, but it's also the playground from when you were kids. It's raining inside, just a soft, warm rain that doesn't make anything wet.

"I don't actually care about being right," they say, and their voice sounds younger. "I just thought if I lost this argument, I'd lose you."

You look down and realize you're both holding opposite ends of the same long, tangled scarf. All this time you thought you were pulling against each other, but you've actually been trying to untangle the same knot.

So you decide to do the one thing you never do in an argument. You sit down. Right there on the wet-not-wet kitchen floor, and you pat the space beside you.

After a moment, they sit too.

You don't solve it. The knot doesn't magically come undone. But side by side, it's easier to work at. Your hands keep bumping into each other's, and at some point that makes you both start laughing — not because anything is funny, but because you're suddenly exhausted and relieved at the same time.

"I hate fighting with you," you say.

"Then let's not be good at it," they reply.

A phone starts ringing somewhere deep inside the walls, getting louder and louder, and the playground-kitchen begins to fade at the edges like a photograph in sunlight.

You try to hold onto the scarf, to remember what the knot looked like from the same side —

And then you wake up, with that last sentence still clear in your head, and the odd, calm feeling that the argument was never really about who won.
