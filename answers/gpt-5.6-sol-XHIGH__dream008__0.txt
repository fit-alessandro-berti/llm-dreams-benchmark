You ask what you are supposed to deliver.

Your boss leans close enough that his tie falls over your shoulder. Tiny gold fish are swimming across it. “The building,” he whispers, as if reminding you of something obvious. “We need it finished before it closes.”

All around you, your coworkers begin typing faster. Their keyboards sound like hard rain. You look toward the windows and discover there are no windows anymore, only framed photographs of windows, each showing a different weather. In one it is snowing upward. In another, the sun hangs from a crane.

The countdown reaches six minutes.

You type the word BUILDING into the blank window. The floor gives a small jolt. A gray wall rises between two desks, pushing papers and coffee cups gently aside. Nobody looks up. Encouraged, you type DOOR, and a green door appears beside the printer. Your boss nods, but his face has acquired the disappointed expression he usually saves for quarterly meetings.

“It has to contain everything,” he says.

You do not know what everything means, but your hands begin moving by themselves. You type staircases, elevators, kitchens, sleeping rooms, archives, gardens. With every word, the office rearranges itself. Vines spill from fluorescent lights. An elevator opens where the supply cabinet used to be, revealing a moonlit field. File drawers extend into corridors so long that their far ends vanish in mist. Your coworkers keep typing, though their desks are now scattered on balconies and rooftops at impossible heights.

At four minutes, an email arrives from yourself. The subject is URGENT: MISSING ITEM. There is no message, only an attachment shaped like a small locked door. When you click it, you hear knocking beneath your desk.

You crawl down and find a trapdoor in the carpet. Your boss crouches beside you, somehow still standing at the same time. “You forgot the basement,” both versions of him say.

The trapdoor opens onto a staircase descending through years instead of floors. Each step is labeled with a date from your life. You hurry down, passing a childhood birthday, a hospital waiting room, a summer afternoon you had completely forgotten. Voices drift from behind the walls, asking whether the project is done. The countdown follows you, projected in red on every landing.

At the bottom is a vast underground office. Thousands of empty chairs face a single desk, and on that desk lies a rolled blueprint tied with a red thread. You untie it. The paper is warm and pulses under your fingertips.

The blueprint shows the building you have been making. Its corridors twist into the shape of a sleeping animal. At the center is a tiny square marked YOU ARE HERE, but the square moves whenever you look away. Near the edge of the page is an unbuilt room labeled THE REASON.

Two minutes.

You grab a pencil and draw the missing room. At once, a wall behind the desk splits open. Beyond it is your original office on the morning of your first day. The desks are new, the plants are alive, and no one knows your name yet. Your younger self is sitting in your chair, waiting for someone to explain what the job will be.

You step through. The younger you looks up and asks, “Did we finish?”

You want to say yes, but the word will not come. Instead, all the things you have ever left unfinished begin fluttering from your mouth: half-written messages, unmade apologies, abandoned plans, books with bookmarks near the beginning. They circle the room like moths. Your younger self opens a desk drawer, and they pour into it until the drawer contains a night sky full of restless stars.

One minute.

Your boss appears in the doorway, but he is very old now. The fish have escaped his tie and swim slowly around his head. He holds out a timesheet that is hundreds of pages long.

“What goes in the last room?” he asks.

You look again at the moving square on the blueprint. Suddenly you understand that the building cannot contain everything while you remain outside it, trying to complete it. You take the pencil and draw yourself into THE REASON. The line tickles across your skin. Your body becomes thin and blue, then folds neatly into the plan.

You are inside the building now. You can feel every door and pipe, every humming light, every person hurrying along your corridors. You feel the forgotten summer afternoon warming one of your windows. You feel the moth-stars settling into the ceiling. Deep in the basement, something enormous turns over in its sleep.

Ten seconds.

There is still no roof.

Everyone begins shouting at once. Rain pours down through the open floors, soaking computers and gardens and childhood photographs. Your boss points upward, his mouth opening and closing like one of the fish. The DELIVER button floats above the building, enormous and red, just beyond reach.

You stretch every staircase toward it. The staircases bend together into a hand. At one second, the highest step touches the button.

Everything becomes silent.

For a moment you believe you have succeeded. Then the building exhales. Its walls soften, its elevators fill with dark water, and the whole immense structure rises from the ground like a paper lantern. You watch it drift into the artificial sky, carrying your coworkers, your younger self, and all the unfinished stars. Your boss stands beside you in an empty field, holding nothing but the loose end of his tie.

“Is that where it was supposed to go?” you ask.

He shrugs. Without the building, he looks small and unfamiliar. “There was never a deadline,” he says. “Only a door that wanted to know whether you would open it.”

Behind him, the green door is standing alone in the grass. You open it and find your bedroom on the other side, faintly lit by morning. Before stepping through, you glance back. High above, the drifting building has become a new constellation, its windows blinking one by one as the people inside turn off their lights.

On your pillow lies a tiny gold fish, breathing calmly. When you pick it up, it unfolds into a note in your own handwriting.

It says: BEGIN.
