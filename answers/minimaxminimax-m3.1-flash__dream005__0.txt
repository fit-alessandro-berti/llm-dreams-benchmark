...various groups of people and conversations that never quite make sense.

The first group is three men in identical grey coats, arguing in a language you understand perfectly but cannot repeat afterward. They fall silent as you pass. The tall one — whose face you can see but can't hold in memory — nods at you, as though you're expected. Then all three turn back to their argument, and you realize, with a lurch, that the argument is about you.

Beyond them, a woman is telling a story with her hands. Her hands *are* the story: little lights flicker between her fingers, a sudden small rain, a door opening and closing. Everyone around her listens with the rapt attention of the very bored. You lean in. The lights go out. She looks at you and says, "You weren't in this part," and the circle closes.

You keep walking.

The room is larger than any room has a right to be. The ceiling is high and low at once, like a held breath. People drift past carrying drinks that never spill and names you almost recognize. A man in a velvet suit keeps appearing at the edge of your vision, always three steps ahead, and every time you nearly catch him, he is entertaining somebody else, and he is laughing far too loudly at whatever they say.

That's when you notice the music. It comes from everywhere and it is *almost* — almost — a song you know. You can't place it. It sits just behind your hearing, like the memory of a song.

And then you remember: her name. Margaret. Or Maureen. It slides away each time you reach for it, but it returns stronger in the aftermath, the way a word on the tip of your tongue will not stay fetched. You know three things about her. She is here. She came looking for you. And she will not look at you first.

The rules of the gathering assemble themselves without anyone announcing them. You cannot ask for her directly. You cannot say her name out loud — you try, once, in a hallway, and the name comes out as static; the woman beside you hears static too, and looks relieved, and something in the whole room exhales.

So you search by signs.

A coat on a coat rack, familiar. A laugh in a doorway, wrong in pitch but the right in shape. A hand — the back of it, the wrist, a small old scar — and your heart lurches so hard you stumble into them. It's someone else's hand. You apologize. They say, "You were looking for someone." You say yes. They say, "She went out." You say, "Out where?" And they say, "She's always out," and smile like a door.

The party does not thin so much as *empty*, the way a dream empties: the same room, fewer people, and now all the chairs are facing the same direction.

Down a corridor of tall windows, the night outside is another room full of people, all of them pressed against the glass, watching you with the polite attention of a crowd. You wave. They wave back, all together, in unison, and the glass fogs from their side.

Then: the kitchen. Someone is making coffee at three in the morning, or in a dream the hours don't hold. Steam. A cup set out for you, and a second cup, still full.

And there she is, at the counter, back turned, humming something tuneless and absolutely hers.

Your heart does the thing. You know this is the trick of it — the dream knows you know — and you walk toward her slowly, because rushing has never once worked in a dream, has it? You say her name. It comes out clear this time, a little hoarse, as though you'd been shouting it for hours.

She turns around.

It isn't her face. It's yours. A little older. A little tired. Wearing an expression of profound, patient relief.

"You took the long way," she says, in your voice. "I was starting to think I'd have to be you again."

The coffee goes cold. The crowd at the window presses closer. The humming starts up again behind you, in a voice you almost know.

"Do you want to finish the dream," she says, "or do you want to wake up? Because if you wake up, I won't be here. And if I don't wake up—" she gestures at the gathering, the endless hall, the grey-coated men, the lights between the fingers — "well. There's more party."

And you understand the terrible, comfortable truth: there is no one else here at all. Every face in this room is a face you've worn, or will wear, or half-remember wearing. The only person you've ever known in a crowd of strangers is yourself, and you are looking for her, and she is looking for you, and neither of you will admit it first.

You take the cup. It is warm. It tastes like the smell of a coat rack, of a hallway, of a name on the tip of your tongue.

"It's good you found me," she says. "I was beginning to worry I'd lost you in the crowd."

*You wake up. Your mouth is dry. For a moment — just a moment — you can't remember anyone's name at all.*
