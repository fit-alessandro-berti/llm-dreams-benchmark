You begin to speak—and the words come out as lanterns. Each sentence lifts off your tongue, floats a few feet above the crowd, and hangs there, glowing. People reach up and take them. Some lanterns turn into birds. Some become keys. One old man in the third row catches a sentence that reads *you already know the way home*, and he starts to cry, quietly, the way rain starts.

You look down at your notes. They are blank, then they are a map of a city you have never visited but somehow remember. The stage floor softens under your shoes. It is moss. Somewhere behind the curtain, a train is arriving, though there is no station.

You say the thing you were afraid to say. The microphone does not amplify it; the room does. Every face in the audience is someone you have loved, or will, or almost did. They are all nodding, not because the speech is perfect, but because in the dream, being heard is the same as being understood.

When you finish, there is no applause. There is weather. A warm wind moves through the aisles, and the lanterns go out one by one, gently, like eyes closing. You step off the stage into a hallway that smells like rain on hot pavement, and you are not late for anything.
