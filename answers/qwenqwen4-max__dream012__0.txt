...and you find that your hands already know what to do, the way hands do in dreams.

"Strike the topsails," you say, and your voice arrives before you decide to speak it — low, unhurried, a voice you have never owned but have always been able to borrow. The crew moves. Ropes run through their fists like water through fingers. Above you the canvas cracks once, a sound like a door slamming in a house you grew up in, and then it is gone, taken by the wind, and no one mourns it.

The helmsman is your father, though he is young, younger than you are now, and he will not look at you. He watches the compass, whose needle is not pointing north but turning slowly, patiently, like something grazing.

Rain begins to fall upward. You notice this without alarm. The deck grows wet from below, and the sea throws its own water back into the sky, and you understand, in the flat certain way of dreams, that the storm is not attacking you. It is asking you something. It has been asking for a long time and you have been answering with sails and rigging and orders, which are not answers.

You go below to consult the chart. The chart is a page from a book you were reading as a child, and the coastline drawn on it is the shape of a face asleep on a pillow. Someone has penciled a small X in the hollow of its throat. The lantern swings. Its light does not move across the table; the table moves through the light.

When you come back on deck, the crew has stopped working. They are all facing you, and their faces are the faces of everyone who has ever waited for you to say something — and you see, with a lurch that is not the ship's, that they are not frightened of the water. They are frightened of you. They have been at sea for years. They have forgotten there is such a thing as land, and they have kept going only because you kept saying *north*, and *hold*, and *soon*.

The wheel is warm under your palms. It has a pulse.

"Let it come," you tell them. "Turn us into it."

Your father turns the wheel. The wave rises — it goes up past the mast, past the height where anything should stop, and it hangs there, green and lit from inside, and in its curve you can see the whole sea folded down and down like a corridor of mirrors: ship inside wave inside ship inside wave, and in every one of them a captain with your hands saying something to a crew who is listening.

The wave does not break. It leans in close, the way an animal does when it wants to be recognized.

And you take off your coat, because you are suddenly, unbearably warm, and you realize the storm has been the sound of your own breathing this whole time — and there is a light left on somewhere, in a room with no water in it at all, and someone has been calling your name from the shore of it for a while now, gently, and not for the first time.
