It is a foolish decision. Everyone tells you so.

“You can’t eat hope,” says the woman who trades batteries beneath the overpass.

But she gives you a dented watering can anyway.

You choose a patch of earth behind the abandoned library, where the walls still hold a little warmth after sunset. You clear broken glass with your bare hands. You bury three beans, a shriveled potato, and a seed you found inside a locket.

Every night, something walks around the garden.

You hear its feet dragging through the ash. In the morning, there are enormous footprints between the rows, careful not to crush anything.

On the seventh night, you stay awake.

The visitor arrives carrying a rain cloud in its antlers.

It is taller than the library doors, made of roots and rusted wire. Where its face should be, an old traffic light glows amber.

You ought to run.

Instead, you point to the watering can.

“I’ve been trying,” you tell it, “but there isn’t enough.”

The creature kneels. Rain falls quietly over the little beds. You hold out your hands, then fetch every bucket you own.

By morning, green shoots have broken through the soil.

People come to stare. Some laugh. One man weeps without making a sound. The woman from the overpass brings a sack of dirt she has carried for eleven miles.

That evening, you notice the seed from the locket has grown into a small wooden door.

Something knocks from underneath.

You kneel beside it, surrounded by the smell of wet earth.

“Who’s there?” you whisper.

A child answers.

“Is it safe to come out yet?”

You look at the ruined buildings, the gathering neighbors, the strange creature asleep against the library wall.

“Not quite,” you say. “But you can stay with me.”
