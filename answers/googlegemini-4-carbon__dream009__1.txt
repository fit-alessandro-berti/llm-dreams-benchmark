Instinctively, you shove your hands deep into your pockets, only to realize you aren't wearing your own clothes anymore. You are wrapped in a heavy coat of coarse,undyed wool that smells faintly of cedar and woodsmoke. Beneath your boots, there is no asphalt, only uneven cobblestones slick with morning mist.

Looking up, the sky feels impossibly vast, unbroken by power lines, skyscrapers, or the distant whine of airplanes. Timber-framed houses lean inward over the narrow lane like sleepy giants sharing secrets. A horse-drawn cart rattles past, its wooden wheels groaning under the weight of oak barrels, and the driver tips a felt hat to you without breaking stride. 

In the strange, unquestioning logic of dreams, panic fades into sudden, absolute purpose. Your fingers brush against something cold in your coat pocket. You pull it out: it isn't your phone, though it has the exact same weight. It is a tarnished brass compass, its needle spinning wildly before locking onto the end of the street, toward the harbor. 

You walk toward the water, weaving past fishmongers shouting prices in a thick, archaic dialect, past women carrying wicker baskets of lavender and warm loaves of bread, and past a town crier whose words seem to dissolve into flocks of sparrows the moment they leave his mouth. 

At the docks, a massive wooden galleon is moored, its canvas sails glowing amber in the rising sun. Though you have never seen this ship before, you know with total certainty that you are the navigator, and the crew has been waiting a hundred years for you to wake up. 

You step onto the creaking wooden gangplank. Just as your boot touches the deck, the ship’s heavy brass bell begins to toll. It rings once, twice, three times—growing louder, sharper, and strangely rhythmic, until the scent of salt air fades into the warmth of cotton sheets, and the ship's bell becomes the steady chime of your morning alarm.
