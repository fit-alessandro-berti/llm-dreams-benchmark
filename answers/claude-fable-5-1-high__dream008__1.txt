You are at your workplace, and suddenly, you are given a project with an impossible deadline. Your boss is waiting for you to start, and you nod as if you understand, though the brief he handed you is a single sheet of paper with nothing on it but a date. The date is today. Not the end of today; just today, as if the day itself were the deadline and every minute you spent reading the sheet was a minute already owed.

He does not leave. He stands beside your desk with his hands folded behind his back, the way people stand at the edge of a pool waiting for someone else to jump. You turn to your monitor and it is on, but the screen shows the room you are sitting in, filmed from somewhere above and behind you, so that you can see your own shoulders and the back of your own head and your boss standing patiently at your left. When you move your hand toward the mouse, the hand on the screen moves a half-second later. You decide not to think about this.

"What is the project?" you ask, and your voice comes out reasonable, almost cheerful.

"It's the one we discussed," he says.

You have never discussed anything with him. You are fairly sure you have never seen him before, though he has the face of every manager you have ever had, layered one over the other like tracing paper, so that when he turns his head slightly a different man looks out. Still, the sentence lands in you as true. You did discuss it. You just cannot remember where the memory is kept.

You open a folder. Inside is another folder with the same name. Inside that one is a document titled FINAL, and inside FINAL is a paragraph you recognize as your own writing, in your own voice, describing a project you are certain you have never worked on. It is good. It is better than anything you would write on purpose. It ends mid-sentence, and the cursor blinks after the last word like something waiting to be fed.

The office is filling up. You did not notice people arriving, but every desk is now occupied, and each person is typing steadily and none of them are looking at their screens. They are looking at you. Not unkindly. The way a choir looks at whoever is about to sing the solo.

"They're all on it too," your boss says. "But it's your part that's holding things up."

You put your fingers on the keys. You know, with the flat certainty that only arrives in dreams, that you can finish this. That the sentence wants to be completed and the completion is already inside you somewhere, the way a word you've forgotten is still inside your mouth. You type a letter and it appears on the screen a half-second late, and then another, and then you look at what you've written and it says: I am at my workplace, and suddenly, I am given a project with an impossible deadline.

You delete it. You type again. It says the same thing.

The clock on the wall does not have hands. It has a small window, and through the window you can see the sky over the building, and the sky is the color it gets just before it decides between evening and night. You understand that this is the clock, and that it is running.

Your boss leans closer. He smells like the inside of a new car and like your father's coat. "You're overthinking it," he says gently. "Just write what happens next."

So you do. You stop trying to write the project and instead you write the room: the choir of coworkers, the windowed clock, the man made of managers. You write the screen watching you. You write your own hands. And as you write each thing, it goes quiet. The typing around you stops, desk by desk, as each coworker is described and released. The clock, once written, holds its color. Your boss, when you reach him, does not disappear but sits down on the edge of your desk, tired in a way that seems to have taken years, and reads over your shoulder without comment.

The last thing left is the deadline. The sheet of paper with today's date on it is still beside your keyboard. You pick it up to write it down and see that the date has changed; it is now a date you don't recognize, far off, in a year that hasn't been invented yet. Or it is the date of your birth. It is hard to tell in this light.

"There," your boss says. "That wasn't impossible."

"It was," you say. "I just didn't do the thing you asked."

He shrugs, and in the shrug you see him become, briefly, someone you actually liked once, a long time ago. "Nobody does," he says. "That's why we give it to people in their sleep."

The screen shows the room from above. On it, you can see yourself lower your head to the desk, the way you did in school when the teacher turned the lights off for a film. Your hand on the screen closes the document. Your hand at the desk is already still. Somewhere the choir has begun, softly, to hum a tune you know and cannot name, and the impossible deadline arrives, and passes, and nothing that was supposed to happen happens, and it is fine, it is fine, you are allowed to rest now, the building is closing around you like a hand.
