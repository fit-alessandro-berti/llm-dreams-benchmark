They scatter through the auditorium, brushing against the lights and circling the balconies. As each bird passes, you glimpse a sentence written across its wings: *I’m sorry. I don’t know. I wanted to stay. Please don’t go.*

The audience does not laugh. One by one, people raise their hands, and the birds settle into their palms.

A child in the front row catches the last one. She reads its message aloud: “I was afraid you wouldn’t like me.”

A murmur moves through the crowd—not of judgment, but of recognition. Then everyone opens their hands. From between their fingers rise thousands more birds, carrying all the things they, too, have never said.

For a while, the hall is filled with wings.

You look down at your speech. Every page is blank except the last, where a single line has appeared:

**Now it’s your turn.**

So you step away from the microphone. You don’t clear your throat. You don’t pretend to be calm.

“I’m afraid,” you say.

Your voice is quiet, but somehow everyone hears it.

“So are we,” the audience answers.

The ceiling opens then—not breaking, simply unfolding like a paper flower—and beyond it is a pale morning sky. The birds rise together, carrying the unfinished speech, the empty chairs, and finally the stage itself.

You remain standing in the soft blue air, strangely light.

When you wake, your room is silent. But on the pillow beside you is a single white feather, and for just a moment, you can remember exactly what you were going to say.
