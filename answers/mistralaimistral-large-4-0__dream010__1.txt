Next, you must cross a bridge of transparent glass suspended over a canyon filled not with water, but with liquid "what ifs" that whisper your insecurities back to you in your own voice. To cross, you cannot look down. You must walk backward, trusting that your footsteps will find purchase in the invisible path ahead while the other competitors—shadowy figures wearing your face but with hollow, jealous eyes—try to block your way. They offer you comfortable distractions: a warm bed, a familiar routine, the anesthesia of "someday."

At the summit, the trophy isn't gold or crystal, but a mirror made of morning light. When you look into it, you don't see a winner holding a prize. You see yourself, fully awake within the dream, understanding that the competition was never against the others—they were all reflections of your own hesitation, dissolving now into mist. The "dream job" reveals itself not as a corner office or a title, but as the simple, terrifying, beautiful realization that you were already qualified the moment you dared to enter the arena.

You reach out to touch the mirror, your fingers passing through the glass like water, and feel the weight of your pillow pressing against your cheek as the alarm begins its song—but you carry the baton with you, tucked safely in your pocket, ready for the waking world.
