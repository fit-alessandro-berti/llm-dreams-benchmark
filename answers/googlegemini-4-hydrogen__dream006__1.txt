A cold flutter of panic hits your stomach. You look down at the note cards trembling in your hands, only to find that the ink has melted into tiny silver fish swimming across the cardstock. When you try to read them, they scatter into the corners.

You glance up, bracing yourself for laughter or boos, but the audience hasn't blinked. Every single person in the sprawling auditorium is leaning forward with intense, solemn focus, nodding slowly as if your clock-chime was the most profound thing they’ve ever heard. In the front row, someone who looks suspiciously like your third-grade teacher is furiously taking notes on a napkin.

"What I mean to say is—" you start again, gripping the edges of the podium to steady yourself. Beneath your palms, the polished oak feels strangely soft, like warm bread dough. 

Suddenly, the stage tilts slightly to the left. You realize you’re no longer wearing shoes, and the floor beneath you isn't hardwood anymore—it’s ankle-deep in cool, clear ocean water. Nobody in the crowd seems to notice, even as the velvet theater seats begin to bob gently like buoys. 

You know you have to finish the speech. It feels desperately important, even though the topic keeps slipping out of your head like a wet bar of soap—something about the weight of forgotten passwords, or maybe why the moon is always late on Thursdays. 

You lean toward the microphone, only to see it stretching upward like a vine, blooming into a bright yellow megaphone just out of reach. When you jump to grab it, gravity forgets to pull you back down. You drift upward, floating effortlessly above the submerged rows of applauding silhouettes, rising toward the high ceiling where the chandelier has turned into a glowing flock of paper lanterns—and just behind them, slightly ajar, is the front door to your childhood home.
