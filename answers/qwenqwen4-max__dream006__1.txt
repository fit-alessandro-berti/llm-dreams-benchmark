...begin to speak, and the first word out of my mouth is a word I have never heard before, though I know exactly what it means. It means *the feeling of arriving somewhere you have already been*. The audience nods. Of course. That was the title of the talk.

I look down at my notes. They are written in my handwriting, but slanted the wrong way, like handwriting seen in a mirror, and though I can read them perfectly out of the corner of my eye, each line goes blank the moment I look at it directly. This is fine. I have given this speech many times. I have given it in this room, which I have never entered, on nights I don't remember.

The microphone is warm. It is slightly soft, like the skin of a peach, and I decide not to think about that.

The hall is longer than it was when I walked in. The back rows are far enough away that they have their own weather — a small grey drizzle falling over the folding chairs, and people there have opened umbrellas, politely, quietly, so as not to interrupt me. In the front row sits my grandmother, young, younger than I am, wearing a coat I recognize from a photograph. She is not dead here. She is simply early. She checks her watch and mouths *keep going*, and I understand that she has somewhere to be afterward, and that this is not sad, it's just scheduling.

I say the thing I came to say. It comes out whole and clean, the way sentences never do when I'm awake — no hedging, no *sort of*, no restarting. It is the truest thing I have ever said and it takes four seconds and I can feel it land in the room like a stone dropped into deep water. Everyone hears it. Everyone has always known it. They are only glad I finally said it out loud.

Then I notice I am holding not notes but a set of house keys, and I have been thanking the audience for coming to see the house. There is a door behind me that was a projection screen a moment ago. Behind the door is the hallway of my childhood home, lit at that particular four o'clock angle, and the smell of it comes out into the auditorium and reaches the back rows, and the rain there stops.

I step through to get something I left in the kitchen. It's important. It's the one thing I forgot.

The audience begins to applaud, and the applause is the sound of the shower running upstairs, and someone is calling my name from a room I have not been in for twenty years, and I am running toward it, and the hallway is getting longer the way hallways do —
