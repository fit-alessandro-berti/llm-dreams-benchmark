You notice the questions are not questions at all.

The paper is blank except for one line at the top, printed in your own handwriting: *You forgot to call Mom back.*

You flip the page. The next page is a photograph of your childhood bedroom. The next is a grocery list you wrote three Tuesdays ago. Then a page of math problems you studied for, but the numbers keep sliding off the page like wet ink.

You raise your hand. The proctor, who looks exactly like your high school guidance counselor, doesn't look up. "You have three hours," he says, though his mouth doesn't move.

You try to write an answer anyway. Your pen leaves no mark. When you look down, the desk is gone and you're sitting on the floor of an empty classroom that is somehow also your kitchen at 2 a.m. The exam booklet is now a stack of unsent texts, old permission slips, a résumé with the job title erased.

A bell rings. No one stands up. You realize you've been staring at the same first question for what feels like an hour, and the words have changed while you weren't looking.

They now read: *What were you supposed to be doing right now?*

You wake up before you can answer.
