You are at a large social gathering where you only know one person. As you try to find them, you encounter various groups of people and every group seems to be in the middle of a conversation that started long before you arrived and somehow includes you. A woman in a green dress says, "We were just talking about you," and everyone turns, smiling, holding glasses of something amber that never spills no matter how they tilt them.

You smile back and say you're looking for someone. "Aren't we all," says a man whose face you almost recognize — a teacher, maybe, or a cousin, or someone from a bus you took years ago. He points toward a hallway you're certain wasn't there a moment ago.

The hallway is lined with coats. Hundreds of coats, and you understand without being told that one of them is yours, though you don't remember wearing one. You run your hand along the sleeves as you walk, and the hallway keeps producing more of itself, the party's noise thinning behind you into something like the ocean heard through a wall.

At the end there is a kitchen, too small for the size of the house, and in it three people are laughing at a joke that ended before you came in. "You just missed them," one says. "They went up to see the roof." You didn't say who you were looking for. You never say the name — you realize you can't quite remember the name, only the certainty of the person, the shape of them, the way it will feel when you finally see them across a room.

The stairs go up longer than any house allows. On the landing, a child is sorting buttons into cups and tells you, seriously, "You're not late. Everyone here is early." You want to ask what that means but the child has become a plant in a brass pot, and it seems rude to question a plant.

The roof is a garden. The party is here too — it was always here, apparently — the same woman in green, the same amber glasses, the same laughter unspooling. The city below has rearranged itself into streets you knew as a child. And there, at the far edge by the railing, someone is standing with their back to you, and you know it's them. You know it the way you know things in dreams, completely and without evidence.

You cross the roof. It takes a very long time. Guests drift past like weather, offering you small food you never manage to taste. The distance stretches and folds. You call out — not a name, just a sound, "Hey" — and the person begins to turn.

They turn slowly, the way a page turns, and just before you see their face, you understand something that makes your chest go warm and loose: it doesn't matter who it is. It was never about finding them. The whole party, the coats, the stairs — all of it was just the long, patient work of getting you to this railing, this rooftop, this soft blue hour.

The face, when it comes, is your own. Younger, or maybe older, smiling like a host.

"There you are," you both say, at exactly the same time.

And the party applauds, gently, the way rain starts, and you lean on the railing together, watching the streets of your childhood glow like something remembered by someone else, until the light comes up slow and even, and the gathering folds itself away, guest by guest, and you wake with the feeling — lasting only a few seconds, but real — that you were, all night, exactly where you were supposed to be.
