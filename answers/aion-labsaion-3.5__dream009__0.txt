...find food, water, and shelter before nightfall.

The cobblestones beneath your bare feet are slick with morning mist. Around you, timber-framed buildings lean toward each other like old friends sharing secrets, their thatched roofs nearly touching across the narrow street. Somewhere nearby, a rooster crows, and the smell of woodsmoke and fresh bread drifts from an open doorway.

You look down at yourself—roughspun linen, a rope belt, leather shoes that are far too small. Your hands are calloused in places you don't recognize. Hands that know work.

A woman in a linen coif pauses at a well, eyeing you with suspicion. "You'll be late again," she says, and though you've never heard these words in your life, you understand them. The language sits strangely in your ears—familiar and foreign at once, like a song you knew as a child.

*Late for what?* you wonder, but your feet already know the answer. They carry you through winding streets, past a blacksmith's forge spitting orange sparks, past children chasing a pig, past a church bell tolling six times. Your body remembers a life your mind never lived.

You arrive at a baker's shop, and your hands move without instruction—kneading dough, stoking the oven, shaping loaves with a rhythm that feels like breathing. The heat from the oven warms your face. Flour dusts your forearms. And strangely, you feel *useful* in a way you can't remember ever feeling before.

As the sun climbs higher, a strange thought crosses your mind: no notifications, no screens, no noise except the honest sounds of the world—cart wheels, voices, the crackle of fire. There's a simplicity here that terrifies and soothes you at once.

But then, as you slide the last loaf from the oven, you notice something odd. The bread is glowing. Faintly, softly, like moonlight trapped in crust. The baker beside you—when did he arrive?—leans close and whispers:

"Wake up before you eat it. That's the rule."

You look at the bread. You look at him. His eyes are the eyes of your oldest friend, the one you haven't spoken to in years.

You reach for the glowing loaf anyway—

And the bell tolls a seventh time, which it should never do.

You wake, back in your own bed, phone buzzing on the nightstand, the smell of bread somehow still lingering in the air.
