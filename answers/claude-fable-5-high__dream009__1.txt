You wake up in a completely different era, with no modern technology around. As you explore your new surroundings, you realize you need to blend in quickly, before anyone notices that you don't belong.

The air smells of woodsmoke and wet straw. You are standing in a narrow lane between timber houses whose upper stories lean toward each other like old men sharing a secret. Somewhere a bell is ringing — not an alarm, not a phone, but a real bronze bell, and its sound moves through the air slowly, as if the air itself were thicker here.

Your clothes are wrong. You know this the way you know things in dreams, without checking. You duck behind a cart loaded with cabbages and find, folded neatly on a barrel as though someone left it there for you, a rough woolen cloak. It fits perfectly. Of course it does.

A woman carrying two buckets on a yoke nods at you as she passes. "You're late," she says, and you understand that in this era you have always existed, that there is a version of you here with duties and debts and a name you don't yet know. You fall into step behind her because the dream wants you to.

She leads you through a market square where merchants argue over spices you can smell from thirty paces — saffron, clove, something darker underneath. A man is selling small mirrors, and when you glance into one, your reflection is a half-second behind you. You look away before it catches up.

"The workshop," the woman says, setting down her buckets in front of a low stone building. "He's been waiting since dawn."

Inside, an old man sits among gears. Hundreds of them — wooden gears, brass gears, gears the size of coins and gears the size of wagon wheels, all turning slowly with no engine you can see. In the center of the room stands an unfinished machine, tall as a doorway, and you recognize it with a jolt: it's a clock. But where the face should be, there is a hole, and through the hole you can see, very faintly, your own bedroom — the ceiling, the corner of the window, the light of early morning.

"You know what it needs," the old man says. He doesn't look up. "You've always known. That's why you came back."

You reach into your pocket and find that you are holding your phone — the only modern thing that made the journey with you, its screen dark and dead. It is exactly the size of the hole in the clock.

"Once it's placed," the old man says, "the clock runs forward again. You go home. But everything here—" he gestures at the workshop, the market, the bell still ringing somewhere above the rooftops, "—everything here stops waiting for you."

The woman with the buckets is standing in the doorway. Behind her the square has gone quiet, and you realize all of them are watching — the spice merchants, the mirror seller, the children — not with menace but with a kind of patience, the patience of people who exist only when you are dreaming of them.

You place the phone into the hole.

It fits like a heartbeat returning to a chest. The gears catch. The whole machine shudders and begins to turn, and the bedroom on the other side of the clock face grows brighter, closer, and the old man says something you can't hear over the rising sound of the bell, which is not a bell anymore but your alarm, and the woolen cloak is your blanket, and the last thing you see before waking is the woman raising one hand — not waving goodbye, but the way you'd gesture to someone you expect to see again tomorrow.

You wake up in your own era. On the nightstand, your phone shows the correct time. But it is a half-second behind, and all day you keep catching it — that small delay — like a reflection still learning to follow.
