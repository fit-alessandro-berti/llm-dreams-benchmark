You are walking through a peaceful forest when suddenly the weather changes dramatically. You feel a storm approaching and the light drains from the leaves as if someone has turned down a dimmer. Birds go silent mid-song. The air thickens with iron and crushed ferns.

Wind arrives first as a rumor along the ground, then as a hand at your back. The trees lean toward the storm, not away from it. Their branches click like knuckles. Leaves turn their pale undersides toward you; the whole forest is listening.

You keep walking. In dreams you always keep walking.

The sky darkens to wet slate. Thunder does not crash. It unrolls from inside the trunks, a long sound that has been waiting there. Lightning does not strike. It unzips. For a moment there are two forests at once: the gold one you were walking through, and a drowned cathedral of roots where pale shapes move between the trunks.

When the rain arrives it is not rain. Thin translucent sheets slap against your face—water or paper or old photographs. On them you catch fragments: a kitchen you almost remember, a door you never opened. The rain is warm, almost like being remembered.

The storm reaches you and passes through you. You open your mouth to speak and the thunder answers in your own voice. Behind you the path is gone. Ahead, the forest inhales.

You wake with the taste of rain and the certainty that the forest is still walking.
