You wake up in a completely different era, with no modern technology around. As you explore your new surroundings, you realize you need to find out what year it is without letting anyone discover that you do not belong there.

You are lying in the middle of a road made from flat white stones. On either side, fields of wheat bend beneath a wind you cannot feel. There are no poles, no wires, no engines in the distance—only a walled city on a hill and, above it, a sky crowded with birds flying backward.

Your clothes have changed. You wear a rough blue tunic belted with rope, leather shoes, and a small iron key on a cord around your neck. The key is warm, almost feverish. When you touch it, every bell in the city begins ringing.

A cart approaches, pulled by an animal that resembles a horse until it turns its head and you see the grave, bearded face of an old man. The woman driving the cart does not appear to notice anything strange about this. She stops beside you and asks whether you are going to the coronation.

“Whose coronation?” you ask.

Her expression changes. “You had better remember before the gates close.”

She lets you climb into the back among baskets of figs. As the cart rattles uphill, you search for some clue—a coin, a date carved into stone, a familiar style of dress—but everything seems borrowed from several centuries at once. Soldiers carry bronze spears and wear powdered wigs. Monks hurry past on wooden skates. A stained-glass window glows in the wall of a building that has not yet been built.

The woman tells you the city has been waiting three hundred years for this morning. At noon, the new king will be crowned, and time will begin moving forward again.

“Which way has it been moving?” you ask.

She points behind the cart.

The road is disappearing. Not receding in the distance, but lifting stone by stone into the air, where each slab becomes a white bird and joins the backward flock.

At the gate, guards search everyone. You are suddenly certain they must not find the key. You hide it in your mouth. It tastes of rain and blood. When your turn comes, the youngest guard stares at you with such recognition that you nearly speak.

“You’re late,” he whispers.

Before you can ask what he means, he stamps your wrist with a seal of black wax and waves you through. The seal bears your own face, older than you are now.

Inside, the city folds around you like a maze remembering itself. Alleys lead upward into ceilings. Laundry hangs between towers, but the garments are full of invisible people, sleeves gesturing and skirts kicking gently in the air. Everyone is carrying a clock with no hands. They cradle them like sleeping infants.

You follow the crowd toward the palace. Somewhere nearby, a child is crying your name.

In the market square, vendors sell memories from shallow bowls: a first snowfall, a mother’s voice, the smell of a school corridor after rain. You recognize several as yours. One merchant holds up a small glass vial containing the exact blue of your childhood bedroom.

“How much?” you ask.

“You already paid,” he says. “You paid tomorrow.”

The bells ring again. Eleven times, then ten, then nine. The crowd quickens.

At the palace steps, you see a colossal clock built into the façade. Its hands are not hands at all, but two human figures slowly climbing around the dial. The longer one is the woman from the cart. She looks down at you from above the numeral six and shakes her head, warning you away.

Yet the key at your throat grows hotter. A narrow door beneath the clock has a keyhole shaped precisely like it.

You push against the crowd and reach the door. Behind you, trumpets announce the arrival of the king. Ahead, the key turns by itself.

The door opens onto your own kitchen.

For a moment relief overwhelms you. The table, the chairs, the chipped cup beside the sink—everything is exactly where it should be. Then you notice sunlight flickering too rapidly across the walls. Seasons flash in the window. Fruit blackens in its bowl, shrivels, blossoms again. The hands of the wall clock spin so fast they blur.

An old person sits at the table with their back to you. They are wearing your clothes.

“Close the door,” they say in your voice. “You’re letting the past in.”

You obey. The noise of the crowd becomes a low murmur behind the wood.

The old you turns around. Around their neck hangs another iron key, identical to yours except that it is cold and silver with frost. They explain that the coronation is a trap. The city has no king because the throne belongs to Time, and Time refuses to sit still. Every hundred years the people seize a stranger from the future, crown them, and force them to carry all the years the city has lost.

“Today the stranger is you,” the old you says.

“Then who are you?”

“What remains after the ceremony.”

Outside, the bells have counted down to three. Heavy fists strike the door. The old you takes off the frozen key and lays it beside your burning one. The two keys begin sliding toward each other across the table, trailing frost and smoke.

“There is another way,” they say. “But you will forget everything that comes after this moment.”

The door shudders. Two.

You think of the life you came from, but when you try to picture it you can see only objects without their names: a bright rectangle held in your hand, metal animals rushing along black rivers, a room lit by captive lightning. The past has already begun eating the future.

One.

You press the two keys together.

They melt into a small, living bird.

It is made of flame on one side and ice on the other. It flutters once around the kitchen, scattering burning snow, then flies through the clock above the sink. The glass face ripples like water. All at once the bells stop.

Silence pours through the city.

When you open the palace door, everyone is frozen in place. The trumpeters stand with cheeks puffed. A dog hangs in mid-leap above a puddle. The backward birds are nailed across the sky like black stars. Only the child who was calling your name can move.

She steps out from between the guards. She is carrying no clock. In her cupped hands sits the little bird, dim now, its wings beating more slowly with every breath.

“Where do I take it?” she asks.

Somehow you know. Together you climb the palace stairs, pass the empty throne, and ascend a spiral staircase inside the great clock. With every turn, you grow younger. Your borrowed shoes become too large. The child beside you grows older. By the time you reach the top, she is the woman from the cart, and you are small enough for her to carry.

There is a nest behind the clock face, woven from hair, wheat, and the missing hands of ten thousand clocks. She places the bird inside it. Immediately it lays a single transparent egg. Within the egg, tiny cities rise and fall. Forests become coal, coal becomes light, light becomes a pale dawn falling across a familiar bed.

The woman asks which era you want.

You try to say your own, but you no longer know which one that is.

Below, the frozen city waits. Beyond the glass, all of history lies in layers: pyramids beneath train stations, sailing ships crossing the tops of skyscrapers, dinosaurs sleeping under cathedrals. You understand that every age has been dreaming it is the present.

So you point to the pale dawn.

The woman cracks the egg against the rim of the world.

You fall through a rush of bells, feathers, and white stones. Just before you wake, you feel someone close your fingers around a small object.

Morning is quiet. Your room looks ordinary, though for several seconds you cannot remember the names of anything in it. In your clenched hand is a warm iron key.

Outside the window, a single bird flies backward across the sky.
