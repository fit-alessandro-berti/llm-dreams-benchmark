You are participating in a competition where the grand prize is your dream job. As the competition progresses, you face various challenges and the challenges keep changing shape before you can finish them.

The first one is simple enough. A woman with a clipboard hands you a stapler and says, "Staple the morning." You laugh, but nobody else laughs, and the other contestants are already stapling. They have sheets of pale light in their hands, thin as onion skin, and they are fastening the corners together with small decisive clicks. You look down and there is light in your hands too, warm and slightly damp, and you understand that it will get away from you if you are slow. You staple. The stapler makes the sound of a door closing somewhere far off in the building. When you are done, the woman with the clipboard writes something, and you cannot tell if it is a number or a word or just the shape her pen makes when she is disappointed.

The building is the kind of building you have been inside a hundred times without ever having been there. Long carpet. A fountain in the lobby that runs upward. Elevators with buttons for floors that have names instead of numbers: Patience, Overhead, The Year You Turned Nine. You do not press any of them. You take the stairs because someone told you once that taking the stairs shows initiative, and you feel the other contestants watching the backs of your knees.

The second challenge is an interview, except you are the one asking the questions and the job is sitting across from you. It is not a person. It is more of a weather. It fills the chair and leans back and the whole room smells faintly of pencil shavings and the ocean. You ask it what a typical day looks like, and it answers in your own voice, describing a day you have already lived, down to the sandwich, down to the moment you stood at a window and thought about calling someone and did not. "Is that the job?" you ask. The weather shrugs, which makes the blinds move. "It is what you'd be doing," it says. "Whether that's the job depends on you." The woman with the clipboard is in the corner, writing. You have not seen her come in.

Between challenges there is a waiting area with a vending machine that sells résumés. Not yours. Other people's. You put in a coin and get a folded life: a person who was a lighthouse keeper and then went into logistics. The paper is warm. Someone sitting beside you, a man with your father's hands, tells you the trick is to buy several and staple them together, and then for a while you are that combined person, a lighthouse keeper in logistics who also once taught swimming, and you feel fuller and less afraid. But then a bell goes off, and the résumés turn to moths in your lap and lift away toward the fluorescent lights, and you are just you again, holding a stapler you do not remember picking back up.

Some contestants are eliminated. You do not see how. They are simply not in the next room, and no one mentions them, and there is a faint dust in the air where they stood, like the dust that hangs in a room after you move a bookshelf that has not been moved in years. You want to ask what happened to them and you know that asking is itself one of the challenges, and that there is a right way to ask, and you do not know it.

The third challenge is held on the roof. The city below is spelled slightly wrong. Familiar streets have swapped places, the river runs down a street you used to walk to school on, and your old school is out in the water with its lights on, holding class. The woman with the clipboard says, "Describe your greatest weakness," and everyone else begins to fold paper airplanes out of the pale stapled morning and throw them off the edge. Theirs go far. You fold yours badly. You know it's badly folded even as you fold it, the way you always know, and you think: that's it, that's the weakness, it's this. You throw it anyway. It does not fly so much as decide to stay in the air, hanging in front of you at eye level, turning slowly, and on its wing, in handwriting you recognize as your own from a younger age, it says: you keep waiting to be told you're allowed.

The woman with the clipboard does not write anything down this time. She looks at the airplane, and then at you, and for a moment her face is your mother's, and then your first boss's, and then no one's, a face like the underside of a leaf.

You lose track of the number of rounds. There is a round where you have to carry water in your hands from one end of a corridor to the other, and the corridor grows as you walk it. There is a round where you are asked to sing your qualifications, and you find you know the tune. There is a round where everyone is given a key and told to find the door it opens, and you wander for what might be a day, trying the key in filing cabinets and mouths and the trunks of trees that have grown up through the parking garage, and at last you try it in your own chest, almost as a joke, and it turns, and inside there is a small office with a window and a desk and a chair that is already warm.

You sit down in it. On the desk there is a nameplate with your name, though the letters keep gently rearranging themselves, trying out other spellings, other people you could have been. Through the window you can see the competition still going on, the other contestants stapling and folding and carrying water, the woman with the clipboard moving among them like a heron. From here it looks very far away and also like something you could reach out and touch. You realize you are not sure whether you have won or simply stepped out of the room.

There is a phone on the desk. It rings. You know before you pick it up that it will be the weather, the job, calling in your voice, and that it will ask what you are doing, and that the honest answer is: sitting here, finally, with my hands empty and the morning unstapled and coming apart into ordinary light. And that this may or may not be the job. And that you have stopped needing to be told.

You pick up. The line is open. Somewhere down it, very faintly, you can hear a stapler closing, once, like a door far off in the building, and then nothing, and then the sound of your own breathing, which you had not noticed you were holding.
