You are about to take the final exam for a course that determines your future career. As you sit down to start the exam, you notice the questions are written in your own handwriting — neat, a little older, ink that hasn’t quite dried. You do not remember writing them.

Question 1 is multiple choice, but every option is a life.

A) the job from the brochure  
B) the job someone else could pronounce at dinner  
C) the work you do in the hours no one is watching  
D) a blank line labeled *the thing you actually meant*

When you look away to think, the letters trade places. *Cardiologist* becomes *cartographer* becomes *caretaker of a building that only exists on Tuesdays.* When you look back, the blank line has filled itself in with a word you cannot pronounce.

The other desks are occupied by people wearing your face at the wrong ages. A child version of you is already finished, swinging their legs. An older one has written a single word over and over until the page tore. The clock on the wall has no numbers. Its hands are pencils, and they shorten as they move.

You pick up your pencil. It is heavier than a pencil. The paper is warm, like a cheek.

On the last page there is only one question, printed so large it uses the whole sheet:

*What were you afraid the test would find out?*

In the margin, in smaller type: *If you have noticed that the clock has no numbers, you may wake up.*

You know the answer. You have always known it. You open your mouth to write it and taste metal, and the bell rings inside your teeth, and the paper is a pillowcase, and you are late for something that has not started yet.
