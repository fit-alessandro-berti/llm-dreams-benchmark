You grip the tiller with both hands, knuckles white against the damp wood. The rain cuts through the flash of lightning, and the hull groans like a tired animal. You take a breath, slow and steady, and call out over the howling wind:

"Come on, my friends. Breathe with me. I've got our heading, and I've got you."

You don't shout over the noise—you steady it. You don't promise calm; you promise direction. You adjust the sails, doubling the sheet, letting the wind press against the sails at just the right angle. The waves crest and break in slow motion, each one a drumbeat counting you down to the trough. You read the sea like a map: *here, the current pulls left; there, the tide pushes shallow. We ride it, we don't fight it.*

The crew's eyes flicker from your face to the gray horizon, and you meet each one. A hand shake, a nod, a quiet murmur of "we're coming through." You remind them of the anchor line you've checked, the watertight doors you've secured, the plan you've drawn in the salt air. *We hold the center line. We don't chase the storm. We climb it.*

Then, just as the cloud splits, the wind shifts. The sea flattens. The sky clears. The ship rides into the following breeze, steady and sure. You lower your voice to a whisper:

"It's over. Nothing was lost. We made it."

A cheer breaks from the deck, but you don't celebrate yet. You survey the crew, the hull, the horizon. You give the order: *all hands on deck, check the rigging, dry the deck, take the night watch.* You've brought them through. Now you lead them into the calm.
