...and every group you approach turns out to be a different kind of conversation, each one feeling like a door to a room you don’t quite have the key for.

The first group is laughing in perfect unison, but their mouths are just open windows. They wave you over and ask if you’ve seen the moon—have you seen where it’s hiding? You say yes, it’s probably in your pocket, and they nod as if this is exactly the answer they expected, then dissolve into confetti that tastes like old phone numbers.

You move on. The walls of the gathering begin to breathe. You find a cluster of people all sitting at a table made of mirrors, drinking tea that is just the steam from your own exhalations. They don’t need to introduce themselves; they already know your middle name, but they insist on spelling it differently every time. One of them offers you a seat, but the chair is the exact shape of a question you asked your mother decades ago, so you decline and keep walking.

A hallway opens where there was no hallway before, and you step into a garden party happening inside a closet. The people here wear formal coats made of static electricity and speak only in colors. You try to ask if they’ve seen your friend, but they show you a map drawn on a napkin that keeps changing its borders—your friend is always just one room away, but the room is always behind you.

Then you encounter the loudest group: they are all versions of you at different ages, arguing about whether to stay or leave. The youngest one grabs your wrist and tells you not to waste the dream looking for anyone else, because the one person you know wasn’t invited—they just forgot to turn up, or they were never here at all. You feel a strange relief, like a dropped coin.

You turn to leave, but the gathering wraps around you like a warm, loud blanket. The rooms merge. The mirrors, the static coats, the laughing windows—all of it becomes one continuous sound, a murmur that sounds almost like your own name.

And then you find them—not your friend, but the feeling of them, sitting with you in the center of it all, and you realize the gathering was never about finding anyone. It was just the architecture of loneliness trying to be kind.

You wake up with the taste of that tea still in your mouth, unsure if it was steam or something else, and for a moment, the room around you feels like it might also be a group of people deciding whether to stay or leave. You sit up, reach for your phone, and for one split second, you hear them laughing in perfect unison from the other side of the ceiling.

Then it’s just morning, and the silence is very ordinary.
