You reach into your pocket for your phone to check the address, only to pull out a heavy brass pocket watch with no hands. When you look up again, the street signs have changed from letters into small, intricate illustrations of moths and staircases. 

Panic begins to hum in your chest. You stop a passerby—a tall woman in a trench coat walking a bicycle with square wheels—and ask her where the Henderson Tower is. She nods sympathetically, reaches into her coat, and hands you a warm, slightly damp envelope. "You're already late," she whispers, before riding smoothly away up the side of a brick wall.

You tear open the envelope, expecting an address, and find only a single subway token and a folded map of an ocean. Somehow, you know you need to go underground. 

Spotting a rusted stairwell descending into the sidewalk, you hurry down. The steps seem to stretch endlessly, spiraling past flooded rooms where fish swim lazily around floating office desks, and narrow hallways lined with velvet curtains. Every time you check a clock on the wall, it reads 2:55, though you feel hours slipping through your fingers.

At the bottom, you push through a set of heavy oak doors—and step straight into a blindingly bright conference room. 

A long mahogany table stretches out before you, surrounded by a dozen shadowy figures in tailored suits. At the head of the table sits your third-grade teacher, tapping a wooden ruler against a stack of blank paper. Everyone turns to look at you.

You open your mouth to apologize for being late, to explain about the city and the stairs, only to realize you aren't holding your briefcase. You're holding a bowl of melting ice cream, and you've forgotten how to speak.

Before anyone can say a word, the floor drops away like an elevator, your stomach lurches into your throat, and the conference room dissolves into the sound of your alarm clock ringing.
