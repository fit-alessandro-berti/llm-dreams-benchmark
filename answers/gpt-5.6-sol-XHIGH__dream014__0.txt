You are feeling lost and alone in a bustling city. Suddenly, a group of kind strangers offers to help you find your way, and you follow them because each one seems to recognize a different part of you.

There are five of them, though whenever you try to count them there are six shadows sliding over the pavement. An old woman in a yellow coat carries a cage with no bird in it. A boy has a subway map tattooed across both palms. Two sisters share a red umbrella despite the cloudless sky. The last stranger is a tall man whose face keeps being hidden by passing buses. They do not ask where you are going. They say they already know.

The boy opens his hands, and the bright veins of the map wriggle into new arrangements. The station you need, he explains, has no name, only a small blue circle. You remember the circle with a jolt. It was drawn on the door of your childhood bedroom, though you are certain there was never any mark there.

They lead you through streets that grow narrower and more familiar. Every shop window contains something you once lost: a green mitten, a library card, a key with a plastic star, the exact afternoon light from a summer when you were eight. Behind the glass of a bakery, your mother is kneading clouds into loaves. She looks up and smiles, but flour covers her mouth, and before you can reach the door the old woman gently takes your wrist.

“Not this way,” she says. The empty cage at her side begins to sing.

The song is your own voice humming from far below the city. You descend after it through an entrance concealed behind a newspaper kiosk. The escalator is impossibly long. Advertisements along the walls show photographs of places you have not visited yet: yourself beside a black sea, yourself asleep in a field of silver grass, yourself much older, waving from a balcony while snow falls upward. In every picture, the kind strangers stand somewhere in the background.

At the bottom, a train waits with all its doors open. There are no passengers. Instead, each carriage is filled ankle-deep with warm seawater, and tiny fish flash beneath the seats like loose coins. The sisters close their umbrella and hand it to you. “You’ll need this when we arrive,” they say together.

The train moves before anyone boards. Somehow you are all inside it anyway.

Outside the windows, tunnels give way to rooms. You pass through kitchens at midnight, hospital corridors, forests growing beneath chandeliers. You glimpse people pausing in the middle of their lives as the train rushes silently past. A bride turns from a mirror. A sleeping infant opens its eyes. A lonely man eating soup raises his spoon as if in greeting. For one instant you see yourself back on the crowded street, still standing where the strangers found you. That other you watches the train and mouths a warning you cannot hear.

You turn to ask the others what it means, but their seats are empty. Only the cage remains, swinging from a handrail. Its little door has opened.

The blue circle appears outside the window. It is not a station but a moon, huge and underwater, shining at the end of the tunnel. The train plunges into it without slowing. You raise the red umbrella, and with a sound like a thousand birds taking flight, the roof of the carriage dissolves.

Now you are drifting above the city. The umbrella holds you aloft while streets unroll beneath you in luminous ribbons. From here the city is no longer confusing. It forms the shape of a sleeping animal, curled around a dark central garden. The skyscrapers are its teeth, the traffic its bloodstream, and the river is one long closed eye. The strangers stand at five different intersections below, looking up. Their sixth shadow flies beside you.

It has wings made of maps.

You follow it down into the central garden, where no sound from the city can enter. In the middle grows a single enormous tree covered not with leaves but with doors. Hundreds of them sway from the branches: painted doors, glass doors, doors from schools and houses and hotel rooms. One small white door bears the blue circle.

You understand that this is where the strangers meant to bring you, but not why. The door is too high to reach. Then the empty cage drops gently onto the grass and breaks apart. Its golden bars lengthen into a ladder.

As you climb, the people you passed on the train gather beneath the tree. The bride, the infant, the man with the soup, your mother with flour on her hands—all of them hold the ladder steady. None tells you what waits beyond the door. Their faces are calm, almost expectant, as if you are the one who has promised to help them.

At the top, you place the star-shaped key into the lock. It fits.

Behind the door is the bustling street where the dream began, but it is early morning now and entirely empty. In the pale light you see chalk arrows covering the pavement. They point in every direction at once. Your other self stands among them, no longer lost, holding the hand of a frightened stranger.

You step through. The tree and the crowd vanish behind you, and the little door closes with the soft click of a bird’s beak. The red umbrella has become a folded map in your pocket. When you open it, there are no streets drawn on it, only names—thousands of names glowing faintly, including yours.

Footsteps approach around the corner. Someone is coming, alone and uncertain. You suddenly know the city by heart, although it is already changing. Buildings lean aside to make a new passage. The river opens its eye. Above the rooftops, snow begins to fall upward toward the waiting sky.

You walk toward the footsteps, ready to offer directions, and as you round the corner you hear the empty cage singing inside your chest.
