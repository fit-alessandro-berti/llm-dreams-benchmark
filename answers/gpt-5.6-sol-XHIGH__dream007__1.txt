“There,” they say. “You felt that. You can’t keep pretending it didn’t happen.”

You know they are not talking about the door. You are arguing about a promise the two of you made as children, though neither of you can agree what the promise was. You remember standing beneath a red railway bridge in summer rain, swearing that one day you would leave the town together. Your friend remembers the roof of a school in falling snow, the two of you promising never to leave at all. Each memory is so clear that it seems impossible for the other to exist.

You decide to settle it by going back.

The moment you say this, the door opens onto a railway platform. No one seems surprised. The station clock has two minute hands, racing in opposite directions, and every departure on the board is listed simply as THEN. Your friend walks ahead without waiting, still angry, their coat snapping behind them in a wind you cannot feel.

A train arrives soundlessly. Its windows are full of rain on some carriages and snow on others. You choose a rain-streaked compartment. Your friend chooses one white with frost, yet when you sit down you find yourselves facing each other across the same small table.

Between you lies a red key.

Neither of you admits to putting it there.

The train moves through the old town, although the town appears to have been taken apart and rebuilt from memory. Your childhood houses stand with their rooms exposed, stacked one above another like open dollhouses. In one kitchen, two small figures sit under a table sharing an orange. In an upstairs bedroom, those same figures are much older and are refusing to speak. At every crossing, crowds wait with closed umbrellas while snow falls upward around them.

“That’s proof,” your friend says, pointing to the snow.

“Then what’s the rain?” you demand.

The argument begins again, but now every accusation you make appears as a dark moth and beats against the ceiling. Your friend’s words fall onto the table as little glass teeth. Soon the compartment is crowded with wings and glittering enamel, and you both have to stop speaking in order to breathe.

The train lets you off beneath the red bridge.

It is also the roof of the school.

Steel girders arch below your feet while chimneys and television aerials rise around you. Summer rain falls on one side of your body and snow gathers on the other. At the center stands a narrow red door with no wall around it. The key in your hand is warm now, almost painfully so.

Your friend tells you not to open it. You say opening it was their idea. They say coming here was your idea. For one strange second you can see both of you as children between the two adults you have become. The children look exhausted. One holds a dripping red umbrella; the other wears a wool hat crusted with snow. They are holding hands so tightly their knuckles have gone white.

You put the key in the door.

On the other side is the day of the promise.

It is raining and snowing. You are beneath the bridge and on the roof. The town stretches below you and also surrounds you. Your younger selves are speaking, but their voices arrive backward, drawn syllable by syllable out of the air. You listen until the sounds reverse themselves.

“Whatever happens,” the children say together, “don’t let being right become more important than finding each other.”

Your friend looks at you. Their anger is still there, but it has become small enough to hold. You realize yours has too. It sits in your chest like a hot coin.

“That isn’t what I remember,” you whisper.

“Me neither.”

Behind you, the door has begun to shrink. The bridge is sinking into the roof, the roof folding into the station, the station closing like a book. You reach for your friend at the same moment they reach for you. For an instant you miss, each hand passing through the other as though through cold smoke. Then the two children step forward and guide your hands together.

The town vanishes.

You are back in the room where the argument began. The frightened door has gone still. On the floor lie a wet umbrella and a melting wool hat. Neither of you mentions them.

Your friend opens their hand. The red key has left a deep mark in the palm, shaped not like a key at all but like a tiny railway line leading beyond the edge of the hand. You have the same mark. When you press your palms together, the two broken tracks join.

Outside, something enormous passes in the night with the soft, rhythmic sound of a train. The windows tremble. You stand there until it has gone, still holding on, neither of you asking where it was headed or which of you had remembered correctly.
