You are the captain of a ship in the middle of a turbulent sea. Your crew looks to you for guidance as the storm worsens, and you open your mouth to give the order — but what comes out is not a word. It is a note, low and round, like a foghorn, and the crew nods as if this is the clearest command they have ever received. They begin to sing it back to you, and the rigging hums along.

The waves rise higher, and you notice they are not made of water anymore. They are made of pages — thousands of them, handwritten, curling at the crests. You lean over the rail and recognize your own handwriting on every one, though you cannot remember writing any of it. A wave breaks across the deck and the pages plaster themselves to the boards, to the masts, to the faces of the crew, who keep working without complaint, reading themselves as they haul the lines.

The first mate approaches. She has your mother's eyes and the coat of a schoolteacher you had when you were nine. "The compass is asking for you," she says, and you understand that this is serious, because the compass has never asked for anyone before.

Below deck, the compass sits in its brass housing, but the needle is gone. In its place, a small dark fish swims in slow circles, pointing its nose wherever it pleases. "North is a fiction," the fish says, not unkindly. "You've known this since you boarded. The question is where you actually want to go."

You want to answer, but the ship tilts, and you are suddenly aware that you have been holding the wheel this whole time — that you never left the deck, that the conversation with the fish happened somewhere between one spoke of the wheel and the next. The storm is directly overhead now, and when the lightning comes, it does not flash. It stays. The sky holds still, lit white, like a photograph of a storm rather than the storm itself.

In that long frozen light, you see the whole sea at once. Other ships, hundreds of them, each with a captain at the wheel, each captain turning to look at you at the same moment you turn to look at them. You raise your hand. They raise theirs. You cannot tell if you are one captain reflected many times, or many captains dreaming they are one.

The lightning releases. Sound comes back all at once — the crew singing your foghorn note, the pages rustling in the waves, the fish humming in its housing below.

"Steady," you say, and this time it is a word.

The ship rides down the long slope of a wave, and at the bottom of the trough there is no more sea. There is a field, wheat-colored, moving the way water moves. The ship glides into it without slowing, parting the stalks like foam. The crew leans over the rails and trails their fingers through the grain. The storm continues behind you, politely, at a distance, like something that has finished its part.

The first mate joins you at the wheel. "Where are we?" she asks.

"Almost awake," you tell her, though you don't know how you know. The masts are becoming bedposts. The wheel in your hands is becoming softer, rounder, a fold of blanket. The crew's song is a radiator ticking somewhere in a room you are beginning to remember.

You hold the wheel a moment longer anyway. The field goes on and on, golden, and the ship sails through it, and you let it.
