In the strange, unquestioning logic of the dream, this feels far more urgent than finding food or shelter. The sky above is a bruised shade of violet, swirling with silent ash, and the skeletal remains of skyscrapers lean inward like exhausted giants. Out in the haze, red eyes glint from the shadows of overturned cars, and the ground occasionally tremors with the footsteps of things better left unseen. 

You don't have proper tools—only a rusted tire iron and a pocketful of smooth river stones—yet your hands move with practiced certainty inside the belly of the instrument. Plucking a frayed copper string sends a hum through your fingertips that tastes faintly of rainwater. 

When you finally sit on the torn velvet bench and press the yellowing keys, no ordinary sound comes out. Instead, the first chord ripples outward as a visible wave of warm, golden light. Where the melody touches the cracked asphalt, bright green moss and luminescent wildflowers erupt in fast-forward, swallowing the rust and decay. 

The dangers lurking in the ruins begin to emerge. Stalking silhouettes made of smoke and scrap metal creep onto the highway, their jagged teeth bared—until the music washes over them. As you play a slow, aching lullaby, their glowing red eyes soften to amber. A creature the size of a bear, bristling with rebar and shattered glass, curls up beside the piano pedals and lets out a sigh that sounds like wind chimes.

You play faster, emboldened. With every crescendo, the violet ash in the sky clears, revealing two enormous, fractured moons hanging impossibly close. The highway beneath you detaches from the earth entirely, lifting gently into the warm night air like a ribbon, carrying you, the piano, and the sleeping monsters safely above the ruined world—higher and higher, until the notes dissolve into the soft, rhythmic sound of your own breathing, and you wake up.
