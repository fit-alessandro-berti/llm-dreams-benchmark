You are given a mysterious locked box with no key in sight. As you examine it closely, you notice intricate patterns and grooves worn into the wood, spirals that seem to have been carved not by a hand but by something slow and patient, like water. When you tilt the box toward the light the spirals shift, and you realize they are not carved at all. They are moving. Very slightly, the way a sleeping animal breathes.

You do not remember who gave you the box. There was a hand, you think, and a voice that said "you'll know when," and then the hand and the voice were gone and you were standing in a hallway that smelled of rain and pencil shavings. The hallway is your grandmother's hallway, except it is much longer than it ever was, and the wallpaper repeats a pattern of small blue boats that you are fairly sure you have never seen before, though they feel older than you.

The box is warm. You put your ear to it and hear a ticking, but it is not a clock. It is irregular, hesitant, like someone tapping out a word they keep forgetting how to spell.

There is no keyhole. You turn it over and over. On the bottom, pressed into the wood, is a shallow impression exactly the shape of your own thumb. You know it is yours because of the small scar across the pad, the one from the broken glass when you were nine. You press your thumb into it and nothing happens, and you feel embarrassed, as though the box has caught you trying to cheat.

You walk down the hallway with the box under your arm. The doors along the wall are the doors of every house you have lived in, and you know without opening them which rooms they lead to, and that the rooms are all empty except for one chair each, facing the window. You do not open any of them. The box has begun to tick a little faster and you understand, in the way you understand things in dreams, that it is listening to your footsteps and counting.

At the end of the hallway is a kitchen table and at the table sits a woman with her back to you, sorting keys into piles. Hundreds of keys, thousands, brass and iron and one made of ice that is slowly becoming a puddle. She does not turn around.

"None of those are for this," you say, and you are surprised at how certain you sound.

"No," she agrees. "Those are for the things that were meant to be opened."

You sit across from her. Her face is your face, but tired in a different place than you are tired. She reaches out and touches the box lightly with two fingers, and the spirals on the lid gather toward her touch like fish rising to a surface.

"You keep trying to get in," she says. "Have you thought about what it's trying to get out?"

You had not. You set the box on the table between you and it is suddenly very quiet. The ticking has stopped. In the silence you notice the box is breathing more than before, that the lid rises and falls, and that the grooves are not spirals at all but a single line, one line folded and folded on itself so many times it looked like a pattern. You follow the line with your finger. It leads from the corner of the lid, down the side, around the base, up the back, and ends at the thumbprint.

You press your thumb there again, but this time you do not push. You just rest it. You wait.

The box opens from the inside.

Not the lid. The whole thing unfolds, the way a paper flower unfolds in water, the sides lying down flat on the table, then the flat sides unfolding again, and again, until the table is covered in a thin wooden map of a place you almost recognize. There are the blue boats. There is the hallway. There is the small scar shaped like a river. In the center, where the box used to be, is a hollow no bigger than a walnut, and in the hollow is nothing, and the nothing is warm, and it is the warmest thing you have ever touched.

"It was never locked," the woman says, and she is gathering her keys into her apron, and the ice key has finished melting and left a wet shape on the wood like a small door. "It was holding still. Those aren't the same."

You want to ask her what was inside. You want to ask her what the ticking was, and whether it will start again, and why the hollow feels like something you set down a long time ago and forgot to pick back up. But she is already walking down the long hallway, past all the doors, and the wallpaper boats have turned their sails to follow her.

You put your hand in the hollow. It fits exactly.

Outside, somewhere, it begins to rain, and the rain sounds like tapping, patient and irregular, like someone finally remembering how the word is spelled.

You wake with your thumb pressed hard against the scar on your other hand, and for a moment, before the room assembles itself around you, you are certain you can hear the whole house breathing.
