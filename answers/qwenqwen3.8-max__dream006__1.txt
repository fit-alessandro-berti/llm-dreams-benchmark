speak—but instead of words, a soft golden light spills from your mouth, drifting over the audience like fireflies.

The crowd grows very still. Each tiny light lands gently on someone’s forehead, and when it does, they suddenly remember something they had forgotten: a childhood song, a lost friend’s laugh, the feeling of rain on a summer afternoon.

You realize you are not giving a speech at all. You are giving them pieces of a dream they all once shared.

Behind you, the stage begins to change. The curtains turn into tall curtains of willow leaves. The floor becomes warm sand. The microphone in your hand turns into a small white bird, and it looks at you as if waiting for permission to fly.

You open your hand, and the bird rises into the air.

As it flies, the audience begins to whisper, but not to you—to each other. They are smiling, crying a little, pointing at the lights still floating around the room. The ceiling disappears, revealing a sky full of slow-moving stars.

Then you hear your own voice, calm and clear, though your lips are not moving.

“You already know what I came to say.”

The crowd leans forward together, like a field bending in the wind.

And in the dream, you understand: the speech was never about your voice. It was about everything they had been waiting to hear from themselves.

The lights brighten. The stars hum. The white bird circles once above your head.

Then the audience begins to applaud—not with their hands, but with their hearts, and the sound is like distant thunder rolling gently across the night.

You bow, and as you do, the stage dissolves into morning.
