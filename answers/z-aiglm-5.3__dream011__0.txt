…written in my own handwriting, in an alphabet I never learned. This does not strike me as strange. What strikes me as strange is that the clock above the whiteboard has only a minute hand, and it's moving backward, and nobody else seems to mind.

*Question one: Explain the migration patterns of the birds you loved as a child. Show your work.*

I raise my hand to tell the proctor there's been a mistake — I never took this course — and the proctor is my third-grade teacher, except she has my dentist's head, and she says "You're late" in a voice like a seatbelt warning. Everyone turns to look at me. Every face that turns is my own, at different ages.

*Question two* is a door. Not a question about a door — an actual door, printed on the page, cardboard-colored, slightly ajar. The other students are lining up to walk into theirs, folding themselves flat like laundry and sliding through. Out the classroom window I can see them emerging onto a gray beach, older, holding coffee, waving back at us.

My pencil is the fat beginner kind, chewed at the end, gone from my life for twenty years. And I understand — the way you understand things in dreams, all at once, without being told — that I can't leave until I finish, and I can't finish until I remember the answer, and the answer is the name of a summer I forgot on purpose.

At the bottom of the page, a final question is rising up through the paper like something surfacing in water, one letter at a time, and I know what it says before I can read it:

*What were you so afraid of?*

Somewhere, my grandmother's phone begins to ring — that particular ring, from that particular kitchen — and I know that answering it will wake me, and that waking means leaving the question unanswered forever.

My hand is already reaching.
