...speak, but instead of words, what leaves my lips is a warm, golden vapor that spirals upward and hangs in the air like suspended constellations. The moment they catch the light, the audience inhales in unison, and their expectant faces soften into quiet recognition.

I glance down at the podium, but my prepared notes have dissolved into watercolor stains. Each blot blooms into a scene: a rainy street, a child’s laughter, a train pulling out of a station I once promised to leave. The ink bleeds downward, dripping through the wood and pooling on the floor until the stage itself becomes a shallow lake. Ripples spread outward, and where they touch the front rows, the chairs dissolve into stepping stones, allowing people to wade closer without losing their footing.

The microphone in my hand grows familiar, then strange, then utterly weightless. It’s no longer metal and wire; it’s a polished river stone threaded with copper vines. I realize, without surprise, that it doesn’t amplify my voice. It conducts the space around me. When I press it gently to my chest, the theater’s acoustics fold inward, and I hear the echo of conversations I’ve never had: my younger self asking if this matters, an old teacher murmuring that patience is its own reward, a stranger’s whisper saying, *you’re already here*.

I stop trying to recall my outline. Instead, I lean into the stone and exhale. Out comes not a speech, but a current of cool, mint-scented wind that fans across the room. Every time it passes, the walls breathe. Paintings peel free from the backstage drapes and take flight as origami birds. The ceiling lowers, then stretches, resolving into a canopy of intertwined branches that hold glowing fireflies. The distinction between performer and witness thins; everyone’s shoulders drop, their postures mirroring mine, and for a moment, we’re not an audience and a speaker. We’re just a forest remembering how to sway.

I don’t know when I stop moving. One step forward, then stillness. The stone in my hand crumbles into rich earth, which seeps between my toes. Tiny green shoots push up through the floorboards, unfurling into broad leaves that drip with dew. Above me, the original lights dim, replaced by dappled sunlight filtering through leaves I didn’t notice rising. 

The dream doesn’t end with applause. It ends with silence that feels full, with roots settling, with the gentle understanding that some things aren’t meant to be delivered—they’re meant to be allowed to grow. And as the greenhouse air fills my lungs, I realize I’m no longer standing on a stage at all.
