You are participating in a competition where the grand prize is your dream job. As the competition progresses, you face various challenges and the rules refuse to stay written down.

The arena is an office that is also a stadium. Clocks grow out of the walls and tick in different years. Judges sit in glass boxes drifting overhead; their faces keep swapping — a hiring manager, a teacher who once wrote *potential* in a margin, a version of you who already left.

They hand you a blank résumé. It writes itself as you walk. Every line you padded dissolves. The quiet things you actually did — staying with a scared intern, fixing a process no one noticed — rise in gold ink. A judge with a familiar voice asks why you want the job. You open your mouth and moths come out, each one a different answer: money, meaning, a window with honest light, not having to explain yourself at parties.

The next challenge is a hallway of doors labeled with titles that once felt like destiny. Behind one: an office that never darkens. Behind another: applause that turns into static. Behind a third: a desk by a window, ordinary and exact, with a nameplate that has not decided on a title yet. Competitors flicker through the corridor. One is you from last year, still apologizing. Another is already tired of winning. They keep finishing your sentences until you stop competing with them and start handing them water.

Then the scoreboard goes dark. The final trial has no audience. A judge who looks like you after a long sleep asks the only question that remains:

*What would you still do if no one was keeping score?*

You answer without a pitch. You sit. You begin the work — the unglamorous Tuesday part, the problem that actually yields. The room forgets to be a stadium. The competition banner slumps into an office plant, slightly thirsty.

When they come to tell you that you have won, the prize is warm and oddly light. It is not a title. It is a coat that fits in the shoulders and pinches at the throat, pockets full of ordinary mornings: coffee going cold, weather in the window, a key to a room you recognize because you have already been working in it.

Someone in the wings mouths *wake up*. The dream, generous for once, lets you finish the sentence you were writing first.


You are still holding the key when the light changes. That is how you know the dream is ending — not because the prize was announced, but because the work, for one more moment, was already yours.
