You are about to take the final exam for a course that determines your future career. As you sit down to start the exam, you notice the questions are written in your own handwriting—but from every age you have ever been. A child’s block letters ask, *What do you want to be when you grow up?* A teenager’s scrawl asks, *Who are you when no one is watching?* A tired adult’s cursive asks, *What will you regret not trying?* The last line, still wet, is in a hand you don’t recognize yet: *Will you answer honestly?*

The clock has no numbers. The proctor is a version of you who chose differently, and they smile sadly. Every answer you write becomes a door in the wall. Some open onto offices, some onto hospitals, some onto stages, some onto quiet kitchens at midnight. You realize the exam is not asking what you know. It’s asking what you’re willing to lose.

You put down the pencil and stand. The room exhales. The doors close one by one until only one remains—plain, unmarked. You walk through, and it opens into morning. You wake up before you see what’s on the other side, but you’re already taking the exam.

You always were.
