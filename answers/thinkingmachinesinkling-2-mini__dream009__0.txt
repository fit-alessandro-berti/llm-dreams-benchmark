In the dream, the first thing I notice is the cold against my skin—not the chill of a cellar, but the clean metallic kind of cold that comes after thunder. I’m lying in what looks like a chapel courtyard: moss in the seams, a cracked fountain, a door of black oak that doesn’t open with a click because there are no locks, only weight and patience.

I sit up. My hands ache. There’s no phone to check, no screen glowing, no soundscape of engines or voices, no vibration humming in my pocket. The world is made of dust and shadow. A bell somewhere across the cobbles sounds thin and carried, like something calling rather than warning.

I explore. Past a market stall with heaps of cloth, bread, ochre dyes, and dried herbs. Under the stall, in a narrow space between crates, I find a worn pouch—paper that smells of tobacco, a stub of candle, a brass compass whose needle drags sleepily. Not a compass that knows my world. Just something old enough to belong to a different kind of map.

As I cross a bridge over a fast brown river, the dream tightens around one realization: I don’t know the customs here. I don’t know the names of people, the language, the rules for speaking, eating, asking, or leaving. I don’t have the tools to confirm whether the world is dangerous, hospitable, or simply foreign. I realize I need to learn—quickly, carefully, and without presuming I know what I’m doing—how to survive and find out who I was before this.

Then, just as I start to cross the bridge, the bell stops. The fountain drips. Somewhere in the stone, a small door creaks open. I’m not sure whether to run, ask, or stand still—and that uncertainty feels less like a problem than a doorway into whatever the rest of the dream is trying to tell me.
