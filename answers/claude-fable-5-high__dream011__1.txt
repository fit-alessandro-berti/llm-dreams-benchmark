You are about to take the final exam for a course that determines your future career. As you sit down to start the exam, you notice the questions are written in a language you almost remember — the letters swim like tadpoles across the page, arranging themselves into words just long enough for you to begin reading, then scattering again.

Question one settles for a moment: *Describe the sound your name made before you were given it.*

You look up. The exam hall is enormous, larger than it was when you walked in, the far wall so distant it has weather. Hundreds of other students bend over their papers, writing furiously, and you realize with a cold drop in your stomach that all of them are people you have met exactly once — a woman who gave you directions in a train station years ago, a boy from a summer camp whose name you never learned, the dentist who retired. None of them look up. Their pencils make a sound like rain moving across a lake toward you.

You have no pencil. You check your pockets and find instead a house key you don't recognize, warm as if someone just set it down, and a small folded note. You unfold it. It reads, in your own handwriting: *You studied for this. It's in the other room.*

The proctor drifts down the aisle. She has your third-grade teacher's cardigan and your mother's way of holding her chin, and when she stops beside your desk her shadow falls upward, across the ceiling. "Time is a component of your grade," she says kindly, and taps your paper. Where she taps, the tadpole-letters freeze into a second question: *If the door was always unlocked, why did you carry the key?*

The key in your hand grows heavier. You understand, the way you understand things in dreams — sideways, completely — that the exam is not about the course. There was never a course. The building you're in is every school you've attended, folded together like wet pages, and somewhere in it is the other room from the note.

You stand up. Nobody stops you. This astonishes you so much you almost sit back down.

You walk between the desks, and as you pass, you see the other students' papers: they are all writing the same sentence over and over, and the sentence is your name. The rain-sound of their pencils gets softer the farther you walk, until the hall narrows into the hallway of your childhood house, carpet the color you'd forgotten carpet could be. At the end is a door. It is, of course, unlocked. You use the key anyway, because you carried it, and it seems rude not to.

Inside is a small room with a single desk and a window showing not outside but the exam hall you just left, all those bent heads, seen from very far above. On the desk lies your exam paper, already completed, in handwriting that is yours but steadier. At the bottom, where the grade should be, someone has written: *You may keep the key.*

You feel the specific, enormous relief of a dream ending well — the certainty that you passed something, even if you couldn't say what, even if the passing was only walking out. Behind you the door clicks shut on its own, soft as a period at the end of a sentence, and the light in the little room goes the warm dim gold of morning arriving somewhere beyond your eyelids, and the desk, the window, the paper with its impossible grade all begin politely dissolving, letting you go.

You wake with your hand closed, still holding it.
