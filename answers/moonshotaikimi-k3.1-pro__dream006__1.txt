speak, but every sentence comes out as weather.

“Thank you all for being here,” you say, and a warm rain sweeps through the auditorium. The audience opens umbrellas and nods approvingly.

Encouraged, you continue. A passage about courage produces a powerful wind; a joke brings three seconds of snow. Then you look down and discover that your notes have disappeared. Only one line remains:

**WHEN YOU RUN OUT OF WORDS, TELL THE TRUTH.**

So you grip the podium and admit, “I have no idea what I’m supposed to say.”

The weather stops.

For one terrible moment, there is silence. Then someone in the back laughs. Someone else joins in, and soon the entire crowd is laughing—not at you, but with the relief of people who have been waiting years for permission to admit the same thing.

Their laughter shakes the roof loose. It opens like a flower, revealing an ocean suspended upside down in the sky. Enormous whales drift above the seats, trailing ribbons of blue light.

You step away from the microphone. The audience rises, not to applaud, but to float. One by one, they drift toward the shining water. You follow them, your abandoned notes turning into paper boats beneath your feet.

Just before you reach the surface, you hear applause behind you. You turn, but the auditorium is gone. Only the podium remains, floating in the blue, with your glass of water still waiting on it.

You reach for the glass—and wake.

Your room is dark. Beyond the window, it is raining, and for a few seconds you could swear the rain sounds like applause.
