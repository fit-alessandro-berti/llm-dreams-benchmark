…written in your own handwriting, but in a language you no longer remember speaking.

The first question is only a blank space. The second is a mirror. The third asks you to list the names of every version of yourself who has already passed the exam, and as you stare at it, the paper begins to breathe under the fluorescent light.

You reach for your pen, but the cap comes off in your hand, and instead of ink, a small silver bell falls from the tip and lands on the desk with a sound like a single clear thought. The other students in the room are not writing. They are standing in a long line behind their desks, each one carefully removing their shadow from their chair and folding it into a paper bag.

Your professor, who you now realize is an older version of yourself wearing the uniform of a person who has already chosen a career, leans forward and says, “The exam does not test what you know. It tests what you are willing to become in order to read the next question.”

You open the test booklet, and the pages are not paper but thin sheets of weather. A snowstorm moves across the multiple-choice answers. A thunderstorm is hiding in the essay section. One question glows faintly:

**If the course that determines your future career begins with a dream, what is the final exam actually for?**

You realize, with the calm certainty that only exists in dreams, that you have already answered it. The answer is not on the page. It is the reason you can feel your heartbeat even though your body is asleep.

The clock above the classroom door is not ticking. It is hatching.

As the shell of the clock cracks open, a small bird flies out and lands on your wrist. It opens its beak, and from inside it comes the sound of a new beginning, not a warning.

You look down at the exam, and the questions have changed.

They are all just your name, written over and over, becoming less like a word and more like a door.

And when you finally stop trying to understand the dream, you realize the exam was never testing you.

It was letting you in.
