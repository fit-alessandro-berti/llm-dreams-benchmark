You are walking through a peaceful forest when suddenly the weather changes dramatically. You feel a storm approaching and the light turns a strange green-gold, the color of old bottle glass. The birds stop mid-song, all at once, as if someone has lifted the needle from a record.

The first drops of rain do not fall. They rise. You watch them lift from the ferns and the moss, small silver beads climbing back into the sky, and you understand—the way you understand things in dreams, without asking—that the storm is not coming down to you. You are being drawn up into it.

Thunder rolls, but it is soft, almost apologetic, like furniture being moved in a room above you. You keep walking because your feet insist on it, and the path underfoot changes from dirt to something darker and more yielding, like the cover of a book pressed open too many times. The trees lean in. Their bark is covered in writing now, letters you almost recognize, and when the wind moves through the branches you can hear the words being read aloud in your grandmother's voice, though you cannot make out a single sentence.

The rain that rises grows thicker until the whole forest is climbing skyward around you—leaves, seeds, a startled fox that gives you a look of profound embarrassment as it floats past. You alone stay on the ground, growing heavier, and the heaviness feels like a kind of honor. Someone has to hold the forest down, the storm explains, not in words but in pressure, in the way the air leans against your chest. Someone has to remember where everything goes.

Lightning comes. It does not flash; it stays. A single bright seam opens in the sky and hangs there, and through it you can see another forest, identical to this one, where the rain falls properly downward and another you is walking the same path, looking up at their own seam of light, wondering about you.

You raise your hand. The other you raises theirs. Between the two forests, in the space where the storm lives, all the risen rain hangs suspended—millions of drops, each one holding a tiny curved reflection of both of you.

Then the thunder clears its throat, the seam closes gently like an eye going to sleep, and everything the sky borrowed comes drifting back down: the leaves, the seeds, the fox (still embarrassed), and finally the rain itself, warm now, falling on your upturned face.

The birds resume exactly where they left off, mid-note. The path is dirt again. Somewhere ahead, past the wet and shining trees, you can hear your grandmother's voice finishing the last line of the story, and you walk toward it, unhurried, knowing the words will wait for you. The forest smells of rain and of pages. You are almost home, or home is almost you—in the dream it is the same thing—and you keep walking until the green closes softly around you like a book being shut with your place kept.
