If I continue the dream, the paper grows warm in your hands and the questions shift before your eyes—not like mathematics or law or medicine, but like a mirror held up to the room. Instead of “which of the following is correct,” they ask: *Why are you here? What did you want before this degree mattered? If the result disappeared, would you still choose this work?*

You scan the choices, and they aren’t fixed. The eraser moves the words aside; the pencil writes a different sentence each time you read. “Be honest,” one line says. “The room is listening,” another. Outside the vague window, the hallway becomes the street you’d left behind—half familiar, half remembered.

And at the bottom of the page, where the final question should be, there is no multiple choice. Just: **Do you mean it?** You don’t raise your hand to answer. You stand, walk out past the empty chair, and the exam dissolves into a corridor that opens onto the ordinary moment you’ve been trying to earn: waking up, noticing you are here, and deciding what to say next.
