and each one seems to have been designed specifically for you.

The first challenge is a maze, but the walls aren't made of stone — they're made of all the rejection emails you've ever received, printed out and stacked ten feet high. Every time you doubt yourself, a new wall appears and blocks your path. You only make it through when you stop trying to outrun the doubt and start reading the walls out loud, like they're just paper. Because they are.

The second challenge is a team round. You're put in a room with five other competitors, all brilliant, all terrified, and for some reason you've all swapped voices. You have the deep voice of the engineer, she has your laugh, he has your nervous habit of saying "sorry" before asking a question. You can't win by being the loudest anymore. You have to actually listen, finish each other's sentences, build the strange, floating bridge in the middle of the room piece by piece. When you cross it together, it doesn't collapse behind you.

Then comes the final.

No audience. No judges. Just a quiet office at the top of a building that shouldn't exist, with one chair on your side of the desk, and one chair on the other. Waiting for you in the other chair is you — five years older, wearing the clothes you always imagined wearing at your dream job, looking calm in a way you haven't felt in a long time.

She doesn't ask about your qualifications. She asks, "What would you do if you knew you couldn't embarrass yourself?"

And in the dream, you finally answer honestly. Not the polished interview answer. The real one. The project you'd start on day one. The problem you can't stop thinking about in the shower. The work that makes time disappear for you.

She smiles, slides a golden key across the desk — not heavy, warm, like it's been held for a long time — and says, "Then why are you waiting for permission?"

The office doors burst open, not to a prize ceremony, but to the actual workplace, alive and sunlit and a little chaotic, full of people waving you over to a desk with your name already on it.

You step forward, reach for the handle—

—and wake up.

Your heart is pounding, the dream already fading, but that last sentence is still ringing perfectly clear. On your nightstand, your phone lights up with a calendar reminder you forgot you set: *Applications close today.*

You sit up, smiling, a little out of breath, and for the first time, you open your laptop not because you're afraid of missing out...

...but because you already know exactly what you're going to say.
