…stop talking.

Not to concede. You stop because you've noticed that the sound of your voice is arriving a half-second after your mouth moves, like a film badly dubbed, and you want to see what happens if you let the silence catch up.

It does. The kitchen goes very quiet and very bright. The linoleum is the linoleum from your grandmother's floor, though this is your apartment, and the refrigerator hums a note you recognize from a song you can't name.

On the table between you is the thing you were arguing about: a house key. Brass, worn smooth, warm as a held hand. Neither of you will take it. It belongs to a house that burned down, or was sold, or was never built — you know all three of these are true and it doesn't seem like a contradiction. Your friend says something about fairness. Their mouth makes the shape of your name and the sound that comes out is your mother's.

You look at them properly then. They are seventeen. They have the haircut they had at seventeen and the shirt they wore the summer everything went wrong, and they are still furious, and you love them so much it's like standing too close to a heater.

You pick up the key. It's heavier than a key. You press it against the wall above the sink, expecting resistance, and the wall takes it the way a plum takes a thumb — soft, yielding, a little wet — and there is a click you feel in your teeth.

A door opens where the door has no business being.

Through it you can hear the argument still going. Both voices. Yours and theirs, carrying on without you, but softer now, the way an argument sounds from the next room when it's almost over, when someone has finally said the true thing underneath the loud thing.

You step through to hear how it ends.

Behind you the door is a wall. You run your palm along it, looking for the seam, the warmth, the plum-soft place. Plaster. A calendar. A light switch that doesn't do anything.

The voices fade into the sound of a refrigerator humming.

You stand there with your empty hand, certain that if you'd stayed one more minute you'd have learned what you were actually fighting about — and waking, that certainty stays with you longer than the dream does, longer than the face, longer than the key.
