Your shoes slap against cobblestones that shift color with every step—blue, then gold, then the deep red of old wine. The city rises around you like a maze built by someone who forgot what gravity was supposed to do. Streets fold upward into staircases. Windows blink like eyes. Somewhere above you, a clock tower chimes once, though it does not look like a clock at all; its face is a mirror, and in it you see yourself arriving late to a hundred different rooms.

You clutch your bag, suddenly certain it contains the thing you need for the meeting, though you cannot remember what it is. A map? A contract? A small living bird made of paper? You don’t open it to check. In dreams, looking too closely makes things worse.

A tram glides past without touching the rails. The passengers are all wearing your face, each one slightly older or younger, some smiling, some terrified. One of them presses a hand to the window and mouths, “This way.”

You follow the tram through an alley that smells like rain and oranges. At the end, there is a door standing alone in the middle of the street, tall and black, with a brass handle shaped like a question mark. You know, with absolute dream-certainty, that this is the entrance to your meeting.

But when you reach for it, the door whispers, “Not yet.”

The ground tilts. You slide gently backward, not falling exactly, but being carried, as if the city itself is rearranging you. Buildings lean in to watch. Streetlamps bend like curious flowers. Your hour is almost gone, and still you do not know where you are going—only that you are expected.

Then a woman appears beside you. She has no shadow, and her coat is stitched from maps. “You’re late,” she says, “but so is everything else here.”

She hands you an envelope. It is warm.

“Your meeting,” she says, “is with the part of yourself you keep forgetting.”

You open the envelope.

Inside is a single sentence:

“Turn around.”

You do.

Behind you stands the city, vast and unfamiliar, except now you realize it is not unfamiliar at all. Every street is a memory. Every building is a choice. Every hidden corner is something you never said aloud.

And at the center of it all, waiting patiently beneath the mirror-faced clock, is a version of you holding the door open.

They smile.

“You’re right on time,” they say.

You step through.
