Rain rattles through the canopy. You duck under a fallen beech and find a fox already there, its paws tucked neatly beneath it. Neither of you has room to be afraid of the other. You sit with one shoulder getting wet and watch the creek rise.

Then comes a knock from inside the log.

“Come in,” you say, out of habit.

A little door swings open in the wood. Beyond it is your grandmother’s kitchen, warm and bright, with the chipped blue teapot on the stove. She glances up from peeling an apple. You have so many things to ask her that for a moment you can’t speak.

“Well?” she says. “Are you going to leave that poor fox out in the rain?”

You move aside to let it through. By the time you shut the door, it’s curled up under her chair as if it has lived there all its life. Your grandmother cuts a slice of apple and hands it to you on the flat of the knife, the way she used to. Outside, the storm can take as long as it likes.
