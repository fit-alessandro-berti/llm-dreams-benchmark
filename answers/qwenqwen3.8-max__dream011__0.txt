…not questions at all, but small, luminous objects pressed into the paper: a key, a feather, a cracked compass, a seed.

The invigilator leans over and whispers, “Choose the one you cannot carry.”

You reach for the key, but the moment your fingers touch it, it turns into your mother’s voice asking why you never call. The feather becomes the day you quit something you loved without telling anyone. The compass spins wildly until it points directly at your chest. The seed hums faintly, as if something inside it is waiting to be named.

You look around the exam hall, hoping for help, but the other students have no faces. Their heads are bent over their papers, and from each desk rises a soft murmur of possible futures: doctor, architect, ghost, lighthouse keeper, translator of forgotten languages, keeper of the door between sleep and waking.

You look back at your paper. A new line appears in elegant ink:

**Question One: What have you pretended not to want?**

Your pencil moves without you. It writes the truth before you can stop it. The answer disappears from the page and reappears on your skin as a thin blue line, like a river drawn on your arm.

**Question Two: Who taught you to measure your worth?**

The room stretches. The desks become small boats drifting across a dark, polished sea. The invigilator is now standing at the far end of the hall, but his face has changed. He looks like you—older, calmer, wearing the expression of someone who has already failed and survived.

You understand, suddenly, that this exam is not testing what you learned in the course. It is testing what you have been too afraid to admit.

**Question Three: If you become everything you hoped for, what will you have to leave behind?**

Your throat tightens. You think of the people who love the version of you that is still uncertain. You think of the parts of yourself you have carried only because they were familiar. You think of the dream you have been using as a shield.

You write: *Safety.*

The word sinks into the page like a stone into water.

Then the final question appears. It is not written. It is a mirror, small and round, set into the center of the exam paper. In it, you see your reflection—but not as you are now. This version of you is standing in bright morning light, unburdened, smiling as if they have just remembered something important.

Beneath the mirror, words form slowly:

**If you pass, you must forgive the version of yourself who is failing.**

Your hand trembles. The pencil feels heavier than before. Around you, the faceless students begin to stand, one by one, and walk toward doors that were not there earlier. Some doors are made of wood, some of glass, some of sound, some of silence.

The invigilator—the future you—speaks gently.

“There is no correct answer,” he says. “Only the one you can live with.”

You press the pencil to the paper and write your name.

The moment you finish, the exam sheet folds itself into the shape of a bird. It lifts from your desk, circles once above your head, and flies toward the ceiling, which is no longer a ceiling but an open sky full of pale, waking stars.

The bell rings.

The exam hall dissolves into morning light.

You wake with the pencil still in your hand, and on your palm, in blue ink, a single sentence you do not remember writing:

**Begin again, but this time without asking permission to exist.**
