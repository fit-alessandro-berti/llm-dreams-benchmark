stop speaking.

Not because you have nothing left to say, but because every word has begun turning into a small glass bird. They dart between you, striking the ceiling, falling at your feet. Your friend is bleeding from a tiny cut on their cheek. When you touch your own face, your fingers come away red too.

“We can’t keep doing this,” you say, and the last bird settles on the table.

The room grows quiet. Outside the window, the sea has risen to the second floor.

You remember what the argument is about: your friend wants to sell the house where you grew up together. You want them to stay. Or perhaps they want to leave the city. The details keep changing, but your hand is still gripping the key.

Your friend looks exhausted.

“What happens if I go?” they ask.

The answer you have been defending all evening—*you shouldn’t*—suddenly seems useless.

“I don’t know,” you say.

They pull out a chair. You sit opposite them, careful of the glass beneath your shoes. For a while, neither of you tries to fix anything.

Then your friend reaches across the table. You think they want the key, and your fist tightens. But they turn your hand over and gently remove a splinter from your palm.

Outside, a boat bumps against the window.

You are still angry. So are they. When they open the window, you almost tell them to close it.

Instead, you ask where they’re going.

They tell you, and this time you listen closely enough to remember.
