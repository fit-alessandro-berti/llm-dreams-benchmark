...in a language you don't recognize — at first.

You stare at the first page, heart hammering, while everyone around you is already scribbling furiously like this is perfectly normal. The fluorescent lights buzz too loudly. The clock above the whiteboard is ticking, but its hands are moving backwards.

You lean closer. The strange symbols on your exam begin to shift when you don't look directly at them. Letters rearrange themselves out of the corner of your eye.

Then, suddenly, you can read it.

**Question 1: What were you doing at 7:14 p.m. on the last Tuesday of October, when you were nine years old?**

You blink. That's not on the syllabus.

You look at Question 2.

**Question 2: Describe, in detail, the conversation you were too afraid to have last year.**

Your pen is already in your hand, though you don't remember picking it up. Around you, no one else seems surprised. The girl to your left is crying silently while she writes. The guy in front of you is laughing at something only he can see on his page.

The proctor walks slowly down the aisle. When he passes your desk, you realize he looks exactly like you, only older — tired eyes, same scar, wearing a suit you would never buy. He stops and whispers, without moving his lips:

"You've studied for the wrong test."

You turn the page.

**Question 3: If you fail at the thing you think you want, who will you become instead?**

Your hand starts writing before you've decided what to say. It writes things you haven't admitted out loud — not to friends, not to family, not even to yourself at 2 a.m. It writes about not actually wanting the prestigious job everyone expects you to want. It writes about being terrified that wanting something *smaller* and *quieter* means you're wasting your life. It writes about the version of you who just wants to be proud of a Tuesday.

The ink doesn't dry. It pools and swirls, and for a second you can see your reflection in it — not as you are now, stressed and hunched over a desk, but as a child sitting cross-legged on a bedroom floor, completely absorbed in something pointless and wonderful.

The exam room stretches. The walls pull away until the other desks are islands in a fog. The clock melts and reforms into a window, and outside the window it's not the campus parking lot — it's every place you've ever lived, stacked on top of each other.

You flip frantically to the last page. There's only one question left, taking up the whole sheet in huge, clean print:

**Final Question: You know you're dreaming. So why are you still afraid to fail?**

The pen stops.

Total silence. The older you is now sitting in the empty desk beside you, reading your answers over your shoulder. He doesn't look disappointed. He looks relieved.

"Well?" he says.

And for the first time in the dream, you realize you don't have to write anything at all. You can stand up. You can tear the exam in half. You can walk out that door and the career, the future, the whole crushing weight of *this decides everything* will stay right here, in this room that doesn't exist.

So you do.

You stand, and the floor feels like warm grass under your bare feet. You walk past rows of students still hunched over their impossible questions, and you push open the classroom door —

— and you wake up to your alarm, heart still racing, morning light coming through your blinds.

The real exam is in three hours. Your notes are on the desk. Your coffee is cold.

But the panic is strangely gone, replaced by that last question still echoing in your head, clearer than any formula you memorized:

*If I'm not afraid to fail, what would I actually choose?*
