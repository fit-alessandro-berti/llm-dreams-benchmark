The first puzzles are mechanical: rotating brass rings, sequences of colored lights, locks that open when you arrange twelve stones according to the phases of the moon. But soon the riddles begin referencing things they could not possibly know. A door asks for the name of your childhood imaginary friend. A chessboard contains only the pieces you were afraid to lose. One corridor fills with whispers whenever you make a mistake, each whisper repeating a different version of what you should have done.

Every solution causes the walls to shift. You hear them grinding behind you, patiently rearranging the path, and you realize that solving the puzzles does not bring you closer to the exit. It only brings you deeper.

At last, you reach a circular room with no doors. In its center sits a wooden desk, and on the desk lies a single sheet of paper:

**THE FINAL PUZZLE HAS ALREADY BEEN SOLVED.  
WHY ARE YOU STILL HERE?**

You search for hidden numbers, examine the desk, measure the walls. Hours pass—or perhaps only seconds. The more desperately you think, the smaller the room becomes.

Finally, exhausted, you sit on the floor and do nothing.

In the silence, you notice a sound beneath the whispering and the grinding stone: your own breathing, slow and distant, as though coming from another room. You follow it—not with your feet, but with your attention.

The maze shudders.

For the first time, you understand that the walls are not closing in. They are being drawn around you, line by line, each time you insist there must be something more to figure out.

You stand, walk toward the nearest wall, and gently push.

It opens like a curtain.

Beyond it is your bedroom, pale with morning light. You step through just as you wake, relieved to find yourself safely in bed—until you notice a sheet of paper beside your pillow.

In handwriting you almost recognize, it says:

**Good. Now solve the day.**
