You wake up in a completely different era, with no modern technology around. As you explore your new surroundings, you realize you need to find water before anything else, because in dreams thirst arrives before logic does.

The village around you is made of timber and smoke. Chickens the size of dogs step politely out of your way, nodding as if they know you. A woman in a linen cap hands you a wooden bucket without being asked, and you understand — the way you understand things only in dreams — that you have lived here for years, that everyone remembers you even though you remember nothing.

The well is at the center of the square, but when you turn the crank, the rope keeps coming up and coming up, coils of it pooling at your feet like something being unknotted from the earth. There is no bucket at the end. There is, instead, a small brass key, still dripping, though the water it dripped is somehow warm.

"That opens the church," says a blacksmith who was not there a moment ago. His forge glows behind him even though it stands in the open street, doorless, wall-less. "You've been keeping it for us. Don't you remember?"

You don't, but your feet remember. They carry you up a hill you've never seen, past fields where farmers are harvesting something silver — not wheat, but you don't want to look closely enough to name it. The church at the top is small, stone, older than the village, maybe older than the hill. The key turns before you even touch it to the lock.

Inside, there are no pews. There is only a long table, and on the table, laid out carefully like museum pieces, are all the objects from your other life: your phone, dark and dead. Your keys. A charging cable coiled like a sleeping snake. A single earbud. They look ancient here — artifacts, relics, things behind glass in your mind even though there is no glass.

The whole village has followed you in. They gather around the table in silence, hats off, heads bowed, the way people stand at a graveside.

"We knew you'd want them kept safe," the woman with the bucket whispers, "until you decided."

"Decided what?"

"Whether to go back."

You pick up the phone. It has weight it never had before, the weight of a stone tablet. In its black screen you can see your reflection, and behind your reflection the candlelight, and behind the candlelight, faintly, another room — a bedroom, a window with blinds, a bed with someone shaped like you still asleep in it. The screen is not a screen. It is a distance.

You could press it, you realize. One touch and you'd wake.

Outside, the evening bells begin to ring, though you saw no bell tower. The sound comes up through the floor, through your feet, through your teeth. The villagers are watching you with an expression you finally recognize: not curiosity, not worry. Hope. They are hoping you'll stay. Somewhere in the last hour, without noticing, you started hoping it too.

You set the phone face-down on the table, gently, the way you'd close the eyes of something at rest.

The village exhales. The candles lean. And as everyone files out into the blue evening to show you the way to a supper you can already smell — bread, woodsmoke, something roasting — you feel the dream widen and settle around you, roomy as a coat made to your measure.

You are halfway down the hill when you hear it anyway: a faint buzz behind you, muffled, insistent, coming from inside the locked church. The phone, ringing face-down in the dark.

You keep walking. The bells ring louder, covering it, and the woman beside you takes your arm and says, "In the morning you'll have forgotten there was anything to forget."

And in the morning — this morning, the one where you open your eyes to blinds and a ceiling and your phone alarm chiming on the nightstand — you almost have. Except for the smell of woodsmoke on your hands, and a small brass weight in your closed fist that melts like ice as you wake, leaving nothing but the cold shape of a key pressed into your palm.
