...written in a language you somehow know you once spoke fluently, but the words slide around the page whenever you look straight at them. The first question reads:

**"Describe the color of the sound your childhood kitchen made."**

You glance up. The clock on the wall has thirteen numbers, and its hands are moving in opposite directions, unbothered. The proctor, who is also your third-grade teacher and also a tall heron in a cardigan, taps a talon on her watch. "Pencils only," she says. "Preferably ones you've already lost."

You look down and find a pencil in your hand. It's warm, like it's been waiting for you.

**Question 2:** *"Using only what you remember from the lectures you never attended, explain why the bridge was built in the wrong direction."*

Strangely, you know this one. Your hand starts writing before you've decided anything, and the answer comes out in confident, looping cursive: *Because the river asked it to.* The page glows faintly, as if approving.

Around you, other students are calmly folding their exams into paper boats and setting them on the floor, which has become a shallow, glittering stream. Their boats drift toward a door you hadn't noticed, slightly ajar, with morning light coming through.

**Question 3** appears last, in your own handwriting:

*"Now that you've been paying attention, what do you actually want to do?"*

The pencil hovers. The heron stops tapping. The clock hands pause, and everything in the room waits for you.

You realize the exam was never about the answers. It was about whether you'd notice the door.

You fold the paper into a small boat, set it on the water, and watch it drift toward the light. You stand up to follow it, and as you step through, you wake with the faint, certain feeling that you just passed.
