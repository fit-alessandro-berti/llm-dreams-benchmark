A murmur ripples through the crowd. You clear your throat and try again, and this time sound does come out, but it's not your voice. You glance down and notice you're wearing pajamas. Or perhaps a graduation gown. It's impossible to tell, and somehow the distinction doesn't matter. The podium has grown taller—you're standing on tiptoes now, gripping its edge—and the audience has begun to shift, faces blurring and rearranging like watercolors left in the rain. Your third-grade teacher is there. A stranger from a train you rode years ago. Someone whose face you can't quite see no matter how hard you look, though you feel certain they matter.

The lights dim. Somewhere, a phone rings, and everyone reaches for their pockets at once.

Then, with the peculiar certainty that only dreams allow, you realize you know exactly what you were supposed to say all along. The speech was never written down because it lives somewhere behind your ribs. You open your mouth, the whole room leans in—

—and you wake, heart thudding, the last sentence already slipping away like water through fingers.
