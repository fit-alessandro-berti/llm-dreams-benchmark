—the wind screamed through the rigging like something wounded, and the deck slammed beneath my boots a third and fourth time before I even dared to speak.

"Hold the mast!" I shouted over the roar. "Helm, aim us for the—" The water below had already stopped looking like water. It was a churning black throat, and every swell lifted us and dropped us and dragged at the hull like a patient hand testing for weakness.

The crew bent to the order. Ropes sang. Somewhere to starboard a man was trying to tie down a loose hatch, and the sea was already winning.

I climbed to the rail. The storm had eaten the moon, but I could see the crest of the next wave coming like a white wall, and I knew the old shape of it the way a hunter knows wind. Ahead and to the left, a long ridge broke the surface—too small to be a true island, too steady for the water. A reef, then. Or a shoal. Either way, if we didn't change course in the next hundred heartbeats, we'd ride it down and go through like a paper boat.

"Port lead! Port lead!" I called, and the wheel groaned under my hands as she fought the current.

The crew watched me. That was the worst of it—not the cold spray or the shrieking sails, but the fact that every one of them had already decided what I was. Whether I was the kind of captain who could hold the wheel when the wall came down.

I spat salt water, wiped it from my eyes, and said, as calmly as I could make it come out, "We need every hand on the ropes. Not to save the ship. To save each other. Now!"

And then the sea came up.
