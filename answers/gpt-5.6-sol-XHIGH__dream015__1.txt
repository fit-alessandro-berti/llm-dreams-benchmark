You find yourself in a post-apocalyptic world, with resources scarce and dangers everywhere. Despite the hardships, you decide to plant a garden.

The decision comes to you while sheltering in the gutted shell of a bus. Rain ticks on the roof, though when you hold out your hand through a broken window the drops rise from the earth and vanish into the clouds. Across the aisle, a faded advertisement shows a family laughing over a bowl of strawberries. Someone has scratched out their faces, but the berries remain impossibly red.

In your pocket are seven seeds wrapped in a bus ticket. You do not remember finding them. Each is shaped like a tiny sleeping ear.

At dawn you leave the bus and follow the dry canal toward the city center. The towers there have melted sideways and hardened in the act of falling. Wind moves through their empty rooms, opening and closing hundreds of doors at once. Far off, the scavenger bells begin to ring, warning that something large is crossing the salt flats. You keep walking.

You choose a roundabout for your garden because a bronze horse stands in its center. Its rider is gone, but the horse still strains forward, green with age, one hoof raised above a nest of weeds. Around it, the road is clogged with cars full of gray dust. In one car you find a child’s yellow bucket. In another, a bottle with half an inch of water. Beneath the bronze horse, when you scrape aside the ash, the soil is dark.

All day you work. You pry up paving stones with a road sign. You carry soil in the yellow bucket and build seven small beds between the roots of the statue. The city watches through ten thousand black windows. Once, you hear claws clicking beneath the cars, keeping pace with you, but whatever owns them never steps into view.

At sunset an old woman appears on the far side of the roundabout. She wears a veil made of cassette tape, which whispers in many tangled voices as the wind catches it.

“Nothing grows here,” she says.

“Then it won’t be any worse after I try.”

She considers this and gives you a thimble of water. You pour one drop into each hole and swallow none. The old woman nods, as if you have answered a question, then climbs into the driver’s seat of a burned-out car. When you look again, both she and the car are gone.

You plant the seeds.

That night you sleep beneath the horse. You dream you are inside the earth, surrounded by patient machinery. Roots turn like gears. Worms in white uniforms consult maps of rivers that no longer exist. Somewhere in the dark, the seven seeds whisper everything the world has lost: names of birds, recipes, lullabies, the sound of ice cubes in a glass. You try to memorize it all, but the words pass through you and sink deeper.

A siren wakes you.

Figures are approaching through the abandoned cars, wearing masks made from animal skulls. Scavengers. Their lanterns burn blue, and their leader carries a rifle longer than she is tall. Behind them drags a cage on wheels. Inside the cage is the rain, thrashing like a silver animal.

“Move,” the leader tells you. “There’s water under that statue.”

You stand in front of the seven patches of turned soil. It is absurd, defending dirt with empty hands. The scavengers laugh. The bronze horse above you trembles, and flakes of green metal fall like leaves.

Then the ground answers.

Seven green shoots break the surface at once. They rise past your ankles, your knees, your shoulders. Stems braid around the horse’s legs. Leaves unfold with the soft report of umbrellas opening. The scavengers stumble backward as vines flow over the asphalt, slipping through cracked windows and under doors, stitching the dead cars together. The leader fires, but the bullet becomes a beetle in midair and lands shining on your sleeve.

The plants flower.

One bears red trumpets from which warm bread rolls instead of pollen. One grows glass bells containing tiny storms. One opens enormous white blossoms, and in the center of each is the face of someone you almost remember. The tallest becomes a tree whose branches are crowded with telephones. Every telephone begins to ring.

No one moves. At last the riflewoman lifts a receiver.

Her expression changes. She sinks to her knees and says, “Mama?”

All around the roundabout, the others answer phones. Some speak. Some only listen. One begins to laugh so hard that his skull mask falls off, revealing the face of a frightened boy. The cage door swings open, and the rain leaps out.

It circles the garden once, sniffing the leaves, then bounds upward and bursts across the sky.

Real rain falls—not upward now, but down, heavy and ordinary. It drums on the cars and runs black through the gutters. The scavengers remove their masks. People emerge from buildings where you had seen no movement: children pale as mushrooms, mechanics, widows, a man carrying an empty aquarium, the old woman with the whispering veil. They bring cups, helmets, saucepans, their cupped hands. No one fights for the water. There is too much of it.

By morning the roundabout has become a forest.

You walk between trees laden with things that should not grow on trees: peaches, batteries, clean socks, small jars of medicine, blue eggs humming with electricity. The bronze horse is alive beneath its patina. It lowers its head to drink from a puddle, metal lips rippling the reflection. Where its missing rider should be, a sapling has sprouted.

The old woman finds you staring at it.

“You’ll have to decide where to go next,” she says.

Beyond the garden, the city is changing. Green spreads along the canal in both directions. Vines climb the leaning towers and pull them gently upright. On distant rooftops, windmills turn their rusted faces toward the weather. Yet at the horizon, the salt flats are buckling. Something vast moves underneath, approaching the scent of rain.

The people gather what the garden offers. The former scavenger leader hands you her rifle. Roots have filled its barrel, and a single violet blooms from the sight.

You leave it leaning against the statue.

Instead, you take the yellow bucket. In its bottom lie seven new seeds, warm as living teeth.

The bronze horse kneels so you can climb onto its back. As it steps down from the roundabout, every telephone in the forest rings once in farewell. You ride toward the trembling horizon while the rain follows overhead, bounding from cloud to cloud. Behind you, people are singing a song you recognize but cannot name. Ahead, beneath the cracked white earth, the enormous thing turns in its sleep.

You reach into the bucket and close your hand around the seeds. This time, when they whisper, they are not reciting what the world has lost.

They are telling you what it wants to become.
