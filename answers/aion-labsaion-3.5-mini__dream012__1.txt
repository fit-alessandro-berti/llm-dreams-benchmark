"Hard to port!" you shout, but your voice comes out as thunder, and the crew nods as if thunder were a perfectly reasonable order. The first mate—his face keeps shifting between strangers you've met and strangers you haven't—points to the wave ahead. It isn't water. It's a wall of clocks, all of them ticking at different speeds, all of them wrong.

You steer into it anyway, because in dreams you always steer into the thing you fear.

The ship groans. The lanterns swing. A boy who might be you at nine years old appears at the rail, calm as still water, and asks, "Do you know where we're going?"

You want to lie to him. You want to say yes, captain's confidence, steady hand, all of that. Instead you hear yourself say, "No. But we're going together."

The wave of clocks breaks over the deck, and each tick that touches the crew changes them—suddenly they are all wearing your face, all gripping the wheel, all shouting "Hard to port!" in your voice. It should be terrifying. It is, a little. But it is also strangely comforting, like realizing you were never steering alone.

The storm doesn't end. Storms in dreams rarely do. But the sea flattens into something like glass, and far off you see a light—not land, exactly, more like the memory of a window you once looked out of. The crew, all wearing your face, begins to hum a song you don't remember learning but somehow know every note of.

You keep steering. The wheel keeps being a dinner table. You keep going.

Somewhere, in a bed, in a dark room, your hand twitches against the sheets, holding fast to a wheel that isn't there.
