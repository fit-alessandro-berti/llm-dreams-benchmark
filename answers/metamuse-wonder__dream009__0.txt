...find a way to blend in, and fast.

Because you are standing in the middle of a dirt market road in your pajamas, and everyone is staring at you.

You woke up moments ago under a thatched roof, on a bed of straw that scratched your skin, to a silence so complete it hurt. No hum of electricity. No distant traffic. No phone on a nightstand. Just birds, a rooster somewhere far off, and the smell of woodsmoke, wet earth, and animals.

Your hands aren't quite your hands. They're rougher, tanned, calloused at the palms — like they belong to someone who has worked outside his whole life.

Outside, the world is wrong in a thousand beautiful ways.

The houses are timber and mud, leaning slightly as if tired. A blacksmith works an open forge, sparks flying with every hammer strike. Women carry water in wooden buckets from a stone well. Children chase a pig between carts piled with turnips, cabbages, and sacks of grain. A priest in a brown robe rings a handbell as he walks.

No one has shoes like yours. Because you don't have any.

"Boy! Boy, are ye daft?"

An old woman grabs your sleeve. Her English is thick and strange, half-familiar, like hearing a song you know played on an unfamiliar instrument. She shoves a rough wool cloak into your arms.

"Cover thyself, lest the Reeve think ye mad."

You don't know what a Reeve is, but the way people step away from a group of men on horseback coming down the road tells you enough. You pull the cloak on. It smells of sheep and smoke, and it's unbearably itchy, but suddenly you are invisible. Just another poor villager in 1215.

And that's when the hunger hits. Real hunger. Not snack hunger. A deep, hollow ache. You have no money — at least, no money they'd recognize. No home. And as you look at the sun, already sinking toward the tree line, you realize people are closing shutters, bringing animals inside, barring doors.

Night here isn't just dark. It's dangerous.

You follow the crowd toward the fields, hoping work means food. An overseer points at you, then at a scythe leaning against a fence. You take it. Your strange, calloused hands seem to know what to do even if you don't. For hours you cut wheat under a white sun, your back screaming, sweat soaking the cloak. Dream-logic takes over — one moment it's morning, the next the sky is bruised purple and your arms are full of sheaves.

A girl no older than sixteen presses a hunk of coarse black bread and a cup of sour ale into your hands. It's the best thing you've ever tasted.

"You are not Thomas," she whispers, studying your face.

Your heart stops. "What?"

"Thomas has a scar here." She touches her chin. "And he does not look at the sky as if he hath never seen it."

Before you can answer, the church bell begins to toll. Once. Twice. Everyone freezes. Wolves, someone mutters. Or raiders. Or sickness in the next village.

Everyone runs.

You run too, bread still in your hand, through mud that sucks at your bare feet, past the well, past the forge now cooling in the dark. The village shrinks behind you and suddenly — as dreams do — you're not on the road anymore. You're at the edge of a river you don't remember crossing. The water is black and perfectly still, and your reflection isn't Thomas, and it isn't quite you either.

It's you, asleep. In your own bed. In your own time.

The bell tolls again, but now it's changing. Lower. Louder. More insistent. Seven tolls.

*Beep. Beep. Beep.*

You gasp awake to your alarm at 7:00 a.m., sunlight through your blinds, phone buzzing, the familiar hum of the refrigerator from the kitchen.

Your back aches like you really did spend a day in a field.

And when you swing your feet to the floor, you stop.

Between your toes is dried mud.

And on your pillow, a single piece of straw.
