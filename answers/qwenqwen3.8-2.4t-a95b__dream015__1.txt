

Despite the hardships, you decide to keep moving toward the thin line of green you’ve seen on the horizon, as if some part of you remembers that the world can still be repaired.

You walk through the hollow streets of a city that used to be loud with traffic and laughter. Now, wind slips through broken windows, carrying dust and the faint smell of rain. Your boots crunch over glass, but you step carefully, not just to avoid noise, but because every object feels like a memory someone lost.

Hunger sits in your stomach like a stone, and thirst makes your thoughts blur at the edges. Yet, when you pass a collapsed library, you stop. Beneath the debris, a single book lies open, its pages fluttering in the breeze. The words have faded, but one sentence remains visible:

*“What remains can still become something new.”*

You pick the book up and place it in your bag. It is not food. It is not water. But it feels necessary.

As evening falls, you find shelter inside an old subway station. Shadows gather along the tracks, and somewhere far below, water drips in a steady rhythm. You build a tiny fire from broken chair legs and paper, and the light makes the tiled walls look like the inside of a jewel.

Then you hear footsteps.

You tense, hand moving to the small knife at your side, but a child steps into the glow of the fire. Their coat is too large for them, patched with mismatched fabric, and they carry a backpack that clinks softly with stones.

“I saw your light,” they say. “I thought maybe you weren’t dangerous.”

You hesitate. In this world, trust is a risk. But their eyes are tired, not threatening.

Slowly, you move aside to make room by the fire. “Sit,” you say. “I don’t have much.”

The child smiles faintly. “I have water.”

They offer you a battered canteen, and in exchange, you share the last piece of dried fruit in your pocket. It is small, almost nothing, but as you split it in half, it feels like a feast.

The child’s name is Wren. They have been alone for weeks, traveling toward a rumored settlement beyond the river, where people are trying to grow food in the ashes of the old world. They speak of it like a bedtime story, afraid to believe it fully.

You listen, and for the first time in days, the knot in your chest loosens.

When night deepens, the fire dims to embers. You dream inside the dream: a field of tall grass, a house with its windows glowing gold, voices calling your name from somewhere safe. When you wake, Wren is already watching the tunnel entrance, alert but quiet.

Outside, dawn has come.

The sky is pale and bruised, but the green line on the horizon looks closer now. It is not just a illusion; there are trees, real trees, thin and struggling but alive.

You and Wren walk together. The world is still broken, still dangerous, still hungry. But now your footsteps have direction. When you reach the river, you find a makeshift bridge built from old doors and rescued timber. On the other side, smoke rises from campfires, and you see tents shaped by careful hands.

Someone sees you and lifts a hand in greeting.

Not salvation. Not safety, not forever.

But possibility.

And in a world that has lost almost everything, that is enough to begin again.
