You are feeling lost and alone in a bustling city. Suddenly, a group of kind strangers offers to help you find your way, and you say yes before you have even understood the question, because their faces are turned toward you the way sunflowers turn, all at once, and it has been so long since anything in this city turned toward you.

There are five of them, or six. You keep counting and the number changes, not because anyone leaves or arrives but because one of them is sometimes two people and sometimes one, a tall woman in a grey coat who occasionally has a child holding her hand and occasionally does not. Nobody remarks on this. You decide not to either.

"Where are you trying to go?" asks a man with a paper cup of something warm. He offers it to you and you drink. It tastes like the inside of a train station, like the smell of it, if a smell could be a flavour. It is not unpleasant.

You open your mouth to tell them the address and find that you do not have one. You have the shape of a place. You know it has a green door and that the stairs inside creak on the third step, and that someone is waiting there who is not angry with you yet but might become so if you take much longer. You try to explain this and they nod as if it is the most ordinary set of directions in the world.

"The green door," says the woman in the grey coat. "Yes. That's this way."

They move and you move with them. The crowd that had been shouldering past you for what felt like hours now parts around the group like water around a stone, and you realize with a small ache that it had always been possible to walk this easily, that you had simply been walking alone.

The streets do not behave. You pass the same bakery three times, each time from a different direction, and each time the woman behind the counter is doing something else: kneading, then sweeping, then sitting on an upturned crate with her face in her hands. The third time, the man with the paper cup goes in and comes out again and she is laughing. He does not tell you what he said to her.

A younger man in the group, who has not spoken, walks beside you for a while. He has your father's hands. Not resembling them; they are your father's hands, the particular scar across the knuckle, the nail he split as a boy. You look at his face and it is not your father's face, it is nobody's face, a kind face that could belong to a hundred strangers.

"You've been here before," he says.

"I don't think so."

"You've been lost here before, I mean. You just didn't call it that."

You want to argue but the street tilts upward and you have to concentrate on climbing. The buildings on either side grow taller and lean inward until the sky is a thin bright seam above you. Somebody takes your elbow. Somebody hums. The tune is one you know from childhood but the words, when you reach for them, come out in a language you have never learned and understand completely.

At the top of the hill the city opens out. You can see all of it, and it is not as large as it felt from inside. From up here it looks like something you could hold, a thing made of lit windows and wet roofs, and you feel a rush of tenderness toward it that surprises you, the way you might feel toward a dog that has bitten you and is now asleep.

"There," says the woman in the grey coat. The child is with her now. The child points.

Below, on a street you had walked down twice already, there is a green door. You can see it from here because it is the only green thing in the whole grey-and-gold spread of the city, a small definite colour, like a word underlined.

"I walked right past it," you say.

"Everyone does," says the man with the paper cup, and hands you another one. You did not see him get it. "That's why we do this."

You start down the hill together, and as you descend the group grows thinner. Not that they leave. They become harder to see, the way people do when you are looking for the thing beyond them. By the time you reach the bottom you are certain there are only two of them left, then one, the younger man with your father's hands, and then you are standing in front of the green door alone, except that alone has stopped meaning what it meant an hour ago.

You put your hand on the door. It is warm, which doors are not.

Inside, the stairs creak on the third step, exactly as you knew they would. At the top, someone is waiting, and their face when they see you is not angry. It is the face of someone who was worried and is now trying not to show how much. You know this face. You have worn it, waiting for other people.

"You found it," they say.

"I had help," you say, and turn to point at the strangers, and the doorway behind you is full of the ordinary evening, the ordinary crowd, no one looking at you at all.

But the paper cup is still in your hand. It is still warm. And when you drink from it now it tastes like nothing in particular, like water, like the inside of your own mouth, and you understand that this is the taste of having arrived.

You sit down on the third step, the one that creaks, and you let the sound of it hold you there for a while, in the house with the green door, in the city that is smaller than it looks, in the company of everyone who has ever stopped to ask a stranger which way.
