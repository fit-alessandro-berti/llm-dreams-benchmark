Each challenge is stranger than the last.

The first round asks you to describe your dream job in a single sentence. You're certain you know the answer—until you open your mouth and birdsong comes out. The judge nods slowly, as if this is precisely what she hoped to hear.

For the second round, every contestant is handed a suitcase to carry across an endless ballroom. Yours grows heavier with each step and whispers your childhood phone number. You are not allowed to open it. You never learn what's inside.

Then there's the kitchen—cathedral-sized, humming—where you must bake a cake using only memories. The flour is a jar labeled *Tuesdays, age nine*, and it tastes like sunscreen and your grandmother's hands. Someone at the next station is crying. Nobody remarks on this.

Time misbehaves throughout. The clock above the stage has no hands, and the competition lasts three hours or several years, depending on how you breathe.

In the final round, only two contestants remain: you, and someone with your exact face wearing a confidence you've never owned. You stand before a towering golden door stamped GRAND PRIZE. The host—who might have been your father earlier, or possibly a mailman—announces that the job belongs to whoever can walk through without wanting what's on the other side.

Your look-alike freezes, white-knuckled, wanting it too badly. But you've emptied out somewhere along the way—too tired, too full of birdsong and Tuesday cake—and the door lets you pass.

Behind it there is no corner office, no title, no desk engraved with your name. Just your old bedroom. Morning light pooled on a made bed. Everything ordinary. Everything breathing.

Somehow, that's the prize.

You wake with sun on your face and the faint taste of Tuesday still in your mouth, late for the job you already have—which, for one lucid moment before the alarm finishes ringing, feels almost identical to the dream one.
