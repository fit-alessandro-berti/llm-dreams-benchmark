encounter various groups of people and each group seems to be caught in its own strange gravity.

One circle of guests is whispering about a song that only plays when no one is listening. Another is seated around a table playing cards, but every time someone lays one down, it turns into a leaf and drifts to the floor. A woman in a silver coat offers you a drink that reflects not your face, but the room you were in as a child. You politely decline, because you are still searching for the one person you know.

You move from conversation to conversation, but every time you ask for them, the answers shift. “They were just here,” says a man whose watch is running backward. “They’re waiting where the noise ends,” says a child carrying a candle that gives off cold blue light instead of flame.

Then you see them for a moment across the room—standing near a tall window, smiling as if they’ve been watching you all along. You hurry toward them, but the crowd rearranges itself like a maze. The music grows louder, then suddenly becomes the sound of rain.

When you finally reach the window, the person you know is no longer there. Instead, there is a door left slightly open, and beyond it is not another room, but a quiet street under a deep violet sky. On the door is a note in their handwriting:

*You don’t have to find me to not be alone.*

As you read it, the gathering behind you softens into silence. When you turn around, the strangers are gone, and the house is filled only with warm light and the faint smell of something familiar. You realize you no longer feel lost.
