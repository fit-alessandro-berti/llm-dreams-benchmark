...begin to speak, and the words don’t come out as sound first. They arrive like light through water: your name becomes a ribbon of silver, the audience’s faces loosen into a tide of soft eyes, and each raised hand turns into a small paper lantern drifting toward the ceiling. You step forward, half-aware that the stage floor is warm like a wrist, and when you open your mouth the air tastes of thunder, honey, and old library paper.

The crowd does not shout agreement. They hold still, listening with the patience of sleeping animals. Somewhere beyond the lights there is a door that is not quite a door, and the speech starts there—not in a podium, but in the pressure behind your ribs. You say: “We don’t enter a room without carrying it.” A few audience members sway as though to a rhythm you can’t see. The screens behind them fill with sky, then with a wound of light, then with your own childhood kitchen, where everyone was talking at once and nobody was shouting.

You begin to understand the speech is not to be performed but to be remembered. The words form a shape you’ve known before: a staircase rising toward a face that is half yours and half the dreamer calling you to wake. Your voice dips into a chord that feels like a question you didn’t ask, and the crowd answers by leaning in, curious, unafraid. Then the curtain opens—not to reveal a hall, but to reveal a morning where every face is slightly possible: familiar, strange, forgiven.

And in that instant, the dream asks one impossible question: if the audience is listening, who is the speaker—and why does it feel less like speaking and more like remembering a language just before it dissolves?
