You know the sound of each solution by now: the clack of the counting stones, the long sigh of air through the organ pipes. Whoever it is gets through the room with the seven clocks much faster than you did. You feel a little put out about that. Then you hear them trying the handle of the blue door, which has to be lifted as you turn it, and you call back, “Up a bit.”

“Thanks,” says a small voice.

Ahead is a circular room with a table set for breakfast. There is one chair. On the table are two cups, a boiled egg, and a slip of paper that reads: *What have you left behind?*

You go through your pockets. Pencil, string, small brass key. All present. You try to remember whether you had a coat when you came in.

The child arrives while you are looking under the table. She has a crescent of blue paint on her forehead from the door. Without asking, she sits down and begins to butter the slip of paper.

“That's the question,” you tell her.

“Oh.” She turns it over. “It's toast on this side.”

You recognize her then. Not from a photograph. It’s the way she butters right up to the corners, because there is nothing worse than the last bite being dry. You used to be particular about that, too.

You pull up a chair. There wasn't another chair a moment ago, but there wasn't any marmalade either, and now there is.

“Did you figure out the clocks?” you ask.

“No,” she says. “I climbed through the window.”

Beyond her shoulder, morning light falls across the floor. The window is low and wide, and through it you can see the garden of the first house you lived in. The grass is wet. Your red sandals are out there, toes dark with dew.

For a moment you ache to get to them before the garden changes into something else. Then the child puts half the toast on your plate.

You eat it while it’s hot.
