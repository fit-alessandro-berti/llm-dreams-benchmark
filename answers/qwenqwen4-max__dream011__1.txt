…written in your own handwriting.

Not printed, not typed — your loops, your lazy crossing of the *t*, the way you always let the last word of a sentence trail off downhill. You don't remember writing them. You turn the page hoping the second page will be in someone else's hand, and it is: it's your handwriting from when you were nine, enormous, each letter a separate act of will.

Question 1 is easy. You know the answer the way you know your own name. But when you uncap the pen, the answer steps politely out of reach, like a word in a language you spoke as a child.

The clock above the whiteboard has no numbers, only a second hand sweeping through positions that mean *soon*, *soon*, *now*, *too late*, *soon* again. The student to your left is filling page after page with small careful drawings of doors. She seems entirely at peace. You notice the room is not the room you sat down in. There are windows now, and outside the windows is the hallway of a building you lived in before this one, and someone down there is practicing the same four notes on a piano, over and over, never getting to the fifth.

Question 7: *Describe the moment you decided on this career. You may not use any words you learned afterward.*

You write *I wanted to be useful* and the ink lifts off the page and rearranges itself into *I wanted to be told I was useful*, and you try to scratch it out, but scratching out only makes it larger, and anyway the proctor is walking past and she doesn't seem to mind. She isn't collecting answers. You see it suddenly, the way you see things in dreams, without any of the steps: she is collecting the *questions*. The papers go into a box and the box is going somewhere to be asked again.

You flip to the last page. There is only one question on it, and it's one somebody actually asked you once, gently, in a real conversation, and you answered it very quickly because you thought quickness was the same thing as honesty. Down here it waits with the patience of a thing that has all night.

You look up. Every desk is occupied by you at a different age, all of them writing, none of them anxious, and the youngest one is chewing her pen and looking out the window at the hallway, at the four notes, and she is not worried about the exam at all, because she hasn't been told yet what it determines.

The bell rings. It sounds like the fifth note.

You stand up with your paper still blank and understand — with relief so total it feels like grief — that you have handed in blank pages for years, and passed, and will be back tomorrow, and that this is not a threat.

You wake with an answer in your mouth. It's a good one. It's gone before you can find a pen.
