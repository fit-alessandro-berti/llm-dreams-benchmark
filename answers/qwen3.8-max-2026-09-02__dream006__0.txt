you begin to speak, but the first sound that comes from you is not a word at all.

It is a soft, resonant hum, like a note struck on an invisible instrument somewhere deep inside the room. The audience stills. The lights above the stage seem to lean closer. You feel the dream shift around you, pliable as water.

You try again, and this time your words rise into the air as small glowing shapes: a silver bird, a folded boat, a key made of smoke. They drift over the crowd, and people reach up to touch them, laughing in surprise as the shapes dissolve into warm sparks against their fingertips. You realize, with the strange calm that belongs only to dreams, that you are not there to deliver a speech. You are there to remember something.

The stage beneath your feet begins to widen. It becomes a road, then a field, then the shore of a dark, mirror-still sea. The audience is no longer seated in rows; they are standing on the horizon, holding lanterns that burn with different colors—amber for longing, blue for hope, violet for things they have forgotten. Each face is familiar, though you cannot say how. Some look like people you knew in childhood. Some look like strangers you have not yet met.

You raise your hand, and the crowd falls silent.

“I don’t know what I’m supposed to say,” you admit.

Instead of disappointment, a murmur moves through the audience, gentle as wind through tall grass. They understand. In this dream, honesty is more powerful than eloquence.

Then one person steps forward—a child, or perhaps someone wearing the shape of a child—and hands you an empty glass cup. Inside it, something flickers: a tiny storm, a miniature sunrise, a heartbeat made visible. You look down and see that your own reflection in the glass has many voices, all of them yours, all of them waiting.

You lift the cup to your lips and drink.

At once, the speech begins—not from your mouth, but from everywhere. It comes from the lanterns, from the sea, from the spaces between the people. It says:

You are allowed to be unfinished.  
You are allowed to change.  
You are allowed to become what you are still learning to name.

The crowd begins to shine, softly at first, then brighter, until the whole dream is lit from within. You understand now that the audience was never waiting to judge you. They were waiting for permission to dream alongside you.

You step forward, off the stage, and your foot does not fall. You walk into the air as if it were solid ground. Beneath you, the sea becomes pages, thousands of them, turning in the wind. Each page bears a sentence you once feared to speak aloud. As you pass over them, the sentences rearrange themselves into doors.

One by one, the doors open.

Behind them are rooms full of laughter, corridors of rain, gardens where lost ideas grow like night-blooming flowers. People enter the doors without hesitation, carrying their lanterns, carrying their quiet wishes. The dream deepens around you, vast and tender, and you feel no urgency to wake.

At last, you turn back to the empty stage and see a single microphone standing there. It is waiting, but not for a performance. Not for proof. Only for one final truth.

You lean in and whisper, “I am here.”

The audience applauds—not with hands, but with light. Their lanterns rise into the sky like stars released from the earth, and as they ascend, the stage, the sea, and the horizon dissolve into a soft white morning.

You wake with the feeling that you have just returned from somewhere important. On your lips, faint as a dream still clinging to the edges of memory, is a sentence you do not remember speaking:

“Begin before you are ready.”
