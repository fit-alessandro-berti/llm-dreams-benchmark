You are participating in a competition where the grand prize is your dream job. As the competition progresses, you face various challenges and the challenges keep changing shape before you can finish them.

The first one is simple enough. You are handed a stapler and told to staple the ocean. You stand at the edge of a pier that smells of tar and warm plastic and you press the stapler against the water and it works, sort of; the waves hold together for a moment with a small silver seam, and the judges nod from their folding chairs. There are three judges. One of them is your fourth-grade teacher, Mrs. Halloran, who has been dead for years but seems fine about it. One is a man in a gray suit whose face you can never quite look at directly; when you try, your eyes slide off him like he's been waxed. The third judge is a very large dog wearing a lanyard. The lanyard says JUDGE.

"Good," says the man in the gray suit. "Now do it faster."

You staple faster. The ocean starts to pucker. Somewhere behind you a woman is timing you with a stopwatch that ticks like a heartbeat, and you realize it is your heartbeat, she is just holding it, and you want to ask for it back but that seems rude in the middle of a challenge.

The second challenge takes place in the building where you would work if you won. You know it is that building because it looks exactly like every office you have ever imagined wanting and also exactly like your childhood home, the two laid over each other like transparencies. The hallway is your parents' hallway, but the carpet is the good corporate kind, blue-gray and dense, and the family photos on the wall are of people you have never met, all of them smiling, all of them wearing the lanyard that says JUDGE.

The task is to find your desk.

Everyone else in the competition already seems to know where theirs is. They walk purposefully past you, holding coffee, and they have names on their doors, and when you look at your own hands you find you are holding a coffee too, so at least you have that part right. You open a door and it is a supply closet full of your old report cards. You open another and it is the pier again, the stapled ocean now bulging at its seams. You open a third door and Mrs. Halloran is sitting behind a desk with your name on it.

"Oh," she says, "were you looking for this? I'm just keeping it warm."

She does not get up.

You want to say that's my desk but the words come out as a question, and she smiles the way she used to when you got an answer almost right, and marks something on a clipboard.

The third challenge is an interview, except you are the one asking the questions and the job is the one answering them. It sits across from you in a chair, and it looks like a warm room with a window, and then like a check with a lot of zeros on it, and then like the face of someone who respects you, and each time it changes you feel a small pull in your chest like a hook being set. You ask it, "Where do you see yourself in five years?" and it says, "Inside you," which you find both alarming and reasonable.

You ask what your weaknesses are. It thinks for a while. It says, "You will do anything for me." It says this kindly.

Between challenges there are hallways, and the hallways are where the other contestants gather, and you begin to notice that they are all you. Not exactly — one of you is taller, one of you went to the other school, one of you stayed in that relationship, one of you is wearing the good coat you almost bought — but all of you have the same nervous way of touching the lanyard, and none of the lanyards say JUDGE. Yours says CONTESTANT. You had not noticed that before. When you see it you feel the word settle around your neck a little tighter.

The taller one leans over and says, "I heard they already picked."

"Picked who?"

"Someone. A while ago. Before it started." She shrugs. "That's what I heard."

You ask her why she is still here, then, and she looks at you like the question is very young. "Where else would I go," she says. Not a question.

The fourth challenge is announced over a PA system that speaks in your own voice, slightly slowed. You are to give a presentation on why you deserve this. You have no slides. You walk to the front of a room that is the pier and the hallway and the office and your childhood bedroom all at once, and the audience is the dog, mainly, and the dog is looking at you with the enormous patience of dogs, and you open your mouth to make the case and what comes out is a list of everything you have ever been afraid of, in order, starting with the dark and ending with this room.

The dog listens to all of it. When you finish, the dog gets up, comes over, and puts its head against your leg.

The man in the gray suit says, "That wasn't the assignment."

The dog says nothing, because it is a dog, but it does not move its head.

Then you are in a small room with a single window, and the window is the ocean, and the staples are gone, and it is just water again, moving how it wants. There is a piece of paper on a table in front of you. This is the final challenge. You understand that without anyone telling you. The paper is a contract, or an offer letter, or a permission slip — the text keeps rearranging itself, but the shape of it is a promise. Sign here and it's yours. All of it. The warm room and the check and the respect, and Mrs. Halloran will get up from your desk, and the lanyard around your neck will change its one word.

There is a pen. You pick it up.

The pen is heavy, and you realize that it is heavy because it is full, and what it is full of is the years. You can feel them sloshing. You are meant to write with them. That is the price and it is written very plainly on the paper now, in the only line that has stopped moving: the letter is addressed to you, and it is signed by you, and it is dated years from now, and it says, in your handwriting, thank you for everything you gave me.

You stand there for a long time with the pen above the line.

Outside the window, the ocean does not care whether you sign. This feels, unexpectedly, like the most generous thing anyone has offered you all day.

You look down and the lanyard has come off in your hand. You do not remember taking it off. The word on it is still CONTESTANT, and you turn it over, and the back of it is blank, and it has been blank the whole time, and you wonder how many of the others ever thought to look.

Then you are walking. Not toward the pier and not toward the hallway, just walking, down a road you don't recognize and are not afraid of. The dog is walking beside you. It has left its lanyard somewhere too. You do not know if you won. You have the strong sense that this is the same thing as knowing.

Behind you, very faintly, the PA system is announcing the next challenge in your slowed voice, and you wait to feel the pull in your chest, the hook setting.

It doesn't come. You keep walking. The road smells like rain, and the dog has found a stick, and neither of you has anywhere to be.
