You turn to leave, but the doorway drifts farther away with each step, like the room is stretching to keep you inside. Behind you, your friend's voice distorts, looping and overlapping: *"You always— you never— why can't you just—"* until it sounds like a chorus of a hundred versions of them, all hurt, all angry.

Then, without warning, the scene dissolves and you're standing in your childhood kitchen. Your friend sits at the old wooden table, calm, stirring a cup of tea. "Sit down," they say gently. "You look tired."

You sit. The anger is still there, but softer now, like an ember wrapped in ash. "I don't want to lose you over this," you hear yourself admit. Your friend's face flickers—briefly your mother, then a stranger, then them again—as if the dream can't decide who should be comforting you.

"You won't," they say. "This argument was never about being right. It's about being heard."

The steam rising from your tea forms shapes in the air: a handshake, a broken chain, an open door.

The dream shifts one last time. You're both standing on a bridge over a river that flows uphill. The argument resumes, but now your voices have become music—clashing harmonies, dissonant chords filling the sky. And then, strangely, you realize you're both saying the same thing, just in different languages, and the meaning lands in your chest all the same.

You wake with your heart pounding. Sunlight. Ceiling. The phrase *"being heard"* still ringing in your ears—and a sudden, urgent desire to call your friend. Not to win. To listen.
