You start to ask a stranger for directions, but when you open your mouth, the address dissolves—you know only that it began with a number, and that the number mattered enormously. So you stop a woman walking three identical greyhounds. When you try to describe the meeting, she nods as though she's been expecting the question. "You were there this morning," she says. "You just have to get back before noon." The dogs watch you with exactly the same expression, and when you blink, she's gone.

Your phone flickers alive in your pocket, but the map shows your blue dot drifting several blocks ahead of you, strolling calmly along without you. Forty-three minutes left. You check again: twelve minutes. Again: two hours. Time refuses to hold still.

You dive into a subway station. The turnstile wants a token you don't have; a child in a school uniform presses one into your palm without a word and is swallowed by the crowd. The train arrives instantly, as if it had been waiting. The destination sign displays your grandmother's street, spelled slightly wrong. You board anyway—wrong trains sometimes know things you don't.

But you surface blocks from where you need to be, and now you can see it: the glass tower, unmistakably the right building, impossibly far away. You run. This is where the dream does its cruelest work—your legs churn but you barely move, wading through air gone thick as honey. A bicycle leans against a lamppost; its pedals spin uselessly under your feet. Somewhere, a clock strikes eleven-thirty, which is not a thing clocks do.

At last, the revolving door. In the elevator, rows of buttons offer every floor except yours—1 through 60, plus M, plus one marked only with your mother's initials. You press it. The doors open at once onto the meeting room.

Everyone is already seated. They turn toward you without annoyance, almost with relief. Your chair waits at the head of the table, and on the table lies the paperback you found inexplicably in your pocket an hour ago. A woman slides a blank sheet of paper toward your seat and says gently, "You're just in time. We couldn't begin without the number."

You wake with your heart hammering, still trying to remember what the number was, whether you made it, whether you ever knew. For a moment the bedroom is unfamiliar city, every corner wrong. Then it settles. Outside the window, in the early gray light, a dog barks three times—each bark exactly identical.
