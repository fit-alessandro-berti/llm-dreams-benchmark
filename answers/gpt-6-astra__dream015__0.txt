It is an absurd decision. You have six mouthfuls of water, a knife with no handle, and three seeds you found in the pocket of a dead man’s coat. Around you, the city stands in blackened ribs.

Still, you choose a patch of earth beside an abandoned bus stop and begin to dig.

That night, something knocks beneath the soil.

Three slow taps.

You press your ear to the ground.

“Is it safe?” a voice asks.

You look at the empty windows, the distant fires, the moon snagged on a radio tower.

“No,” you whisper.

The voice considers this.

“Is there room?”

You widen the hole.

By morning, a small tree has grown there. Its leaves are scraps of paper, covered in handwriting you almost recognize. Grocery lists. Birthday cards. Directions to houses that no longer exist.

On its lowest branch hangs a single pear.

You are reaching for it when a girl steps out from behind the bus shelter. She carries a dented saucepan and watches your hand.

“Is that yours?”

You could say yes. You dug the hole. You spent the water.

Instead, you ask if she has a knife.

She does.

The pear tastes ordinary: a little underripe, gritty near the core. You eat your half too quickly. The girl saves some of hers in the saucepan.

Then the bus shelter creaks. Its faded timetable fills with new writing.
