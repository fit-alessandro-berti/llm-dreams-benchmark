You are given a mysterious locked box with no key in sight. As you examine it closely, you notice intricate patterns and carvings that seem to shift when you aren't looking directly at them. Vines of dark brass curl across the lid, and between them, tiny figures — travelers, maybe, or pilgrims — walk along grooves worn smooth as riverbed stones. When you tilt the box, they keep walking, always uphill, always toward the keyhole.

The keyhole itself is warm. You press your thumb against it and feel a slow pulse, like something inside is sleeping and does not want to be woken all at once.

Someone behind you says, "You've had that box before." You turn, and it's a woman you recognize without being able to name — your grandmother's hands, your old teacher's voice, a coat you once lost on a train. She isn't looking at you; she's looking at the box the way people look at photographs of houses they grew up in.

"I don't have the key," you tell her.

"No one has the key," she says. "That's not how it opens."

The room you are in has changed while you talked. It was a kitchen; now it is a long hallway lined with clocks, none of them agreeing, all of them ticking in a rhythm that matches the pulse under your thumb. You understand, the way you understand things in dreams — completely and without evidence — that the box is keeping time for something, and that it has been doing so for a very long while, and that it is tired.

You walk down the hallway carrying it. It grows heavier with each clock you pass, or perhaps you grow smaller. By the last clock the box is the size of a door, and it is a door, standing upright in the middle of the hallway with the same brass vines, the same walking figures, now life-sized and pausing to watch you.

One of them, a traveler with your face, holds out an empty hand. Not a key — an offer.

You put your palm against theirs. The carved hand is warm, warmer than the keyhole, and it closes gently around yours and pulls you into the pattern. There is no click, no swinging hinge. You are simply on the other side now, inside the box, and it is not dark at all. It is an orchard at dusk, rows of trees hung with small lit windows instead of fruit, each window showing a room from a house you have lived in or will live in. In one, a kitchen. In one, a hallway of clocks, empty now, all of them finally agreeing.

Far off between the trees, the woman in the coat is walking away, uphill, small as a carved figure. She raises one hand without turning around.

You sit down in the grass with your back against a tree, and above you the windows sway in a wind you can't feel, and you realize the pulse you'd felt in the keyhole was your own heartbeat, returned to you, and that the box had never been locked — it had been holding itself shut around this orchard the way a hand holds a firefly, waiting for someone patient enough to be let in rather than to break in.

You close your eyes inside the dream, and somewhere a lid closes softly, like a word at the end of a sentence, and everything the box was keeping is keeping you now.
