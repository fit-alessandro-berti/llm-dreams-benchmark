You wake up in a completely different era, with no modern technology around. As you explore your new surroundings, you realize you need to invent a life before anyone asks you for one.

The first thing that reaches you is the quiet. Not silence — there are birds, a distant ox-bell, someone coughing in a field — but the electric hum you have lived inside your whole life is gone, as if the world has unplugged itself.

You sit up in wet stubble. Smell arrives next: barley, woodsmoke, animal heat, bread baked until the crust is almost bitter. The wheat beyond the ditch is almost painfully gold. Your clothes are wrong — too even in the weave, too clean at the cuffs — and your hands give you away at once. No splits. No rope-burn. No dirt packed into the cuticles.

A rook lifts from the hedge and curses at you in a voice that is only a bird’s.

The lane is rutted with ox-cart tracks. A village sits low against a hill: timber and daub, roofs sagging under moss, a stone church squat as a fist. Smoke hangs from a dozen hearths. In the field, people are bent like commas, scythes flashing. A girl with a yoke of buckets looks at your mouth as if she is counting your teeth. You close it.

You have no lord. No parish. No coin that would pass here. You do not know the saint’s day, or whose land this is, or whether the year is the one before the sickness or the one after. If you say the wrong word — plastic, clock, germ, your own last name — you will not be a traveler. You will be a problem. Vagrants get whipped to the next hundred. Strangers with soft hands get asked harder questions.

The church bell rings once. Then again. Then a third time that lasts a breath too long.

A man in a stained wool cote is already in the lane. Staff in one hand, eyes not unkind — only finished with mystery.

“Whose man are you?” he asks.

You open your mouth. The bell is still hanging in the air. You need to answer before it stops.

And you still do not know what century will come out.
