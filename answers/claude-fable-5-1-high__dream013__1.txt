You are in a maze filled with complex puzzles. Each puzzle requires intense concentration and logical thinking to solve, and as you move deeper into the maze, you begin to notice that the walls are made of paper. Not thin paper, not the kind you could push a finger through, but thick, cream-colored sheets, the kind used for wedding invitations and diplomas, and every wall is covered in handwriting.

The first puzzle was a door with nine brass dials. You solved it without knowing how. Your hands turned the dials and the door opened and you thought, of course, that was obvious, though you could not have said afterward what the sequence was. The second puzzle was a floor of black and white tiles that sank when you stepped wrong. You crossed it the way you cross a room in your own house at night. The third was a riddle spoken by a mouth in the wall, and you answered before it finished asking.

Now you are in a long corridor and the puzzles have stopped being separate things. The corridor itself is the puzzle. The handwriting on the walls is the puzzle. You slow down and read.

The handwriting is yours.

Not your handwriting as it is now but as it was at different ages. Here is the loopy, over-careful cursive of a ten-year-old who has just been told that handwriting matters. Here is the cramped, slanting scrawl from the year you took notes in lectures and never read them again. Here, near the floor, are letters so large and shaky that you know you were holding the crayon in your fist. And the writing says things like: turn left where the light gets colder. And: the answer to the one with the water is not the water. And: don't trust the fourth room, I was tired when I built it.

You understand, with the calm of dreams, that you built this maze. You have been building it your whole life. Every puzzle you ever solved, every time you sat with a problem until it cracked, you were laying down another wall, another dial, another sinking tile. You are walking through the inside of your own concentration.

This is why the puzzles are easy. You are not solving them. You are remembering them.

The corridor turns and you follow the instruction near the floor, the crayon one, which says go where it smells like rain, and there is a room that smells like rain. In the center of the room there is a table, and on the table there is a puzzle you do not recognize.

It is a wooden box, about the size of a shoebox, with no visible seams. It is warm. When you put your ear to it you hear something ticking, but the ticking is irregular, like a heartbeat, or like someone tapping a pencil while thinking.

You look at the walls for help. The walls in this room are blank.

You look harder. There is one line of writing, very faint, in a hand you have never used. It is steadier than any of your handwriting has ever been. It says: this is the one you haven't done yet.

You sit down at the table. You pick up the box. You turn it over in your hands, and you feel the intense concentration start, the familiar narrowing, the way the rest of the world goes soft and far away when a problem takes you. You have felt this ten thousand times. You know it like you know your own breath.

But this time, as you concentrate, the walls of the room begin to write themselves. You can hear the pen. You cannot see it, but you hear the scratch of it moving across the paper, somewhere behind you and above you, and when you glance up there is a new sentence on the wall that was not there before, in a hand that is almost the steady one but not quite, still yours, still forming: the box has no inside.

You look at the box. You know this is true. You have always known it. The box was never the puzzle. The ticking is your own pulse, conducted through the wood into your ear.

You put the box down.

The scratching pen goes on writing. It writes: the maze has no center. It writes: you have never once been lost in here. It writes, more slowly now, as if the hand is tiring or as if it is choosing words carefully: the way out is to stop reading the walls.

You laugh, in the dream. It comes out as a small exhalation. Because of course it is. Of course the puzzle, the real one, the one you built without knowing, is that you cannot stop solving. You have made a place where every wall is a clue and every clue is in your own voice and there is no direction in which the thinking ends.

You stand up. The room that smells like rain has three doors. Each one has a puzzle on it: a lock, a set of symbols, a lever with a sequence carved beneath it. You feel your attention lunge toward them like a dog toward a thrown stick.

You do not look at them. You walk toward the wall between two of the doors, the blank wall, the wall with nothing written on it, and you put your hand on it.

It is paper. It is only paper. It has always only been paper.

You push, and it gives the way paper gives, with a sound like a breath let out, and you step through into a place with no walls at all. It is dim and open and very quiet. You can still hear the pen behind you, faintly, still writing, still trying to explain what you have just done. You do not turn around to read it.

In front of you, very far off, there is something like a horizon, and the sky above it is the same cream color as the walls were, and it is completely, perfectly blank. You have never seen anything so beautiful. You begin to walk toward it, and for the first time in the whole long dream, you are not thinking about anything at all.
