You could stumble into the middle of the crowd and realize the noise has a rhythm—like a city breathing around a wheel. One woman in a mustard scarf catches your wrist, not hard; another taps two fingers against your chest, as if checking that you’re real. Then a boy with a backpack patterned like a map says, “There are three doors in every city: the one you know, the one you avoid, and the one that only opens if you ask.”

The strangers form a loose ring. Not controlling—just surrounding you with familiar faces you haven’t seen before: an older man retracing the sidewalk with chalk, a teenage nurse-like figure with ink-smudged hands, a musician whose saxophone hums your name just out of hearing. They don’t drag you; they lead you toward a corner where the traffic thins, like the city itself has stepped aside for you.

When you ask for the street, they answer with a riddle: “Follow the sound of your own surprise.” You do. It leads to a courtyard lit by floating umbrellas, a fountain that spills coins that never land, and a small doorway made of warm air. At your side, the boy says, “You’re not lost because you’re alone; you’re lost because you’re still looking for permission to arrive.”

You hesitate—not because something feels wrong, but because the relief feels too easy. Then you step forward, and the city around you becomes gentler: the lights widen, the crowd falls back, the noise softens into a kind of hush. You understand that your destination isn’t a place at all but a feeling—the sense that someone can guide you without replacing your choices.

At the edge of the courtyard, a taxi that was never there opens its doors. Inside sits not a driver but an ordinary-looking mirror, and in it you see all the strangers you found—smiling, standing slightly apart, as if they’ve already begun to become the people you met in the dream.

Waking, the city is still loud and crowded, but you feel like you’ve been shown a shortcut to your own footing. In real life, if you’re ever in a similar moment, it helps to stay in bright public places, ask where you’re being taken, and keep contact with a trusted friend or family member—while still letting the dream mean what it can: that help can make a frightening city feel like a place you can move through, not a trap.
