...and each one stranger than the last.

The first challenge takes place in a vast hall where the constellations painted on the ceiling rearrange themselves whenever you look away. You must describe your greatest achievement, but when you open your mouth, every accomplishment dissolves like trying to hold water in cupped hands. Panic rises—until you find yourself talking about something small instead: the afternoon you taught a neighbor's kid to ride a bike, or the time you stayed late just to listen to a friend. The judges' faces soften. Apparently, that counted for more than anything you'd rehearsed.

The second challenge is an elevator with buttons for years instead of floors. You press one at random and step out into a kitchen from your childhood—the light is amber, everything smells faintly of rain—and somewhere in this room is something you forgot you owned. You search until you find it: not an object exactly, but a feeling, folded up small like a note passed in class. You pocket it before the elevator doors close again.

For the final challenge, you must simply do the job. But here is the strange part: nobody ever told you what the dream job *was*. You know only that when you imagine it, something in your chest goes warm. So you begin doing whatever that warm thing turns out to be—explaining something patiently, mending something broken, telling a story that makes someone lean forward—and it slowly stops mattering whether you're doing it correctly. The other competitors, who all look suspiciously like variations of yourself, seem to realize the same thing at the same moment.

When they announce the winner, the dream blurs the name the way dreams do. It might be yours. But the peace that settles over you doesn't depend on it, because you understand suddenly, completely, that you'd already been doing the work—in the hall, in the elevator, in the amber kitchen. The prize was never waiting at the end. It had been happening the whole time.

You wake to your alarm and your ordinary ceiling. For a few seconds you lie there with the feeling still in your chest, half-disappointed, half-lit up. Then you get up, make coffee, and head into the regular day—which looks, if you squint, a little like that hall with the moving stars.

Maybe that was the message the dream was trying to deliver before the alarm cut it off.
