Despite the hardships, you decide to keep walking east.

You've heard the rumors—every survivor has—about a place where the rain still falls clean. Where green things grow without turning black at the edges. You don't fully believe it. But belief, you've learned, is a resource too, and you've been rationing it carefully, saving it for exactly this kind of morning.

The city behind you is a skeleton of rusted rebar and shattered glass, but in the way of dreams, it doesn't stay behind. Sometimes you look over your shoulder and the skyline has rearranged itself, towers leaning at new angles, a bridge you never crossed now stretching where there was only rubble before. You've stopped trying to make it make sense. Dreams and apocalypse share the same grammar: everything is familiar and nothing is.

At midday—you think it's midday; the sun here is a pale disc behind permanent haze—you find a girl crouched beside a dead fire, cradling a mason jar filled with crickets. She looks up at you without fear.

"They sing if you're quiet," she says.

You are quiet. The crickets sing. It's the first music you've heard in what feels like years, and something in your chest that had clenched tight as a fist begins, slightly, to loosen.

She walks with you after that, though you never agree on it aloud. She tells you her name is made of a word that keeps changing every time she says it, and you accept this the way you accept everything here. Together you cross a highway where hundreds of cars sit fused together like a metal river, frozen mid-flow. You pass through a town where the buildings breathe—walls swelling and settling in long, slow rhythms—and the girl warns you not to wake them, so you step softly, softer than softly.

On the third day, or the hundredth, you crest a hill and see it: a valley where the haze simply stops, cut off in a clean line like a curtain lifted. Beyond it, the sky is impossibly blue. Actual blue. And there are trees—green ones, alive, their leaves moving in wind you can't yet feel.

The girl stops at the boundary. "I can't come," she says, and hands you the jar. The crickets have gone silent. "But they can."

You want to argue, but dreams don't allow arguments at the important moments. You step across the line, and the wind hits you—clean and cold and smelling of rain—and behind you the girl waves once, small against the grey, and then the haze closes like water over her, and you understand, the way you only understand things in dreams, that she was never walking east at all. She was walking with you. That was the whole point.

You wake with the smell of rain still in your nose, and for a long moment you lie there, holding the fading warmth of the jar in your hands, listening—really listening—for crickets.
