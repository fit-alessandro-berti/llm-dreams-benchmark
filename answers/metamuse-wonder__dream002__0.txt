The birds stop singing all at once. The leaves, which had been drifting lazily in the sunlight, begin to tremble and turn over, showing their silver undersides. The air smells of wet earth and electricity, and the hair on your arms stands up.

You start to run, not really afraid, but thrilled — like when you were a child and chased a thunderstorm home. The wind pushes at your back, carrying flower petals and glowing fireflies through the darkening trees. Thunder rolls somewhere far above the canopy, not with a crack, but with the deep, slow sound of a giant turning over in its sleep.

Then the rain comes.

But it doesn't fall down. It falls *up* — silver drops rising from the moss, from the ferns, from your own skin, floating toward the black clouds like the forest is exhaling. You laugh and hold out your hands, and the drops pass right through your palms, cool and weightless.

Lightning flashes, and for one frozen second, every tree casts a shadow in a different direction. In that light, you see a door standing alone between two oaks where there was no door before. It's your childhood front door, painted blue, with the brass handle worn smooth.

You open it.

Inside is not a house, but the eye of the storm: perfectly still, perfectly quiet, with warm sunlight falling in a circle on a bed of pine needles. You step inside and sit down, watching the impossible rain swirl silently around you, washing the forest clean.

When you finally open your eyes, you're awake in your own bed. It's morning. It's raining softly outside your window.

And there, on your pillow beside you, is a single silver-green leaf, still wet.
