You find yourself in a post-apocalyptic world, with resources scarce and dangers everywhere. Despite the hardships, you decide to plant a garden.

It makes no sense, and in the dream that is exactly why it must be done. You kneel in the ash beside the collapsed overpass and press seeds into the grey dust — seeds you do not remember gathering, warm as coins in your palm. Each one hums faintly, like a radio tuned between stations.

The first sprout comes up before you have finished planting the second row. That is how time works here: consequence outruns cause. The sprout unfurls into a tomato vine, but the tomatoes are small glowing lanterns, and inside each one you can see a tiny room — a kitchen, a child's bedroom, a hallway with coats on hooks. Rooms from before. You pick one and hold it to your ear and hear a kettle whistling, someone laughing at a joke you almost remember telling.

Others come. You don't hear them arrive; they are simply there at the edge of the garden the way people are in dreams, familiar strangers with faces that keep almost resolving into people you know. A woman in a firefighter's coat three sizes too big. An old man carrying a door with no house attached to it. A dog that walks upright when it thinks no one is watching. Nobody speaks above a whisper, because it is understood — the way things are understood in dreams, without being said — that loud noises attract the Quiet.

The Quiet is what ended the world. Not bombs, not plague. One day everything simply stopped being said, and then stopped being. The ruins around you aren't burned; they are unfinished, like sentences trailing off. Buildings dissolve at their upper floors into static. The horizon is a rough draft.

So you garden. The woman in the coat plants a row of streetlamps that come up already lit. The old man plants his door, and by evening it has grown a frame, and through the frame you can see a summer that hasn't happened yet. The dog buries something and refuses to say what, and where it dug, a small blue bicycle grows, bell first.

At night — night falls in the dream like a curtain, all at once — the Quiet comes to the fence. You feel it the way you feel someone reading over your shoulder. It presses against the garden, tasting the edges, and where it touches, the lantern-tomatoes dim one by one.

You understand, suddenly and completely, what you are supposed to do. You stand up in the dark and you speak to it. You tell it about the kettle, the coats on the hooks, the joke you almost remember. Your voice is thin as a seedling. The Quiet leans in. It has never been spoken to before, only fled from. It doesn't retreat, exactly — it settles, the way snow settles, and becomes ordinary silence, the kind gardens actually need.

In the morning the ash is soil. The overpass is a trellis. Down the row, the woman in the enormous coat is picking lanterns and putting them in a basket, and each one she picks, somewhere far off a window lights up.

You kneel to plant the last seed, the one you'd been saving, the warm one that hums the loudest. But your hand passes into the ground as if into water, and the earth takes hold of you gently, the way sleep takes hold, and you realize you were the seed all along, that you have been planting yourself this whole time, one memory at a time.

You begin to wake. The garden holds on for a moment — you can feel the vines wrapped kindly around your wrists, reluctant to let you surface — and then it lets go, and the last thing you see before the ceiling of your own room fades in is the dog, standing upright, unashamed now, raising one paw to wave goodbye.

Somewhere, a kettle is whistling. You are almost sure it is real.
