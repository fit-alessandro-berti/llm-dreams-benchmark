You are at a large social gathering where you only know one person. As you try to find them, you encounter various groups of people and each group is a small weather system. One cluster argues about the color of Tuesday; another sings a song you remember from a childhood you didn’t have. A woman in a paper crown asks you to hold her drink, which is just a glass of distant thunder. You ask everyone the same question—“Have you seen—” but the name keeps slipping off your tongue like a wet leaf. They nod and point you through doors that open into the same room from different angles. In the kitchen, someone is crying softly into a bowl of ice. In the garden, a choir of strangers hums the melody of your pulse. Then, at the top of a staircase that wasn’t there a moment ago, you see them: the one person you know, waiting under a lamp made of moonlight and old voicemails. They don’t look surprised. “You got lost,” they say. “That’s okay. I kept a seat for you.” You sit down, and the whole party goes quiet, as if it had been waiting for you to arrive. Then you wake up with their name on your tongue.
