You are walking through a peaceful forest when suddenly the weather changes dramatically. You feel a storm approaching and—just like that—the entire forest seems to inhale.

The birdsong cuts off mid-note. Every leaf turns silver-side-up, and the path behind you disappears behind a curtain of green-grey mist. The air tastes of ozone and crushed pine, and the light turns the color of deep water.

Then the storm arrives. But it is not rain that falls from the bruised sky: it is light. Thin silver threads spill between the clouds and weave through the branches, stitching the forest together with a soft, humming glow. Thunder rolls, low and patient, and for one impossible moment it sounds like your own name being spoken by someone who has known you forever.

You realize, with the calm certainty that only dreams allow, that you are asleep.

You hold out your hand, and the falling light pauses above your palm. Each droplet becomes a tiny sphere holding a memory—birthday candles, a slammed door, a road you never took, the smell of someone you loved. They hover there, waiting. The storm is not attacking the forest; it is trying to give something back to you.

At the heart of the gale stands an ancient oak you somehow know has always been waiting for you. In its trunk is a door outlined in light. You reach for the handle, and the storm holds its breath.

Inside is a small room built from the same peaceful forest you walked through moments ago, only now it is frozen in a golden afternoon. A window is open. At a wooden table sits another you—younger, barefoot, writing in a notebook with the fierce concentration of someone who still believes in happy endings.

They look up and smile. “You came back,” they say. “You always come back when the storm gets loud enough.”

“I thought I lost this place,” you answer.

The other you shakes their head. “You didn’t lose it. You just stopped listening.”

They close the notebook and hold it out. The moment your fingers touch the cover, the room dissolves into warm rain, and the forest rushes back in—only now the storm is gentle, a cleansing drizzle silvering every leaf. The thunder is no longer a warning but a lullaby.

You step out of the oak’s shadow, and the dream begins to fade at the edges, the way dreams do when you try too hard to hold them. The last thing you see is a single glowing leaf drifting down toward your outstretched hand.

You wake.

Outside your window, real rain is falling, soft and steady. The tightness in your chest is gone. And on your bedside table—impossibly, or perhaps not—you find a small, damp leaf that smells like cedar and second chances.
