...speak, but the words come out as birds.

The first one is small and gray, a sparrow that tumbles from your lips and flutters awkwardly toward the balcony. You try again, and a cardinal emerges, then a pair of finches, then something long-necked and iridescent that you don't have a name for. The audience doesn't seem surprised. They nod thoughtfully, some of them taking notes. A woman in the third row whispers to her neighbor, "Such a good point about the tides."

You glance down at your notes for help, but the index cards have gone soft and translucent, like the skin of an onion. The handwriting on them is yours, but it's in a language made entirely of staircases. You understand it perfectly, though you couldn't explain how. The first card says: *Remember to mention the house.*

What house? But the moment you wonder, you know. It's the house you grew up in, except it has an extra room you've never been inside. The door has always been there, behind the coat closet, and you've walked past it a thousand times without thinking it strange.

The birds have filled the auditorium now. They perch on the lighting rigs and the backs of the velvet seats, and the audience has begun to rise gently into the air among them, floating upward like dandelion fluff, still politely attentive. Someone in the back raises a hand with a question, but they've drifted too high to hear.

You realize the stage is slowly becoming a kitchen table, the one from your childhood, with the burn mark shaped like Florida. The microphone is a salt shaker. You lean into it anyway.

"The door," you say, and this time the word comes out as a word, clear and ringing. The whole room goes silent. The birds stop mid-flight, suspended like ornaments.

The audience, floating near the ceiling now, looks down at you with enormous tenderness, the way people look at someone who is about to remember something important.

You take a breath. You know that when you say the next sentence, you'll finally understand what's in that room. It's right there on the tip of your tongue, warm and familiar, like a song you used to know.

You open your mouth to speak—

—and somewhere very far away, an alarm begins to ring, and the whole auditorium turns, softly, to morning light.
