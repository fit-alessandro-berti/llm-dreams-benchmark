You are in the middle of a heated argument with a close friend. The disagreement is about something important to both of you, and you decide to walk away — just for a moment, just to breathe — but the door you step through doesn't lead to the hallway you expected. It opens onto the street where you both grew up, though you never grew up on the same street, and somehow in the dream this contradiction feels like the whole point.

Your friend follows you out, still talking, but their words have gone soft at the edges, like a radio between stations. You catch every third word: "never," "always," "you." The old accusatory grammar of people who love each other. You turn to answer and notice the street is filling with water, ankle-deep and warm, rising without urgency. Neither of you mentions it. The argument is too important to interrupt for a flood.

You keep arguing as you wade. The water lifts parked cars gently off the asphalt and they drift past like patient animals. Your friend says the thing they always say, the thing that starts the fight over again from the beginning, and you open your mouth to give your usual answer — the sharp one, the one you've polished — and instead a paper boat comes out. It floats off your tongue and lands on the water between you.

Your friend stares at it. Then they open their mouth to retort, and out comes another boat, slightly larger, listing to one side. Soon the water is crowded with them: every argument you've ever had, folded into small white hulls, sailing away down the flooded street in a long silent fleet.

Without words, the fight can't hold its shape. You watch each other across the water. Your friend's face keeps shifting the way faces do in dreams — for a second they look the way they did the year you met, then the way they looked at their worst moment, when you were the one who showed up. You realize you can't remember what the argument was about. You reach for it and it's like reaching for a word in a language you're forgetting mid-sentence.

The water reaches your waists. Your friend starts to laugh, and the laugh comes out as sound — real sound, the first in a long time — and it breaks something open. You laugh too. The two of you stand in the middle of the drowned street laughing at the fleet of your own stubbornness sailing off toward the horizon, where the street bends upward like the inside of a bowl and the boats climb it, unbothered by physics, becoming birds somewhere along the way.

Your friend takes your hand under the water. It is the exact gesture from a memory you can't place — maybe it never happened, maybe it's owed to you both from some future. "We were both right," they say, and in the dream this is not a compromise but a fact, obvious as weather. "That was the problem."

The water begins to recede, pulling back the way a tide does, politely, taking the cars and the boats and the whole street with it. What's left is a kitchen — theirs or yours, the dream won't commit — with two cups of something warm already poured, steam rising, as if the room had been waiting all along for the argument to finish. You sit down across from each other. Outside the window it is snowing upward, flakes returning to a white sky.

Your friend says something you can't hear, and you understand it perfectly.

You wake with your hand still curled, holding on.
