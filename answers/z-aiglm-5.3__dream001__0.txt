...run, though the streets keep rearranging themselves behind you like a shuffled deck. Your phone shows a map, but the blue dot is somewhere you're not, and when you zoom in, the street names slide off the screen like water.

A woman at a fruit cart is selling clocks instead of oranges. You ask her where the meeting is, and she points with a banana. "You already know the way," she says — in your mother's voice, though she has a stranger's face. "You've always known the way."

You turn a corner and the city becomes the hallway of your elementary school. Your footsteps make no sound on the tile. Somewhere a bell rings — the kind of bell that means something is about to begin without you.

In your pocket, you find a ticket. It has the address on it, but the letters won't hold still long enough to be read. They rearrange themselves politely every time you look. You could almost read it. You have always been almost able to read it.

An elevator opens, though you never pressed a button. A man inside holds the door and asks which floor, and you say "nine" with a confidence you cannot explain. The elevator goes sideways. Through the window — there is a window now — the whole city glows below, every lit window looking like the meeting might be happening inside it.

The doors open onto a room that can't decide what it is: conference room, kitchen, waiting room. Chairs in a circle. Everyone already seated. No one turns around, but you can feel them notice you.

There is one empty chair. It has been waiting for you a very long time.

You sit. The chair fits like it was measured for you years ago.

"Good," someone says. "Now we can begin."

You are an hour late. You are an hour early. You are exactly, impossibly, on time — and you wake just as the first word is spoken, carrying nothing with you but the strange certainty that whatever it was, it went well.
