You are about to give a speech to a large audience. As you step onto the stage, you see the crowd looking expectantly at you, and you begin to speak—but your words don’t sound like your voice at all. They drift from your mouth as visible ribbons of golden light, curling and twisting in the air like smoke before settling into the laps of the people seated closest to you.

You look down at your notes, but the paper has gone soft, like a petal, and the words written on it are in your own handwriting from when you were seven years old. Somehow, you can read them. The speech is about flying. Not airplanes, you clarify, your voice now humming like a cello string, but the exact weight of a heron’s wing, the arithmetic of staying aloft.

The stage beneath your feet turns to warm sand. The lights don’t shine down from above; they bloom from the floorboards like enormous white lilies. In the audience, faces you don’t recognize begin to soften and rearrange themselves into faces you do: your second-grade teacher, a neighbor who moved away, a friend from camp, your own reflection wearing a different hairstyle. They are all holding their golden ribbons, reading, waiting.

Panic flutters in your chest, but your mouth is full of feathers, or maybe just air that tastes like cardamom. You realize, with the absolute certainty of dreams, that you have given this speech a thousand times before. The crowd is not here to judge you. They are the assembled parts of yourself, sitting in rows of folding chairs, desperate to hear what you have to say.

You keep talking. The auditorium walls dissolve into a horizon made of stained glass. Your hands gesture, and seasons move through the seats—autumn in the mezzanine, spring in the balcony, a warm rain falling steadily over the front row. The speech ends not with a conclusion, but with a chord that everyone hums at once, a low, resonant note that vibrates in your ribs.

Then you bow, and the bow takes too long. You keep bending at the waist until you are folded in half, and then, impossibly, you step through your own legs like a doorway. On the other side, you find yourself backstage in the dark, no longer speaking, no longer heard, finally alone with the quiet, ringing warmth of what you just said.
