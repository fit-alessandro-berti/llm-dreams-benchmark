Near the fireplace, strangers are trading objects instead of stories: a brass key, a ticket stub, a blue marble. A woman hands you a paper crane. “They left this for you,” she says. When you unfold it, the creases form a map of the house, but every room is labeled *Later*.

In the kitchen, the guests wear name tags that read *Almost*, *Not Yet*, and *Ask Again*. Someone points you toward a red door. You open it and step into the kitchen again, this time from the opposite side. No one seems surprised.

The longer you search, the harder it becomes to remember the face of the person you know. You try describing them to a violinist, but all you can say is, “When they’re here, I don’t feel as though I’ve arrived by mistake.” She nods, as if this is their full address, and points upstairs.

At the end of a long hallway, you find three doors. Behind one, the party is just beginning. Behind another, it ended years ago. Behind the last, there is only a balcony beneath an enormous, starless sky.

The person is standing there.

“I’ve been looking everywhere,” you say.

“I know,” they answer. “I kept almost finding you.”

You want to ask why the house keeps changing, why everyone seems to recognize you, why you can’t remember their face. But downstairs the music starts again, and suddenly it sounds less like a party than like rain on a roof.

They step aside. Beyond the balcony is not a drop, but the room where you fell asleep. You can see yourself there, quiet and breathing.

“Do I have to go back alone?” you ask.

They take your hand.

“Not this time.”

You step forward together. Just before waking, you realize you still cannot picture their face—but you can remember, with perfect certainty, the feeling of being found.
