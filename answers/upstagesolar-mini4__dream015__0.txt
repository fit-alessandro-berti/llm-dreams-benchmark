You decide to keep walking, not because the road is easy, but because stopping means you stop being yourself. The ruins of the city stretch out ahead like a skeletal reminder of what humanity once built. Dust coats everything. The wind carries the faint, metallic taste of old industry and something sweeter—dry vegetation, hope, maybe.

You move along a cracked highway, bowing low to avoid sniper fire between rusted billboards. Your boots are worn, your pack carries little: a canteen, a fuse, a piece of bread, and the note your sister wrote before she went missing: *"If you find this, keep going. The sick bay is west. Try to hear the bells."* You tuck the note into your sleeve and press on.

Ahead, the sky is a bruised amber. The sun struggles through the smog, the way it used to, but weaker. Somewhere in the distance, a low rumble vibrates through the ground—maybe a generator, maybe a machine waking up. You press yourself against a collapsed overpass, watching for movement. Nothing. Just the wind and the birds that still dare to fly.

Then you see it: a small fire glowing in a hollow beneath a viaduct. Smoke twists upward, but the air around it is calm, almost too calm. You step out, each footfall cautious. Cracks of light reveal stone chairs, a rusted fountain, and strange carvings in the concrete—spirals, hands, and a thread of gold that seems to pulse faintly, as if waiting.

A voice speaks from the shadows, not threatening: *"You made it. We've been keeping the warmth alive."* You freeze. "Who's there?"

The figure steps into the light. It's an old man, thin, with a coat that doesn't quite fit and eyes that have seen too many winters. He holds a wooden staff wrapped in copper wire. "The station doesn't have medicines. But it has power. And warmth. And the tools to restart the water pumps. We've been waiting for someone who remembers how to listen to the land, not just fight it."

Your hands tremble. You have no dossier, no checklist, only your instincts and the weight of the road. But you also have a memory: thunder that smells like rain, a notebook full of sketches, and the aching certainty that the world isn't simply ending—it's waiting for its next chapter.

"You're the one who comes through the ash," the old man says. "What will you build?"

You swallow. The wind shifts. The fire seems to lean toward you, like a question. You take a breath, and with it, you step into the ruins. You pull the note from your sleeve, look at it, then hand it over.

"I bring more than bread," you say. "I bring my people."

The old man nods, and the twisted gate behind him groans open, releasing a warm, earthy light. Less a doorway, more a threshold. You press forward, not as a survivor in a dead world, but as a keeper of what remains.

When you finally set foot on the other side, the air is cooler, heavier, and strangely alive. In the distance, you hear not alarms, but music—soft, steady, like a heartbeat. And you know the dream isn't ending. It's just beginning.
