You are at a large social gathering where you only know one person. As you try to find them, you encounter various groups of people and each group opens for a moment to let you in, as if they had been expecting you all evening. A woman in a green dress says your name — not your name exactly, but the name you had in third grade, the one only your grandmother used — and everyone nods as though this settles something important.

You excuse yourself and keep moving. The room is longer than it was when you arrived. The far wall keeps politely stepping backward every time you get close to it, and the chandeliers overhead are lower now, low enough that the taller guests have to part their conversations around them like water around stones.

You catch a glimpse of the person you know — the back of their head, unmistakable — disappearing through a doorway near the string quartet. But when you reach the doorway it opens into a kitchen you recognize from a house you lived in years ago. The caterers are using your childhood dishes. One of them hands you a tray without looking at you and says, "You're late, but that's fine, everyone is late here."

You carry the tray because it seems rude not to. The strange thing is that carrying it makes the party easier. People stop pulling you into conversations. You pass through the crowd like a small official current, and from this new angle you can see the gathering properly: it is arranged in slow rings, like a galaxy, and everyone is drifting counterclockwise except you.

At the center of the rings there is a table with one empty chair, and sitting across from the empty chair is the person you know. They have been there the whole time. They don't seem surprised. "I saved you a seat," they say. "You kept walking past."

You sit down. The tray in your hands has become a bowl of soup, still warm. The noise of the party recedes to a hum, the pleasant kind, like an ocean two rooms away.

"How do you know all these people?" you ask.

"I don't," they say. "I only know you. Everyone here is somebody one of us almost met."

You look around. The woman in the green dress waves. The caterer from your old kitchen raises a glass. And you understand, in the way you only understand things inside a dream, that this is why the room kept growing: it has to hold everyone, every stranger you ever stood behind in line, every voice from an adjacent table, all of them circling slowly, waiting their turn to be known.

The lights dim like the beginning of a film. Your friend leans in and says, "You can stay as long as you like. The party ends when you learn the last name."

You open your mouth to ask whose. The soup in front of you has become the sea, small and contained in its bowl, with a tiny lighted ship crossing it. You watch the ship instead of answering, and somewhere behind you the quartet begins the song that was playing when you arrived, and you realize the gathering has started over, and that this time you know everyone, and the only stranger left in the room, the one everybody is trying to find, is you.

You wake with the sound of many voices settling, like a room emptying out, or filling.
