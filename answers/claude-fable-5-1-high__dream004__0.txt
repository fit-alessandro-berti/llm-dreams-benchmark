You are in a world where colors and shapes are constantly changing. As you explore this surreal landscape, you come across a blank canvas and it is the only thing that is not moving.

That is what stops you. Everything else here breathes. The hills to your left were violet a moment ago and are now a slow, spreading ochre, and they are not hills anymore but something closer to the backs of enormous sleeping animals. The sky has been trying on triangles. A river runs past your feet in a shade you have no name for, and when you look directly at it, it politely becomes green, as if it does not want to embarrass you.

But the canvas is white. Just white. It stands on an easel of plain wood in the middle of a field that keeps deciding what it wants to be, and the white of it is so still that it hurts a little to look at, the way silence hurts after a long noise.

You walk toward it. The ground rearranges itself under you, but it always puts a next step where you need one, so you stop worrying about it.

Up close, the canvas is larger than you thought. Larger than you, in fact, though from a distance it seemed you could have carried it under one arm. There is a small wooden table beside the easel with brushes laid out on a cloth, and jars of paint, and the paint in the jars is the only paint in this world that holds its color. Cadmium red stays cadmium red. Ultramarine stays ultramarine. You find you are almost moved by this. You had not realized how tired you were of things not staying.

You pick up a brush. It feels right in your hand, as though your hand has been the shape of this brush all along and you simply had not been told.

You dip it in the red and lift it toward the canvas and then you stop, because something occurs to you. In a world where everything changes, whatever you paint here will not change. It will be the one fixed thing. That is a great deal of responsibility for a single brushstroke. You stand there with the red dripping slowly off the bristles onto the shifting grass, where each drop becomes a small orange beetle and walks away.

Behind you, the landscape is making a sound now, a low humming, and you understand that it is waiting. It is not impatient. It has all the time in the world, being the world. But it is curious. It has never had anything still in it before, and it wants to see what a still thing looks like.

You make the first mark.

It is just a line. Red, slightly curved, from the upper left downward. You do not know what it is going to be. But the moment it touches the canvas, the humming behind you stops, and you turn around, and the entire landscape has gone quiet and is looking at the line.

Then something strange happens, although you are in a dream and so it does not feel strange, it feels like the obvious next thing. The hills, which had been ochre and then a kind of salmon, settle. They stop changing. They choose a color and it is the exact red of your line. They lie down in that red and hold it.

You turn back to the canvas and paint a second stroke, blue, crossing the first. Behind you, you hear the river stop rehearsing its greens and settle into blue. When you look, it is running the precise ultramarine from your jar, and it looks, for the first time, like a river that knows where it is going.

You understand now. The canvas is not a picture of the world. The world is a picture of the canvas. It has been waiting all this time for someone to tell it what to be.

You paint more carefully after that.

You paint a yellow circle high up and the sky decides on a sun. You paint a dark shape at the bottom edge and a forest grows along the horizon, and stays. You put in a small white house with one window, not because you planned to but because your hand wanted one there, and when you glance over your shoulder there is a small white house standing in the settled red hills, with a light in its window, and someone moving behind the glass.

You paint for a long time. Hours, or the dream's version of hours, which is to say a single held breath that contains a whole afternoon. You paint a road that goes from the house down toward the river. You paint a boat. You paint a figure walking on the road, and you make the figure very small, so you cannot see its face, and you do this on purpose, though you could not say why.

When you finally step back, the world behind you is finished. It is quiet and beautiful and completely still. The hills are red. The river is blue. The sun hangs where you put it. The little figure on the road is halfway to the boat and will be halfway to the boat forever.

And you feel the thing you should have expected to feel, which is that something has been lost.

The beetles are gone. The sky is not trying on triangles. Nothing is embarrassed or curious. The world was alive with indecision before, and now it is only lovely.

You look at the brush in your hand.

Then you look at the canvas, and you notice that in one corner, a corner you had not painted, a small area of white remains. You had left it blank without meaning to. It is no bigger than your palm. And in that corner, very faintly, the white is moving. It is trying to be cream. Then eggshell. Then a pale suggestion of green.

You do not paint it.

You set the brush down on the cloth and you sit in the settled red grass and you watch that corner of the canvas for a long time, and behind you, in the stillness of the world you made, you become aware of a small unfixed place somewhere off to the north, past the forest, where the colors are still changing, and the shapes have not decided, and everything is still possible.

You think that tomorrow you might walk there. You think the small figure on the road might come with you, if you let it turn around.

Then the dream, which has been very patient, folds the canvas gently in half, and then in half again, until it is small enough to fit behind your eyes, and you wake with the feeling of having held a brush, and a faint red line on your palm that fades before you can show it to anyone.
