You stand at the edge of a market square, and the first thing that strikes you is the smell—woodsmoke, wet wool, animal dung, and underneath it all, fresh bread. Timber-framed houses lean over the street like old conspirators whispering. People in rough-spun tunics brush past you, and you realize your clothes are wrong. Everyone can tell. Nobody says anything yet.

You try to speak, and the words come out strange—your mouth shapes sounds you somehow understand but couldn't have learned. The dream is being generous, in the way dreams are, handing you what you need without explaining where it came from.

You wander toward a well at the center of the square. An old man is drawing water, and when he sees you, he nods as if he's been waiting.

"You left the lamp burning again," he says.

You want to ask what lamp, but the bell in the church tower begins to ring, and it rings thirteen times, and no one else reacts. A woman walks by with a basket of fish, and the fish are singing—softly, in harmony—and no one reacts to that either. You look up and the sun is rising backward, dipping below the rooftops it just cleared, then rising again, like a hand hesitating over a choice.

"You have to find the door before you wake," the old man says, handing you a coin. "You always forget the door."

You don't remember ever being here before, but your feet do. They carry you out of the square, down a lane, through a field of wheat that parts for you as you walk. And there it is, in the middle of the field: a door, standing alone in its frame, no wall around it, warm yellow light leaking through the gap at its edge.

You reach for the handle.

The moment your fingers touch it, the whole world tilts—wheat, well, bell, backward sun—and you fall upward into your own bed. Morning light. Ceiling you know. The hum of the refrigerator down the hall.

Everything is ordinary, except your hand is closed around something, and when you open it, there's a coin in your palm. Old. Heavy. Stained with dirt from a field that shouldn't exist.
