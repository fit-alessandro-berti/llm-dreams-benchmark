You are at a large social gathering where you only know one person. As you try to find them, you encounter various groups of people and each group insists, with complete confidence, that your friend has only just left.

The party occupies an enormous house whose rooms do not seem to agree about what time it is. In one room, sunlight pours through tall windows onto a breakfast table, though everyone is dressed for evening. In the next, candles gutter in silver bowls while people in bathing suits speak in whispers about an approaching snowstorm. You ask after your friend, giving the name carefully, but the guests smile as if you have made a clever joke.

“They went through the blue door,” says a woman whose earrings are two tiny ticking clocks.

There are three blue doors.

You choose the darkest one and enter a long kitchen where dozens of strangers are icing a single vast cake. The cake is shaped like the house, and as you lean closer you see miniature people moving behind its sugar windows. One of them is you, standing in the kitchen and looking down. Another figure hurries along a marzipan corridor just beyond the room. It is your friend. You call their name, and the tiny figure pauses.

At once every person in the kitchen stops working. Their knives hover over the frosting.

“You shouldn’t wake them,” someone says.

The lights go out. When they come back, the cake is gone and you are holding a small brass key sticky with icing.

Beyond the kitchen is a ballroom crowded with people dancing without music. Their shoes make no sound. You move between the couples, trying to catch sight of the familiar face, but each time someone turns toward you, you recognize them for one startled second—as a teacher, a neighbor, a person glimpsed years ago on a train—and then their features rearrange into those of a stranger. Above them hangs a chandelier filled not with bulbs but with pale goldfish. The fish swim slowly through the air, their tails stirring light across the ceiling.

At the far end of the ballroom, your friend stands alone beside a green curtain.

Relief rushes through you so strongly that you laugh. You push through the dancers, calling out, but the room lengthens between you. The couples multiply. Their arms form archways that you have to duck beneath. Your friend watches you approach with an expression you cannot read, then steps behind the curtain.

When you finally reach it, there is no wall behind the fabric. There is a railway platform at night.

Rain hangs motionless in the air like glass beads. A train waits with all its doors open, though there are no tracks beneath it. Your friend is sitting inside by a lit window, tapping the seat beside them. You climb aboard just as the doors close.

“I’ve been looking everywhere for you,” you say.

“I know,” they reply. “That’s what the party is for.”

The train begins to move. The house slides past outside the window, room after room exposed like a dollhouse. You see the breakfast guests raising their cups, the silent dancers turning, the cooks bending over the cake. In every room there is also a version of you, still searching. Some are opening closets. Some are running upstairs. One is standing perfectly still, listening.

You ask where the train is going, but your friend points to the brass key in your hand.

There is a keyhole in the window.

When you insert the key, the glass swings inward. Cold air fills the carriage, carrying the smell of wet grass. Outside is no longer the house but a dark field scattered with hundreds of guests. They stand very far apart, each holding a candle. Seen from above, the candles spell your name.

The train slows. Your friend takes your hand, and together you step down into the field. The moment your feet touch the grass, all the candles go out except one. It is held by a child standing at the edge of a black pond. You know without being told that you must reach the child before the flame dies.

You and your friend begin to run. Yet with every step your friend grows lighter and less distinct, as though made of breath on a mirror. You grip their hand harder.

“Don’t disappear,” you plead.

“I’m not the one disappearing,” they say gently.

You look down and see that your own body has become transparent. Through your chest you can see the field, the extinguished candles, and the first gray hint of morning. Only the hand holding your friend remains solid.

At the pond, the child offers you the last candle. Reflected in the water is the crowded house, but now it is small enough to fit in your palm. All its windows are dark except one on the highest floor. Inside that window, someone is asleep in a bed you recognize as yours.

You understand that the candle is almost gone because morning is coming. You want to ask your friend whether you will remember any of this, but they squeeze your hand before you can speak.

“You found me,” they say. “That’s enough.”

The flame goes out.

For a moment there is perfect darkness, and in it you hear the whole party inhale. Then birds begin singing from somewhere very close. You wake with your hand closed tightly around nothing, and the faint taste of sugar on your tongue.
