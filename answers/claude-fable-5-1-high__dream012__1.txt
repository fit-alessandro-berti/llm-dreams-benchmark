You are the captain of a ship in the middle of a turbulent sea. Your crew looks to you for guidance as the storm worsens, and you open your mouth to give an order and find that you do not know the name of a single thing on the ship.

Not the ropes. Not the sails. Not the tall pole in the center that the sails hang from. You know that you know these words, you can feel the shape of them somewhere behind your teeth, but when you reach for them there is only the wind. So you point instead. You point at the big flapping canvas and you make a pulling-down motion with both fists, and the crew nods as though this is exactly the kind of command a captain gives, and three of them go running.

The rain is coming sideways. It tastes of iron and of something sweeter underneath, like the water from a well you drank from as a child, though you cannot say which child, or which well. The deck tilts and you grab the wheel. The wheel is warm. It is much warmer than wood should be in a storm, and when you look down you see that the spokes are not spokes at all but the fingers of a very large hand, palm up, holding the ship steady from below. You decide not to think about this. A captain must decide what not to think about. This is most of the job.

"Captain," says the first mate. She has a face you recognize from somewhere that is not this ship, a face from a kitchen, a face from across a table. "Captain, the water is rising in the hold."

You go down to look. The ladder has more rungs than it should; you climb for a long time. When you reach the bottom the hold is indeed flooding, but the water is very still, still as a mirror, and it is rising not from any breach but from the floor itself, welling up through the boards the way ink comes up through paper. It is dark blue. When you kneel and put your hand in, it is not cold. It is the exact temperature of your hand, so that you cannot tell where your hand ends.

In the water there are letters. Small ones, floating, the kind you would find in a bowl of soup. They drift together and drift apart. You watch them and understand that these are the words you lost. There is the word for rope. There is the word for the tall pole. There is the word for the thing you are supposed to do now. You try to scoop them up and they scatter between your fingers like minnows.

"Leave them," says the first mate, who has followed you down without making any sound on the ladder. "They'll come back when they're ready. Words are like that. You can't hold them under."

Above you the storm makes a sound like a house being moved.

You climb back up. The climb is short this time, only four rungs, and when you come out on deck the sky has changed. It is still storming but the clouds have organized themselves into rows, like pews, like the seats of a theater, and in each row there are people sitting. Thousands of them. They are very far up and very quiet and they are all watching the ship. Some of them are holding umbrellas. One of them, in the third row, is your mother, and she is knitting something long and grey that spills down out of the clouds and disappears into the sea off the port side, and you understand that it is the horizon, that she has been making it this whole time, that this is why it never gets any closer.

The crew has gotten the sail down. They stand around you in the rain, waiting. Their faces are the faces of everyone you have ever let down, and also the faces of everyone who has ever been kind to you for no reason, and it is a little unsettling that these are the same faces.

You have to say something. You have to steer. But there is nowhere to steer toward; the sea is the same in every direction, the same grey ridges walking at you from every side, and the audience in the clouds leans forward very slightly, and you realize they are not waiting to see if you save the ship. They are waiting to see what you will say.

So you say the only true thing you have.

"I don't know where we're going."

Nobody moves. The rain keeps falling. And then the first mate, the one with the kitchen face, laughs, not cruelly, and says, "Well. Neither does the sea," and one by one the crew begin to laugh too, and it is the kind of laughter that has fear in it and lets the fear out through the top like steam, and you find that you are laughing as well, gripping the enormous warm hand of the wheel and laughing up into the rain.

The ship begins to rise.

Not to lift out of the water exactly; more that the water agrees to become lower. The waves flatten, or the ship grows, or the distinction stops mattering. You are looking down now at a sea that has become a great grey map with the folds still in it, and you can see all the places the ship might have gone, tiny roads of light running across the water toward tiny harbors, and none of them are the right one, and all of them are.

Your mother, in the third row, holds up her knitting so you can see how much is done. It is not very much. There is a great deal of horizon still to make. She smiles at you the way she used to when you asked a question she was not going to answer.

The crew has gone quiet again, but a different quiet. Someone hands you a cup of something hot. You drink it. It tastes like the well.

"Captain," someone says, and you turn, and the word for the tall pole surfaces in your mouth all at once, whole and dripping: mast. Mast. You say it out loud and the crew cheers as if you have sighted land.

Then the others come back, rope and rudder and keel and hull and helm and starboard and lee, arriving in small wet flocks, settling onto the things they belong to, and each object on the ship becomes slightly more real as its name lands on it, the way a room becomes more real when someone turns on a lamp. You name everything. It takes a long time and no time at all. When you are done you look up to name the storm and find that it, too, has been waiting to be called, and you understand, with the plain clarity that only comes at the edge of waking, that the storm is your name, that it has been your name this entire time, that you have been captaining yourself through yourself and asking yourself for orders.

The audience in the clouds begins, very softly, to applaud.

The sound of it is rain.

You steer into it.
