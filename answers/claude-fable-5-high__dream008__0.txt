You are at your workplace, and suddenly, you are given a project with an impossible deadline. Your boss is waiting for you to start, and you open your laptop, except it isn't your laptop anymore — it's a typewriter, and the keys are arranged in no alphabet you recognize. You press one anyway. It types a word you didn't choose: SOON.

Your boss nods approvingly, as if this were exactly the right start. "Good," he says. "Only nine hundred pages to go. It's due at three." You look at the clock on the wall. It already says three. It has always said three. The second hand is moving backwards, but slowly, apologetically, like it's embarrassed to be seen doing it.

You start typing faster. The typewriter doesn't produce paper; it produces hallway. Every line you type extends the corridor outside your cubicle by another meter, and you understand, in the way you understand things in dreams without being told, that the project is the building itself. You are writing the office into existence, and everyone in it is waiting inside sentences you haven't finished yet.

Your coworkers begin to gather around your desk. They hold coffee mugs full of sand. One of them — you know her name, you've known it for years, it's gone — leans in and whispers, "Don't write the ending. The last time someone wrote the ending, we all had to go home, and nobody could remember where home was."

The deadline arrives. It arrives physically, through the elevator doors: a tall figure in a gray suit whose face is a calendar page, today's date printed where the eyes should be. It doesn't speak. It just stands at the end of the hallway you've been typing, waiting at the exact point where your last sentence stops.

Your boss puts a hand on your shoulder. His hand is very light, like a bird landing. "You've done this before," he says gently, and you realize he's right. You have done this every night. This is the same project. It is always the same project, and the deadline is never missed and never met — it just walks toward you at the speed you type, so the distance between you stays exactly the same forever.

You stop typing.

The hallway stops growing. The figure in the gray suit stops walking. The clock's second hand stops, mid-apology. For one long moment, the whole office holds still, balanced on the period you haven't typed yet.

And then you do something you've never done in any version of this dream: you stand up, walk down the hallway you wrote, and shake the deadline's hand. Its hand feels like the last page of a book. The calendar face flips, once, to a blank page.

"Done," you say.

Somewhere behind you, your boss starts to applaud, and the sound turns into rain on a window, and the window turns out to be the one beside your bed, and the project, whatever it was, is finished, because you are waking up, and no one who is awake has ever missed it.
