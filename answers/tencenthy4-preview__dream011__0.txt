…written in your own handwriting, but you don’t remember writing them.

The letters lean too far to the right, the way yours do when you’re rushing. You glance up. The lecture hall is the same one you’ve sat in all semester—same scuffed floor, same humming lights—except the windows now open onto a sky that is too blue, too still, as if the world outside has been painted in and not quite finished.

Around you, the other students are already writing. Their pencils move in perfect unison, like windshield wipers in a storm that hasn’t started yet. None of them look up.

You look back down.

**Question 1:** *Name the thing you would do even if you knew you would fail.*

Your throat tightens. This is not on the syllabus. There is no multiple choice, no case study, no formula sheet. The paper smells faintly of rain.

You read on.

**Question 2:** *When did you stop asking for permission?*  
**Question 3:** *What did you love before anyone told you it was impractical?*

Each question seems to breathe, the ink darkening as you look at it. And as you stare, the room begins to rearrange itself. The chalkboard dissolves into a shoreline. The proctor’s podium becomes a plain wooden door. Your classmates blur into silhouettes of possible selves: one in a lab coat, one on a stage, one barefoot in a garden, one sitting exactly where you are now, still deciding.

Your pen feels heavier than it should. Before you can talk yourself out of it, you write: *Music. I would still write music.* The instant the ink hits the page, the fluorescent lights warm into footlights, and a piano appears at the front of the hall, gleaming and entirely out of place.

But the next question stops you.

**Question 4:** *What are you afraid will change if you choose it?*

The possible selves watch you. The lab-coat self looks tired. The stage self looks terrified and wide awake. The garden self looks peaceful, but a little lonely. You realize, with a strange calm, that the exam is not grading your answers. It is grading your willingness to choose one.

You turn to the last page. There is only one question left, and it is the only one that seems to matter.

**Final Question:** *Whose life are you living?*

The proctor steps forward. It is you—ten years older, softer around the eyes, wearing an expression you don’t yet have.

“Time’s almost up,” the older you says. “But in here, time is negotiable.”

You think of the careers you have rehearsed: the safe one, the impressive one, the one your family would understand without needing an explanation. Then you think of the thing you do when no one is watching, the thing that makes you forget to check the clock.

You pick up the pen.

You write: *Mine. Starting now.*
