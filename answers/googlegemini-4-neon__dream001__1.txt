You look up, and the city stretches impossibly high around you—towers of pale concrete and rusted glass connected by a web of swaying skybridges. The air smells faintly of ozone and wet pavement, and the sky is the bruised purple of late dusk, though you have no idea whether it is morning or evening. 

You begin walking briskly, your footsteps echoing against the stone, though your legs feel faintly heavy, as if you’re wading through knee-deep water. You stop a commuter hurrying past in a crisp trench coat to ask for directions. When they turn around, it’s your old high school history teacher, except they’re holding a stack of porcelain plates. 

"You're going the wrong way," they say gently, not sounding surprised to see you at all. "Take the green line before the tide comes in."

Before you can ask what they mean, they vanish into the crowd. Ahead of you, a set of subway stairs descends into the sidewalk, lit by flickering fluorescent tubes. You jog down, checking your watch—somehow, forty-five minutes have already vanished. You only have fifteen left. You also realize, with a sudden jolt of panic, that you aren't carrying a briefcase or notes. Instead, tucked securely under your arm is a potted fern, and you are completely, inexplicably convinced that the entire meeting depends on it.

A train screeches to a halt at the platform, its doors sliding open with a sigh. You step inside, only to find the interior isn't a subway car at all, but a dimly lit, red-carpeted elevator. There are no floor numbers on the panel—just a row of brass buttons labeled with strange symbols and a single glowing switch that reads *SOON*. You press it. 

The doors shut, and the room lurches—not upward, but sideways, picking up speed until the walls rattle. Through a small circular window, you watch the city blur past in a kaleidoscope of neon signs, floating staircases, and giant clock faces whose hands are spinning backward.

With a gentle chime, the motion stops. The doors slide open onto a vast, quiet foyer with marble floors and ceilings lost in mist. A set of tall oak double doors stands slightly ajar just ahead, warm light spilling through the crack. You can hear the low, expectant murmur of voices inside. 

Heart hammering, you straighten your shirt, adjust your grip on the fern, and push the doors open. 

Inside is an endless conference table stretching out into open sky. Dozens of people in sharp suits turn slowly in unison to look at you, their faces soft and out of focus, like photographs left out in the rain. At the far end of the table, someone clears their throat and gestures toward the single empty chair at the center. 

You take a step forward, opening your mouth to apologize for being late—and suddenly the marble floor gives way to soft air, your stomach drops, and a distant, rhythmic beeping begins to echo through the mist, pulling the room apart into streaks of pale morning light.
