Despite the hardships, you decide to mend the carousel.

The town has more urgent problems. The well rope is down to its last few strands. There’s something living in the school gym that knows how to call people by name. Still, on Sunday mornings, you go to the fairground with an oilcan. For ten minutes you work on whatever part of the carousel squeaks loudest. Then you go help haul water.

One morning a girl follows you. She watches you work for a while before pointing to a pale patch on the blue horse’s flank.

“That’s where my dad sat,” she says.

“Wasn’t he a bit big for it?”

“He rode sidesaddle. He had a bad knee.”

She takes the oilcan from you and crawls underneath the platform, where your hands are too big to reach. Between you, you get the old thing turning. There’s no music. You push while she rides, one leg hooked around the brass pole, both feet hanging to the left.

Each time she comes round, she’s a little older. On the fourth turn she has gray in her hair. On the fifth she has her father with her, a young man in a green coat, who lifts one hand to you as they pass.

You want to stop and ask what became of everybody. Instead you put your shoulder to the blue horse’s rump and give them another turn.
