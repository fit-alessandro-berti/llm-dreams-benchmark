...and you almost say no, because that's what you've learned to say. But your mouth is already opening and something else comes out — a small, cracked sound, like a door that hasn't been used in years.

They don't seem to mind. There are five of them, or maybe seven; the number keeps sliding whenever you try to count. One has your grandmother's hands. One is wearing a coat the exact blue of a swimming pool at dusk. They fold around you without touching you, the way starlings turn all at once in the sky.

"We know where it is," says the one with the hands. "We've been keeping it for you."

You walk. The city begins to behave strangely — the crosswalks light up green before you reach them, and the buildings lean in slightly, politely, like people making room at a table. A taxi honks and the honk comes out as three notes of a song you loved at nineteen and forgot entirely. You start to cry and it isn't sad, it's more like a window being washed.

Someone hands you a map. It's warm, like bread. Every street on it is named after something you've lost: the dog, the apartment with the radiator that clanked, the friend you stopped calling for no reason at all, the reason. You expect this to hurt. Instead you notice the streets are all connected. You notice they all lead somewhere.

"Which one do I take?" you ask.

"Any," they say, delighted, as if you've guessed a riddle. "They're the same street. We just gave it a lot of names so it wouldn't get lonely."

And then you understand, with the flat certainty of dreams, that they were never strangers. They're the parts of you that you left in various cities, in various years, standing around in doorways waiting to be collected. The one in the pool-blue coat is the version of you who still believed things would work out. She takes your hand. Her palm is very cold and very alive.

The buildings open like a chest cavity. Inside there's a kitchen, a lamp, a chair with your jacket on it. The smell of onions in butter. Someone is calling you from another room and using the nickname nobody uses anymore.

You turn to thank them but they're already dispersing, becoming pedestrians, becoming the crowd, becoming the ordinary hum of a place where people live. The last thing the one with your grandmother's hands says, over her shoulder, already halfway gone:

"You weren't lost. You were just early."

You go inside. The door doesn't lock behind you. Somewhere far above the dream, your real body turns over in the dark, and your breathing slows, and stays slow.
