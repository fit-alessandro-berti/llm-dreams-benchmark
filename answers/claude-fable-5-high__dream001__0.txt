You find yourself in a vast, unfamiliar city, and you realize you have an important meeting in an hour but no idea where it is. You start to walk faster, as if speed alone could substitute for direction. The streets are wide and clean and utterly wrong — the signs are written in an alphabet you almost recognize, letters that rearrange themselves whenever you look at them directly, settling into words only in the corner of your eye.

You reach into your pocket for your phone and find instead a small brass key, warm as if someone had been holding it for hours. You know, the way you know things in dreams, that the key opens the room where the meeting is. This should be reassuring. It is not, because a key is not an address.

You stop a passerby, a woman carrying an umbrella though the sky is cloudless. "Excuse me, I'm looking for the meeting," you say, and you're embarrassed that you can't be more specific, but she nods as though this were a perfectly complete question. "Everyone is," she says. "It's up." She points with the umbrella, not at any building in particular but at the city itself, and you notice for the first time that the streets slope gently upward in every direction, that the whole city is somehow a hill with you at the bottom of it.

So you climb. The hour is draining away — you can feel it leaving, the way you feel bathwater leave a tub — but the clocks you pass are no help. One has thirteen hours. One has no hands at all, just a face that watches you back. A third simply says LATER.

The buildings grow older as you ascend, brick giving way to stone, stone to something like coral, as if the upper city had been underwater once and never quite dried. You pass a bakery that smells like your grandmother's kitchen, and for one dangerous moment you want to stop, to abandon the meeting, to sit in that smell forever. The baker in the window shakes his head slowly, kindly. Not yet, the gesture says. You have somewhere to be.

Then the street ends at a door. Not a building — just a door, freestanding, in a frame of pale wood, with the city continuing visibly on the other side of it. The brass plate on the door has no number. It has your name, spelled slightly wrong, the way people who love you sometimes misspell it.

The key fits. Of course it fits.

Inside is a single room that could not fit inside the doorframe, long as a train car, with a table down the middle and windows showing not the city but other times of day — morning in one, deep night in the next. And the meeting is already in progress, and everyone at the table turns to look at you, and every face is your own face at a different age. The child you. The one from ten years ago. One much older, with kind lines around the eyes, sitting at the head of the table with the agenda.

"You're not late," the oldest one says, and slides out an empty chair. "You were never going to be late. This meeting doesn't start until you arrive. That's what makes it important."

You sit down. Someone passes you a glass of water that tastes like rain. You look for the papers you were supposed to bring and find your pockets empty now, even the key gone, and the child version of you laughs — not unkindly — and says, "You always think there's something you were supposed to bring. There isn't. There's never been."

Out the windows, the city is quietly rearranging itself, streets folding up like the meeting is the only fixed point and everything else was scaffolding. The oldest you clears their throat, looks down at the agenda, and reads the only item on it aloud.

"Item one," they say. "Wake gently."

And the room fills with light the color of early morning, and the table grows soft beneath your arms, and one by one the others stand and file out through the windows into their separate hours of the day, each pausing to rest a hand briefly on your shoulder as they pass — thirteen hands, warm, unhurried — and by the time the last one leaves you are already somewhere else: eyes closed, in a bed you recognize, in a city you know, with no meetings at all until you decide to open your eyes.
