

You decide to stop speaking mid-sentence.

The room around you grows very still, as if the dream itself is holding its breath. Your friend’s face is still flushed with anger, but now you notice that their eyes are not full of fury—they are full of fear. The important thing you were arguing about is sitting between you on the table, but in the dream it has become a real object: a small glass lantern, cracked down the middle, still flickering with a weak blue flame.

You realize that if either of you grabs it too hard, it will shatter.

So you lower your voice and ask, “What are you afraid will happen if I’m right?”

Your friend hesitates. The walls of the room begin to soften, turning into curtains, then into trees, then into a night sky full of slow-moving stars. Their answer comes quietly, and when you hear it, you understand that the argument was never really about the thing itself. It was about loyalty, about change, about who you are both becoming.

You reach across the table—not to take the lantern, but to hold your hands around it together. The crack begins to glow instead of break.

The dream ends with the two of you sitting under that open sky, no longer opponents, no longer certain who was right, only certain that you do not want to lose each other over a truth that was never meant to be carried alone.
