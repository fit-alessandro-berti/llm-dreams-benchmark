I wake up in a completely different era, with no modern technology around. As I explore my new surroundings, I realize I need to find out what year it is—before I do something that changes history.

The air smells of woodsmoke and damp earth. Cobblestones press up through thin leather shoes I don’t remember putting on. Around me, half-timbered houses lean toward each other like tired old friends. A woman in a long linen dress hangs wet laundry on a line, and a boy drives a small flock of sheep past a blacksmith’s forge. No electricity. No asphalt. No hum of engines. Only the creak of cartwheels and distant bells.

I pat my pockets. Nothing but a folded piece of paper. I open it: blank. But somehow I know it’s meant to be a map—or a message. I realize I need to follow the river. The paper seems to warm when I point it toward the sound of running water.

So I start walking. The village shrinks behind me. The river glitters gray-green under an older sun. A wooden bridge crosses ahead, and on the far side stands a stranger in a long cloak. He is not surprised to see me.

“You’re late,” he says. “The dream gate only opens when the tide is turning. You need to decide—go back, or stay. But if you stay, you must learn the language of this time and never speak of the world you left behind.”

I look at the river. My reflection looks like me, but wearing a face I slightly recognize from an old painting.

“And if I go back?” I ask.

The stranger smiles. “Then you’ll wake up. But you’ll dream of this place every night until you finish what you came here to do.”

I don’t know what I came here to do. But I know I need to cross the bridge. So I do.

And as my foot touches the first plank, the dream deepens—and I remember: I came here to find the boy who I saw driving the sheep. He was holding a scrap of paper that matched mine. I need to find him and ask what he wrote.

The stranger laughs quietly behind me. “Good. Now the dream truly begins.”
