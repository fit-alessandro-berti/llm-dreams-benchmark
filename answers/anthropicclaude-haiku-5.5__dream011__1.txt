The paper is warm under your palms, as if someone has been holding it just before you. The fluorescent lights hum in a key you almost recognize. Your pencil is already in your hand, though you don't remember picking it up, and it has no eraser.

You read the first question, and the letters rearrange themselves while you watch:

*1. Describe the color of Tuesday. Justify your answer using only the smell of wet pennies.*

You glance up. The other students are bent over their desks, scribbling furiously. One of them, a woman with your mother's haircut, is writing in a language made of small circles. Behind her, the clock on the wall has numbers but no hands, and the second hand is a goldfish swimming slowly in a glass bowl mounted above the whiteboard.

You look back down.

*2. Where did you leave the thing you were supposed to bring? Be specific. Your answer will be graded on the emotional accuracy of the hallway.*

The hallway. You know this hallway. It is the one from your childhood home, the one that stretched longer every year, with the door at the end that always opened onto a different season. You feel a tug in your chest, as if someone is asking you to remember something important you set down and never picked back up.

Your pencil begins writing on its own. It writes: *The thing was a key. I left it in the pocket of a coat I wore when I was nine, the blue one with the broken zipper. I forgot it on purpose.*

You stop. You didn't mean to write that. The handwriting is yours, but the sentence feels like it came from someone standing just behind your left shoulder.

A bell rings. It sounds like someone clinking a spoon against a glass of water. The proctor, who is somehow also the lighthouse keeper from the postcard on your fridge, stands and announces:

"Final question. Your career will be determined by the answer. Choose one: A) the job you were told to want, B) the job you actually dreamed of at age eight, or C) the job that doesn't exist yet and will require you to invent its name."

Your heart pounds. The room tilts gently, the way a boat does when someone steps aboard. Outside the window, the sky is folding itself into origami shapes, and a flock of paper cranes lands on the sill, waiting.

You lift your pencil. The graphite is glowing faintly. You hesitate over the page, and the goldfish on the clock turns to look at you, as if it has been waiting all along for your answer.

You begin to write.
