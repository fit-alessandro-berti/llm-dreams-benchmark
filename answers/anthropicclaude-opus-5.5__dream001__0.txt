...walk, because walking seems like the only thing to do. The sidewalks are made of old piano keys, and each step plays a note, so that by the end of the first block you've accidentally hummed half a lullaby your grandmother used to sing. You don't remember her singing it, but your feet do.

You reach into your pocket for your phone to check the address, but instead you pull out a small brass compass. The needle doesn't point north. It points *late*. You know this the way you know things in dreams: completely, and without evidence.

You follow it.

The buildings lean over you like curious giraffes, their windows blinking slowly. A man selling newspapers calls out headlines, but every headline is a question about you: *LOCAL PERSON UNSURE IF THEY LOCKED THE DOOR. WHAT DID THEY MEAN BY THAT COMMENT IN 2014?* You buy one to be polite. It costs a memory of a Tuesday. You don't miss it.

The compass leads you into a hotel lobby that is also, somehow, your elementary school cafeteria. The carpet is the right carpet, the smell of floor wax and ketchup is exactly right, and there's a concierge desk where the lunch line used to be. Behind it stands your third-grade teacher, Mrs. Albright, who hasn't aged a day, though she now has the head of a heron.

"You're here for the meeting," she says. It isn't a question.

"Yes! Where is it?"

"Floor thirteen."

"Is there a thirteenth floor?"

She tilts her long beak toward the elevator. "There is if you're already on it."

The elevator has no buttons, only a single brass plate engraved with the words *THINK OF WHERE YOU'RE GOING.* You try. You really do. But you still don't know what the meeting is *about*, and the harder you think, the more the elevator drifts sideways instead of up, gliding past rooms full of things you'd forgotten: a lost umbrella, a friend you meant to call back, a drawing you abandoned halfway through.

When the doors finally open, you step into a boardroom at the top of a lighthouse. The table is long and glossy, and the ocean churns silver outside the windows. Every chair is filled with a different version of you: you at seven, missing a front tooth; you at sixteen, arms crossed; you at eighty, calm and wrinkled and smiling like you know the punchline to a joke you haven't heard yet.

The eldest one gestures to the empty seat at the head of the table.

"You're right on time," she says.

"I thought I was late. I didn't even know where I was going."

"Nobody does," says the seven-year-old, swinging her legs. "That's the whole agenda."

You sit. Someone slides a folder across the table. It's heavy, important-looking, stamped **URGENT**. You open it with trembling hands.

Inside is a single sheet of paper. On it, in your own handwriting, are the words:

*You can stop running now.*

Outside, the lighthouse beam sweeps across the dark water, and for the first time all night, the city below goes quiet. Somewhere far away, an alarm clock begins to ring. But you stay in your chair a moment longer, because the meeting isn't over, and for once, you'd like to hear how it ends.
