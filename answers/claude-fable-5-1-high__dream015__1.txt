You find yourself in a post-apocalyptic world, with resources scarce and dangers everywhere. Despite the hardships, you decide to open a library.

Not a real one, at first. Just a card table set up in the mouth of a collapsed parking garage, with eleven books on it that you found in the glovebox of a bus. A field guide to North American birds. A paperback whose cover has been burned off so that it begins on page 37. A hymnal. Something in Portuguese. You arrange them by color because that seems to be the rule here.

The sky is the color of a dirty coin and it has been that way, you understand, for a long time. Nobody remembers the sun exactly, but everyone agrees it was somewhere to the left.

Your first patron is a woman wearing a raincoat made of stitched-together shower curtains. She reads the hymnal standing up, mouthing the words, and when she is done she hands you a fistful of teeth, which is the currency now. You don't want to take them but she insists, and you notice that they are small, milk teeth, and she says, "They're mine. From before." You put them in a coffee tin. The tin is already half full, and you don't remember filling it.

Word spreads. This is what surprises you, in the dream: how quickly word spreads in a world with no wires. People arrive from the direction where the sun used to be, dragging carts, carrying children, carrying nothing. They come to the card table and they read. Some read for hours. A man with one arm reads the Portuguese book cover to cover and weeps, and when you ask him if he speaks Portuguese he says no, no, he doesn't, that's why.

You begin to understand that the library has to grow. So you go out at night, into the ruins, with a headlamp that runs on something you don't want to think about, and you find books. They are everywhere once you start looking. They are in the ovens of restaurants. They are in the trunks of cars that have grown into the pavement. There is a hospital where every drawer of every filing cabinet is full of the same book, hundreds of copies, and the title is your own name, and you do not open one. You leave that hospital very quickly. You do not go back.

The parking garage fills. Shelves happen. You don't build them; they are simply there one morning, made of highway signs and church pews and the ribs of something enormous. The shower-curtain woman becomes your assistant. She never gives her name and you never ask, and in the dream this is a form of intimacy, not distance. She stamps the books with a stamp she made out of a potato. The stamp says DUE, with no date, because there are no dates anymore. Everything is due.

Dangers everywhere, the dream had promised, and it keeps its promise. There are the dogs, who have learned to walk on two legs to seem less threatening, which is much more threatening. There are the tax collectors, who come from a city that doesn't exist and demand a percentage of the teeth. There is the weather, which sometimes arrives in the form of a very tall man who stands at the edge of the lot and just looks, and while he looks nothing can be read; the letters slide off the pages and pool on the floor like mercury and you have to wait for him to leave before you can sweep them back in.

But mostly the danger is quieter. It is the way people go quiet in the stacks. It is the boy who checks out the bird guide every single day and returns it every evening and one day says to you, not looking up, "There aren't any of these anymore, are there," and you say, "Some," and he says, "You're a librarian. You're not supposed to lie," and you realize that this is true, that it is somehow the one law left.

So you stop lying. You tell people what the books are and what they aren't. You tell them the hymnal won't bring anyone back. You tell them the burned paperback will never explain what happened in the first thirty-six pages. And this, strangely, makes them come more, not less.

One night you are alone in the garage, and you hear the sound of a page turning, very far back in the stacks, where you haven't shelved anything yet. You take the headlamp and you walk. The shelves go farther than the building does. You pass sections you don't remember making: a section of letters that were never sent, a section of instruction manuals for machines that were never built, a section of last words, alphabetized by the speaker. The page turns again, closer.

You find the reader at the very back. It is the world. It is hard to describe exactly how you know this, but you know it the way you know things in dreams: the thing sitting cross-legged on the concrete with a book open in its lap is the world itself, the whole ruined thing, small now, hunched, reading by the light that leaks from between its own fingers. It looks up at you. It has the face of everyone who has come to the card table.

"I'm not finished," it says, apologetically.

"That's all right," you say. "Nothing's due."

You leave it there. You walk back through the impossible stacks, past the last words and the unsent letters, and by the time you reach the card table at the front, the sky has changed. Not much. It is still the color of a dirty coin. But there is a smear of something on the left side, low, where everyone said the sun used to be, and the shower-curtain woman is standing in the doorway with her potato stamp, looking at it, and she says, without turning around, "Well. Look what got checked out."

And you go and stand beside her, and you look, and you wake up before you can decide whether it is coming or going.
