> You are about to give a speech to a large audience. As you step onto the stage, you see the crowd looking expectantly at you, and you begin to speak—only the words are not words. They leave your mouth as a drift of glowing seeds, and the air fills with a low, sweet humming, like a cello being bowed by the wind.
>
> You glance down at your notes, but the paper has become a map of a city that does not exist yet, its streets made of your own handwriting. The microphone leans toward you, curious, and when you breathe into it the sound comes back as rain.
>
> The audience does not seem to mind. They lean forward, faces softening, as if they recognize the shape of everything you are failing to say. In the front row, a child who is also your grandmother smiles and holds up a seashell; inside it you hear the speech you were meant to give, and it has already forgiven you.
>
> You stop trying. You let the glowing seeds settle into the curtains, into the listeners’ hair, into the dark warm soil beneath the stage. The theater becomes a garden. The crowd becomes a forest, and the forest whispers, “Go on.”
>
> So you tell them the only true thing you know: “I don’t have the ending yet.” And the forest answers, rustling, “Neither do we.”
>
> Then the lights bloom into sunrise, the stage folds itself into a letter, and you wake with the taste of salt and paper on your tongue, certain you have said enough.
