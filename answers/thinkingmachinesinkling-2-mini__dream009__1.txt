You wake with your fingers numb, shaped into small songs of ash and wool. The air smells of wet linen, cedar, and distant smoke. No phone, no clock, no wires—only grass trembling in a wind that feels almost deliberate. You drag yourself from the clearing and see a ridge of blue hills, a ribbon of reed, and a road that turns not into asphalt but into older paths. You realize you need to find water before the heat closes your throat, and you need to find people before something more uncertain does—not monsters, exactly, but the kind of attention that makes small footsteps sound louder than music.

So you walk slowly, counting steps in the language of your body: breath, ache, grain, cloth. In a hollow oak you find a clay cup, cracked but holding dark water; beside it lies a woven mat, a scrap of wool the color of dawn, and a strip of bark etched with marks you don’t understand. You drink, kneel, and feel the dream’s strangeness settle into something negotiable. By the next hour, a figure appears at the edge of the reed bed—not fully human, not fully animal, a being made of weathered wood and soft featherlight moss—giving you a lantern of amber light and saying, in a voice like leaves being turned over, “All begin with enough: enough to stay, enough to ask.” You answer not with a question but with a gesture: hands open, palms up, as if to say the world can know your name now. By nightfall, the map of your survival is not drawn on a screen but scratched into memory—where the spring hides, which stones are warm, which voices promise food, and which ones only tease.

I’ll stop there as a dream rather than a nonfiction quest: you have crossed a border into another age, and the next thing you need is not a device but a voice that can recognize you—and then a path that can carry you toward it.
