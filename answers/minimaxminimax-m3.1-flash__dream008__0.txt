The dream starts with a smell — burnt coffee and dry-erase marker — and a blue cube on your desk that has no keyhole, no drawer, no purpose. It's just a cube. It was there this morning. It will be there tomorrow. You don't remember buying it, but you know its weight.

Your boss is standing in the doorway with a folder under one arm, and she is already mid-sentence, which means the conversation started before you noticed it, which means you are already late.

"Great," she says. "You're up. I won't belabor it. We need the full audit by nine-fifteen."

She says the date along with the time, the way people say a flight number, and you hear it clearly, but your ears slide off it. Nine-fifteen. Sure. Nine-fifteen.

"What day is it?" you ask.

"Tuesday."

"Which Tuesday?"

She looks at you with the specific patience of someone who has explained this many times, and she says, "The one after this one. Listen — I know it's tight. That's why I wanted you."

There it is. The impossible thing, stated plainly, unremarked upon. Deadlines in dreams are never called impossible. They're called *tight*.

"Okay," you say, because what else. You take the folder. It's heavier than paper has any right to be, and warm, and somewhere inside it, something is faintly ticking.

The office has grown since this morning. You can feel it — the room is longer now, the cubicle rows extending back into a haze of fluorescent light, each one occupied. You look as you pass, and in every cube there's someone. Every one of them is you. Different versions, all wearing your face slightly wrong: one has your hands, one has your posture, one is asleep at the keyboard with their cheek on the keys leaving a little wet print on the space bar.

None of them look up. That's the mercy in it, and also the horror.

You find your desk. On it: the blue cube, a stack of folders with dates scrawled on the spines, and a small laminated card, the kind you'd find in a meeting room, reading:

**DEADLINE: 9:15 — TOMORROW**
**STATUS: ON TRACK**
**OWNER: YOU**

You've seen this card before. That's what makes your stomach drop — not the deadline, but the familiarity. You've held this card before. You signed it. Twice.

Somewhere past the third row of cubicles, a phone starts ringing. It rings the way dreams do — not a ring, but a *ringing*, a sound with a shape, an event in the world rather than a noise in it. Everyone lifts their head at once. Even the sleepers.

"Boss," someone calls out — one of you, from a row deep in the mist. "Is it — should I — "

"Not yet," she calls back, and there's something almost tender in it. "Let him start."

And she leaves. That's the part that undoes you. She leaves, because the dream has decided the pressure is now *yours*, and it can no longer help you.

You open the folder.

Inside: a contract, fourteen pages, in a font you've never seen but instantly understand. Page one has a diagram. Page two has the same diagram, larger. Page three has the diagram again, but now the diagram is of the diagram, and there's a note in the margin in your handwriting: *this is the part where they tell you it's realistic.*

The clock on the wall reads 4:58. You watch it. It reads 4:58. You watch longer and it reads 4:59, and you think, okay, time is moving, fine — and then you blink, and it reads 4:58.

Something loosens in your chest: *the deadline cannot arrive if time is standing still.*

But it's not standing still. You can feel it draining. There's a sound like sand running very slowly through a hourglass the size of the building, and every so often the light in the office dims a half-shade, the way it does at the end of a day that is somehow also a month.

You could do it. Some version of you could do it. You can see it in the rows behind you — the versions that have already done it, sitting there with their finished work, calm, waiting for a deadline that will never come so they can never be found out.

And you understand, all at once, what the dream actually is. It isn't about the deadline. It isn't about the audit. It's about the knowledge — buried somewhere in you, the thing your boss was really saying in the doorway, the thing the card is saying in its neat little laminate — that **you have done this before, and it will not be enough, and someone will be kind about it anyway.**

The blue cube on the desk. You pick it up. It's warm. Turned over in your hands, it's still a cube, but on the underside, in tiny scratched letters, someone has carved:

*4:58 — the meeting moved to 8:40 — ask what changed.*

You look up. The door to the conference room is open. Light spills out, warm and yellow, the old kind, the kind with a table in it and chairs around the table and a few people already seated, waiting for you, not urgently, just *waiting*, the way people wait for the meeting to start.

Outside the windows, the sky is doing something impossible and gentle: it's the last hour of the afternoon, stretched out, golden, held, like a hand cupped around a candle.

The sand sound in the walls begins, very slightly, to run the other way.

You've got maybe a day you can't spend. You've got a deadline that can't be met. You've got a folder with a diagram inside it about a diagram.

But you have a cube. And you have a question.

"Hey," you say out loud, to the room, to the rows of sleeping selves, to the boss who's already gone. "Before I start — what changed?"

And in the dream, for the first time, something answers that isn't a rule.

---

You wake at 4:58. The room is the ordinary kind of ordinary. There's a folder on your desk you don't remember opening. There's a task in your tracker with a due date that hasn't been set yet, and the little space next to it is a small white square, waiting for a number.

Your hands are warm.
