Mid-sentence, you simply close your mouth, and something strange happens — the words you didn't say come out anyway, but as visible things, small silver fish swimming in the air between you. Your friend reaches out and catches one, and when he looks at it, his face changes. It's your face now. No — it's his face when he was ten years old, the way he looked the summer you both fell out of the tree behind his house.

The room you were arguing in is no longer a room. The walls have receded into a gray fog, and the floor is the surface of a lake that holds your weight but ripples with every gesture you make. The thing you were arguing about — you can't remember what it was, exactly, only that it mattered enormously a moment ago — is now a small burning house floating on the water between the two of you. You both look at it. Neither of you moves to put it out.

"I didn't mean it," your friend says, except his voice comes from behind you, and when you turn, he's sitting on the porch of the burning house, unbothered by the flames, eating an apple. The fire doesn't seem to hurt the house. It doesn't even look damaged. It just keeps burning, patiently, like it's been burning for years and will keep burning long after you wake up.

You want to apologize. You open your mouth and a stream of sand pours out, hissing when it hits the water. Your friend laughs — his real laugh, the one from before the argument, before whatever it was came between you — and he tosses you the apple. When you catch it, it's warm, like a stone left in the sun.

The fog thickens. The burning house dims. The last thing you see is your friend waving, already far away, and the strange certainty that the argument is over — not resolved, just over — settles over you like a blanket. The lake rises to meet you, warm as bathwater, and you sink into it without fear, and somewhere far above, someone you love is still eating the apple, and the house is still burning, patiently, waiting for morning.
