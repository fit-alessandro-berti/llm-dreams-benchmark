Your pocket is deeper than it should be. Your hand keeps going — past your knee, past the floor of the stage — and when you finally pull something out, it's not paper at all. It's a small, crumpled drawing you made when you were seven. A stick figure standing on a box, with huge yellow suns for an audience.

You look up, embarrassed, sure the crowd is waiting for you to fail.

But no one is impatient. Hundreds of faces are turned toward you, quiet and expectant, and the longer you look, the more familiar they become. There's your third-grade teacher in the front row. Your best friend from summer camp, still missing his front tooth. Your grandmother, your neighbor, a cashier who was kind to you once on a bad day. Row after row of everyone who has ever listened to you, really listened.

You step up to the microphone. It bends toward you like a sunflower.

"I'm nervous," is all you manage to say.

Your voice doesn't come out small. It fills the theater, echoes off the balcony, comes back to you warm and steady, like someone else is speaking with you.

And suddenly you remember why you came. You didn't have a perfect speech prepared. You had one true thing you wanted to say, and you'd been afraid it wasn't enough.

So you say it.

You talk about feeling unready. About all the times you waited to feel confident before beginning. About how life doesn't wait for that feeling — it just hands you the microphone anyway.

As you speak, the stage begins to change. The heavy red curtains turn to morning curtains in a kitchen. The spotlights soften into sunlight through trees. The drawing in your hand unfolds and unfolds until it's large enough for the whole audience to see, and everyone nods like they have one just like it tucked away somewhere too.

When you finish, there's no thunderous applause. Just a deep, companionable silence. Then, one by one, the people in the audience stand — not to clap, but to step onto the stage with you, until it's crowded and laughing and no longer a stage at all, just a place where nobody has to perform.

The light gets brighter and brighter, until you have to close your eyes...

And you wake up with your heart beating fast, the sentence you needed still clear in your mind.
