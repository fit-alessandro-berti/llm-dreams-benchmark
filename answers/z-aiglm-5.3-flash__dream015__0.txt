…you decide to plant the last apple seed.

It's been in your coat pocket since before you can remember — which, in dreams, is a very long time. You kneel in the cracked asphalt of what used to be a highway and press it into the earth with your thumb. The ground is warm, like something sleeping just beneath the surface.

You don't water it. There is no water. But you sit beside it anyway, because you decided to, and in dreams your decisions carry the weight of law.

The tree grows while you blink.

One moment there is nothing; the next, pale blossoms sway over the ruins, impossibly loud with bees. The scent drifts down into collapsed subway tunnels and boarded-up malls and stairwells where people have been hiding from something no one speaks about. Doors open. Footsteps on glass and gravel — hesitant, then hurrying.

They come out squinting into the light: a woman using a crowbar as a walking stick, two children in bicycle helmets, an old man who salutes the tree like a soldier. Nobody speaks. In dreams, you all understand each other anyway.

Someone begins clearing rubble from the roots. Someone finds a working faucet, and water sputters out — rust-red, then clear. You dig with your hands and notice your palms are soft, and you think: *I must not be a survivor. I must be something else — a gardener, maybe, or a memory the world is having.*

Night falls all at once, the way it does in dreams. But the blossoms glow faintly, so the people stay, trading stories that keep changing shape without anyone minding. The old man says he remembers rain. The children say they remember *you*.

Once, at the edge of the blossom-glow, you see the thing they've all been hiding from — a tall wrongness moving between the broken buildings. It stops at the border of the light. It sniffs the air. Then it sits down the way a lost dog does, and no one invites it in, but no one is afraid anymore, either.

And then the dream-knowledge arrives from nowhere: the seed was never the last one. You reach into your pocket and find it full — more seeds than the pocket should hold, spilling over your fingers like small moons. Enough for every highway. Enough for every ruin.

You wake with your hand closed, fisted around nothing.

But when you open it, you could swear there's dirt under your fingernails.
