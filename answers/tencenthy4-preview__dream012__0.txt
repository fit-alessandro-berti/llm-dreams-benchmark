You are the captain of a ship in the middle of a turbulent sea. Your crew looks to you for guidance as the storm worsens, and you realize, with the weightless certainty of dreams, that the storm is not happening to the ship at all—it is happening to you.

The wheel beneath your hands is warm and familiar, the same shape as the handlebars of your first bicycle. On deck, the crew waits, their oilskins gleaming with rain, but their faces keep slipping: the first mate has your mother’s mouth for a moment, then your oldest friend’s eyes, then your own, younger and unlined. They are not strangers. They never were. They are every version of you that ever felt lost at sea, and they are asking, silently, what you intend to do.

You do not shout over the wind. Instead, you do the only thing that makes sense in a dream: you name the waves. You call the towering one *Grief*, the following hiss *Regret*, the cold spray *Doubt*—and as each name leaves your lips, the sea grows a fraction calmer, as if the water is relieved simply to be known.

Then you turn the wheel hard to starboard, straight into the largest wave, a wall of green glass that should swallow the ship whole. The crew does not flinch. They trust you, because you are them. The ship lifts, tips, and plunges—

—through.

On the other side is the eye of the storm: a perfect circle of stillness ringed by distant lightning. In its center floats a small lantern, identical to the one that used to burn in the hallway of your childhood home. You understand, without words, that you do not need to outrun the storm. You only need to carry the light.

You release the wheel. The ship sails itself now, gliding across the glassy water toward the lantern, toward the dawn leaking through the clouds. The crew fades into gulls, into foam, into memory. The last thing you see before the dream lets go is your own hands, no longer gripping—only open.

You wake in the dark, the sound of real rain on the window, your heart thudding like a hull against water. The storm is gone. But your sheets are salt-damp, your palms remember the wheel, and somewhere under your ribs you can still feel the lantern burning—steady, unafraid, yours to steer by.
