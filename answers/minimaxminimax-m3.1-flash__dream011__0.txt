The questions are not on the paper.

They hover in the air above your desk, each one glowing faintly blue. They rearrange themselves whenever you try to focus.

**What are you afraid of becoming?**

**Who will notice if you fail?**

**What will you do when success no longer excites you?**

The other students begin writing furiously. Their pens scratch like insects trapped beneath glass. No one looks up. No one breathes. The clock on the wall has no hands; instead, a thin green line slowly drains from left to right.

You look down at your exam paper. It is blank except for one line printed at the bottom:

**This exam has no correct answers. It has only consequences.**

A hand lands on your shoulder. The invigilator stands behind you, but her face is your own. She is older, dressed in clothes you have never seen, and she smiles with one side of her mouth.

“You were always going to ask what you’re supposed to do,” she says.

“I don’t know the questions.”

“That isn’t true.” She leans close and whispers, “You know exactly what they’re asking.”

Around you, the floating questions begin to darken. The words blur, multiply, fill the ceiling like storm clouds. Some are questions. Some are names. Some are dates. Others are images: a cramped apartment, a hospital corridor, a handshake, an empty stage, a child asleep in the back seat of a car.

With a soft click, the green line on the clock reaches the end.

“Now,” your double says, “choose the future you can live with.”

But your hands remain empty. No pen has been given to you.

Then you notice a pen lying at the edge of the desk. When you pick it up, it is warm, as though someone has been holding it for years.

You begin to write.

At first, only a single word appears: **No.**

You write it again. Then again. The paper fills with refusals:

No to the career they chose for you.

No to the life you call temporary.

No to every version of yourself that was made to be useful but never allowed to want anything.

The other students stop writing. Above them, their questions shatter into sparks. The room flickers. For a moment, you see through the walls: thousands of desks in thousands of rooms, all filled with people taking exams they never prepared for, each one being quietly asked to decide who they are allowed to become.

Your double watches as the pen moves across the page.

Finally, beneath all the refusals, you write one sentence:

**I will choose the life that feels like mine, even if I have to build it from nothing.**

The bell rings.

This time, it is your voice that answers.

When you open your eyes, you are still holding the pen. Your exam paper is blank, but your palm is aching, and there are ink marks on your fingers.

For the rest of your life, you carry those stains with you. They are not proof that you passed.

They are proof that you finally stopped waiting for someone else to mark the answer.
