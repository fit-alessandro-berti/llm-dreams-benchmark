You are about to give a speech to a large audience. As you step onto the stage, you see the crowd looking expectantly at you, and you begin to speak, but instead of words, a fine stream of sand pours from your mouth.

It patters across the microphone and collects around your shoes. No one reacts. The people in the first row lean forward, smiling politely, as if waiting for you to get to the important part. You try again. More sand spills out, warm and glittering, and among the grains you notice tiny chairs, tiny lamps, tiny white houses with blue roofs. They tumble over the edge of the stage and arrange themselves into a town.

The audience begins taking notes.

You look down at the cards in your hand, but every card bears the same sentence: TELL THEM WHAT HAPPENED TO THE MOON. You cannot remember anything happening to the moon. Above the auditorium, however, the ceiling has vanished. A pale afternoon sky arches over the crowd, and the moon hangs directly overhead, enormous and close, with a dark wooden door set into its surface.

You point to it, hoping the gesture will count as an explanation.

At once, everyone turns to look at you instead.

“Go on,” says a woman in the front row. She has your mother’s voice, though her face is hidden behind a bouquet of clocks. Each clock shows a different time. Their ticking grows louder until it becomes applause.

You bow automatically. When you straighten, the stage has lengthened into a narrow pier. The lectern is now a rusted mailbox, the microphone a black fishing rod, and the audience sits in neat rows beneath the surface of a dark green sea. Their programs drift like silver fish between their hands. Far below, they continue to wait.

You understand that the speech must be delivered from the end of the pier. With each step, the planks behind you loosen and float upward into the sky. You dare not turn around, but you can hear them assembling themselves overhead into a staircase. The little town made from your sand follows alongside you on the water. Lights come on in its windows one by one.

At the end of the pier sits a child in a red coat, dangling her bare feet above the sea. She is holding the missing moon in her lap. It is no larger than a dinner plate now, and she is polishing it with the sleeve of her coat.

“You’re late,” she says.

You ask whether she knows what you are supposed to tell them. This time your voice comes out as a flock of small black birds. They circle her head and settle along the edge of the moon.

“Of course,” she says. “But you made me promise not to remind you.”

She opens the wooden door in the moon. Inside is the room where you grew up, except that rain is falling upward from the carpet and disappearing into the ceiling. On the bed lies a sleeping figure with its back to you. You know without seeing the face that it is also you, younger by many years, dreaming this exact moment.

The audience under the sea begins stamping its feet. The water trembles. Cracks of white light race across its surface.

“They don’t want a speech,” the child says. “They want you to wake that one.”

You reach through the little door. Your arm stretches impossibly far into the bedroom, past the upward rain and the wavering shadows, until your fingers touch the sleeper’s shoulder. The moment you do, the figure turns over.

It has no face. In the smooth place where its features should be, you see the auditorium reflected: the stage, the waiting crowd, and yourself standing at the microphone with your mouth open. Behind your reflected self, something tall is climbing out from between the curtains.

You pull your hand back. The door in the moon slams shut, scattering the black birds. The child drops the moon into the sea.

For an instant, everything is dark.

Then the moon shines beneath the water, illuminating the audience from below. You recognize every face. They are all people you have passed without noticing: passengers glimpsed through train windows, neighbors behind curtains, strangers in old photographs, a man who once held a door for you in the rain. In the very last row, you see the faceless sleeper sitting perfectly still.

You finally remember the speech.

You step off the pier.

Instead of falling, you land on the ceiling of the auditorium. The crowd is now above you, suspended in their seats, and the stage hangs like a chandelier. Your cards flutter down around you. For the first time their messages are different. Each contains a single forgotten thing: the smell of wet pavement outside your first school, the name of a toy you buried in a garden, the last sentence someone said before you never saw them again.

You read the cards aloud. Your voice has returned, but every memory you speak extinguishes one of the lights in the little town. Windows go dark, streets vanish, houses dissolve back into sand. The audience listens without moving. Some are crying upward, their tears rising past you into the open sky.

Only one card remains. It is blank.

The faceless sleeper stands. It raises one hand and removes its blank face like a mask. Beneath it is the dark wooden door.

You know that whatever is behind the door is the final sentence, and that saying it will end the dream. You also know the audience has been waiting all their lives to hear it.

You open your mouth.

From somewhere beyond the auditorium comes the sound of an alarm clock, faint at first, then thunderous. The clocks in the woman’s bouquet all stop at once. The sea rushes upward. The child in the red coat waves to you from the moonlit depths.

“Don’t forget this time,” she calls.

The last blank card folds itself into a white bird and flies through the door before it closes. You wake with your hand raised, the final sentence already fading, and a single grain of warm sand pressed into your palm.
