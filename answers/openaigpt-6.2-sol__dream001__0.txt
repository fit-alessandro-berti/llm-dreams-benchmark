On every corner stands a clock. All of them say you have an hour, though you’ve been walking long enough for the bakeries to open and close. At a tram stop, you find a map with a red dot that says YOU WERE HERE. While you watch, the dot moves one street over.

“Excuse me,” you say to a woman watering the pavement. “I’m looking for a meeting.”

“Oh, good,” she says. “I was starting to think you’d forgotten.”

She sets down her watering can and leads you through a narrow door between two buildings. On the other side is the kitchen from your childhood, right down to the drawer you have to lift a little to open. Rain taps against the screen door. There are two cups on the table.

In one of the chairs sits a child with your face, peeling an orange. They’ve managed to get the rind off in one long spiral.

“Am I late?” you ask.

The child shakes their head and pushes half the orange toward you.

“I wanted to ask you something,” they say. “Did we ever get a dog?”

You’d been bracing for a much harder question. You sit down and tell them about the dog: how she was afraid of her own hiccups, how she used to bring a shoe to the door whenever someone came home. The child wants to know which shoe, and you find you remember. Always the left one, though you never figured out why.

When you wake, you can’t remember the dog’s name. From the foot of the bed comes a long, impatient sigh.
