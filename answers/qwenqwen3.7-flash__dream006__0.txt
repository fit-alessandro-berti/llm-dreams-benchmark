The audience leans forward, their faces softening at the edges—some wear masks of polished driftwood, others hold pocket watches that tick backward. A woman in the third row stands, but she’s taller now, her shoulders brushing a chandelier that wasn’t there a moment ago. She smiles, and the thread from your mouth knots itself into the frame of a door hanging in midair.

You reach for it. The stage dissolves into damp moss beneath your shoes. The air smells like petrichor and pressed flowers. When you step through the doorway, you’re standing in a corridor where the walls breathe gently, lined with arched windows that look out onto seasons changing in reverse. Autumn gold melts into spring green; snow un-falls upward into clouds. At the end of the hall sits a stool made of stacked vinyl records. You climb onto it because it feels familiar, though you’ve never played them before.

A voice echoes from nowhere and everywhere: *“What did you come here to say?”* 

You open your mouth, and this time it’s not threads. It’s water. Clear, cool, and heavy. It pools at your feet, then rises in a slow spiral, forming shapes: a key turning in empty space, a bridge collapsing and rebuilding itself, your mother’s hands shaping dough. The water doesn’t overwhelm you. It listens. In dreams, sincerity has weight, and weight has buoyancy.

The pool settles. Its surface becomes a mirror reflecting a room you haven’t seen yet—but remember anyway. Bookshelves bowing under paperbacks, a teacup sweating condensation, a window cracked open to let in night birds. You realize you weren’t brought here to perform. You were brought here to practice being heard by yourself.

A knock sounds three times. Gentle. Certain. The water drains silently, leaving only faint trails of silt that spell out two words in whatever alphabet your sleeping mind trusts today: *w a k e  n o w.*

Your eyes open. Morning light falls across your pillow, dust motes hovering like tiny constellations. For a long moment, you don’t move. Just listen to the quiet hum behind your ribs, where the silver threads are still knotting themselves into something steady, something yours. And you smile, knowing the speech isn’t over—it’s just learning how to live in the daylight.
