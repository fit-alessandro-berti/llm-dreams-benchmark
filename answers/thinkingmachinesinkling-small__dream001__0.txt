You start to run, but the pavement beneath your feet begins to glow, and the streets rearrange themselves like pages in a book—what was a boulevard of glass towers becomes a narrow canal lined with floating lanterns, then a staircase of moss-covered stone that spirals upward into a sky the color of a forgotten email. You pull out your phone to check the address, but the screen shows only a clock ticking backward, each second draining into a pool of silver ink at the bottom of the display, and the map has been replaced by a photograph of your own childhood bedroom.

You ask a woman standing at a crosswalk for directions, but she turns her head too slowly, and her face is a collage of all the people you’ve ever met; she tells you, in a voice like rustling paper, that the meeting is “inside the sound of rain,” and points to an alley that keeps stretching the longer you look at it. You follow her finger into a tunnel of neon flowers, each petal humming with the frequency of an unread message. The air smells of old books and electricity.

At the end of the tunnel, there is no door—only a mirror. You realize with a jolt that you are already late, but “late” in this city means nothing, because time here is a building with many doors. You open the mirror (it opens like a mouth, or perhaps you simply consent), and instead of stepping through, you feel yourself unfold: the unfamiliar city was never outside you at all. The meeting was always happening inside your own awareness, and the important thing you were searching for was simply the permission to wake up—just as the alarm begins to sound, thin and distant, leaving you with that perfect, unresolvable dream-feeling of having almost understood something beautiful.

You wake with the taste of silver ink on your tongue, and for a moment, you still don’t know where the meeting was. Then you check your clock: you have plenty of time, and the city outside your window is ordinary, and somehow that is enough.
