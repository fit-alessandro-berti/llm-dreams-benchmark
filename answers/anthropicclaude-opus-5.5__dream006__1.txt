...speak, but the words come out as birds.

Small ones at first: sparrows, finches, a nervous wren that circles the podium twice before settling on the microphone. The audience doesn't seem surprised. A woman in the third row nods thoughtfully and writes something down. Someone near the back murmurs, *"Excellent point."*

You try again, more carefully this time, and a heron unfolds from your mouth, grey and enormous, and stalks slowly down the center aisle. Applause ripples through the room.

You glance at your notes for help, but the index cards are blank except for one, which says in your grandmother's handwriting: *You already know this part.* The strange thing is, you do. You've always known it. You just can't remember what "it" is.

The stage has gotten longer. The podium is now very far away, though you haven't moved, and the floorboards smell like the hallway of the school you attended when you were seven. The crowd has changed, too. They're all people you recognize: your old math teacher, a barista from a café you visited once, a childhood friend whose face keeps shifting just as you try to focus on it. They're all waiting. Nobody is impatient. That's somehow worse.

You realize you're not wearing shoes. Then you realize it doesn't matter, because the floor has become shallow, warm water, and the audience is sitting in rowboats now, drifting gently, their faces lit by the paper lanterns that the birds have begun carrying overhead.

"The thing is," you finally say, and this time the words come out as words, clear and ringing, "the thing is—"

Everyone leans forward. The heron pauses mid-step. The lanterns stop swaying.

And in that perfect, expectant silence, you understand the whole speech at once. It's beautiful. It's simple. It explains everything: why the birds, why the water, why your grandmother's handwriting, why you were always so afraid of this moment when it was never anything to fear at all.

You open your mouth to say it—

—and wake up, with the shape of the words still dissolving on your tongue, and the faint, lingering sense that somewhere, a room full of people in rowboats is still smiling, still waiting, and not minding at all.
