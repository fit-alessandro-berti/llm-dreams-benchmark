You plant your boots against the slant of the deck and reach for the wheel. The brass is warm, almost feverish. Beyond the bow, the waves rise so high that the lightning seems to come from under the sea.

“Clip on!” you shout. “Look after the hand beside you.”

The youngest deckhand points to port. A light is moving through the rain: three slow flashes, then two quick ones. You know that signal. When you were small, your father used to blink the kitchen lamp that way when it was time to come in.

The house was sold years ago. Still, for one long second, you turn the wheel toward it.

“Captain?”

Beside you stands Mara, the mate, rain running off the end of her nose. She doesn't ask what you saw. She puts one hand on the wheel and waits until you meet her eyes.

“Take her,” you say. “My hands are shaking.”

“So are mine,” she says. “Leave yours where they are.”

Together you bring the bow round to meet the next wave. It breaks over you with the weight of a falling roof. For a moment there is nothing but green water. Then the deck rolls up beneath your feet, the scuppers gush, and somewhere aft a man begins to laugh in sheer surprise at finding himself still aboard.

By dawn, the sea is merely a sea again. The cook comes up with a dented pot of tea. You drink yours sitting on the wet deck beside Mara, too tired to mind the salt on the rim.

Far off to port, the little light flashes once more. This time you lift your cup to it and let it go.
