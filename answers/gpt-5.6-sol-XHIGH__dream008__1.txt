You are at your workplace, and suddenly, you are given a project with an impossible deadline. Your boss is waiting for you to start, and you realize that the deadline is not written on the brief. It is standing beside him.

It is a tall, narrow figure in a gray suit, with a clock where its face should be. The clock has only one hand. It points straight at you.

“Well?” your boss says.

You open the project folder. Inside is a blueprint of the office building, except the building on the page is much larger than the one around you. It has hundreds of floors, some upside down, some folded into one another like the compartments of a paper lantern. A red circle has been drawn around a room at the very bottom. Your task, according to the first page, is to bring that room upstairs before noon.

You look at the wall clock. It is eleven fifty-nine.

Everyone in the office has stopped working. They are turned toward you, perfectly still, their hands suspended over keyboards and coffee cups. Your boss clears his throat. The deadline takes one silent step closer.

You decide to begin by opening the elevator. The doors part at once, but there are no buttons inside, only a small desk with your nameplate on it. A telephone rings on the desk. When you answer, your own voice whispers, “Whatever you do, don’t take the stairs.” Then the line goes dead, and a new button appears on the elevator wall: BOTTOM.

You press it.

The elevator rises.

Through its glass walls, you see floor after floor drifting downward. On one floor, people are having a birthday party for someone who has not been born. On another, rows of employees shovel envelopes into a furnace while snow piles up around their ankles. On another, your childhood bedroom has been recreated between the photocopier and the supply closet. Your old night-light glows on the carpet. Someone is asleep in your bed, facing the wall.

The elevator stops so abruptly that the telephone leaps off the desk.

Outside is a corridor flooded to the ceiling with blue light. You step out and discover that the light is water, though you can breathe it. Papers swim past like pale fish. Staplers crawl along the walls. Far away, a printer repeats your name in a patient mechanical voice.

You follow it to a door marked 11:59. Behind the door is the room from the blueprint: a tiny windowless office containing one chair and one red folder. The folder is warm when you pick it up. On its cover, in your handwriting, are the words PROJECT COMPLETE.

Relief washes through you, but when you open it, you find only a photograph. It shows you standing in your boss’s office, holding the same photograph. Behind you, the deadline is much closer than you remember. You turn the picture over. There is a note:

YOU FORGOT THE ROOM.

The walls groan.

You remember that you were not sent to retrieve the folder. You were sent to bring the room upstairs.

There are no hinges, no handles, nothing you can carry. In panic, you begin pulling at the wallpaper. It comes away in long strips, and behind it you find not plaster but open sky. A hard white wind tears through the office, lifting the chair, the desk, and all the swimming papers. The room starts to fold inward. You grab its corners as they rise and, without understanding how, crease the walls together. The ceiling folds over the floor. The windowless office becomes a red paper box small enough to hold beneath one arm.

The corridor is draining now. Alarms pulse in the distance. The elevator has vanished.

At the end of the hall, the stairwell door hangs open.

You hear your own warning again: don’t take the stairs.

Behind you, something ticks.

You take the stairs.

Each landing is labeled with a year instead of a floor. At first you climb through years you recognize. Your first day at the company. Your graduation. A summer when you were seven. Then the numbers run backward too quickly, and the stairwell changes around you. Your hands grow smaller on the railing. Your shoes loosen. The red box becomes enormous and heavy, scraping behind you like a trunk.

At the landing marked BIRTH, the stairs end.

There is only a narrow ledge and, beyond it, the office where you began. You can see your boss through the glass, still waiting. The employees remain frozen. The wall clock still says eleven fifty-nine, but the single hand on the deadline’s face is trembling now.

You push the red box ahead of you. It passes through the glass without breaking it and unfolds in the middle of the office. Walls spring outward. The ceiling knocks aside the fluorescent lights. The tiny underground room expands until it contains your boss’s office, then the whole floor, then the entire building. For one dizzy instant, everyone is inside the room you brought upstairs, and the room is inside itself.

All the clocks strike noon.

The sound is deafening. Your coworkers burst into applause. Your boss smiles for the first time, but his teeth are rows of little white keys. The deadline removes its clock face as if it were a hat. There is nothing underneath.

“Excellent,” your boss says. “Now we can begin.”

He hands you another folder. It is impossibly heavy. On the cover is a blueprint of your house, and a red circle has been drawn around the room where you are sleeping.

Somewhere beyond the office, an alarm begins to ring. Everyone turns toward the sound, but no one moves. You open the folder and see your bed from above. You are lying in it with your eyes closed, while beside the bed a tall figure waits, one hand raised to knock.

The hand comes down.

You wake at your desk just before it touches the door, your cheek pressed against the warm red folder. The office is empty and dark. The wall clock has no hands.

Then, from inside the folder, your telephone starts ringing.
