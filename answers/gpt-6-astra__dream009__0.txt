The room smells of woodsmoke and rain. Your bed is stuffed with straw, and where your phone should be lies a small brass key, warm as if someone has just pressed it into your palm.

Outside, carts rattle over cobblestones. You step aside for a woman carrying a basket of silver fish.

“What year is it?” you ask, despite your plan.

She looks at your bare feet.

“The last one, if the bells don’t ring.”

Then she hurries away.

Above the rooftops stands a clock tower with no clock face—only a round, dark opening. Everyone in the square avoids looking at it. A boy selling apples catches you staring.

“You’re late,” he says.

“For what?”

He points to the key.

You close your fist around it. “Do you know me?”

“Not yet.”

He gives you an apple and refuses the coin you find in your pocket. The coin bears your face, older by many years.

At the tower door, the key turns before you touch it.

Inside, stairs spiral upward through a darkness full of quiet ticking. As you climb, you pass narrow windows. Through the first, the square is buried in snow. Through the next, its cobblestones are split by roots. Through the third, you see a railway station, and a woman leaning from a carriage to wave at someone below.

At the top waits a bell no larger than a teacup.

Beside it sits an old man in your childhood kitchen chair.

“I kept it safe,” he says, holding out the bell’s missing clapper.

You know his voice. You have heard it in recordings and disliked it.

“What happens if I ring it?”

“Morning.”

“And if I don’t?”

He glances toward the windows. You look too. Down in the square, the fish are still bright in the woman’s basket. The apple boy has one hand raised to shade his eyes.

You think of everyone you have ever asked for five more minutes.

Then you fit the clapper into the bell.

It rings once.

Far below, the woman sets down her basket and begins to cry. The boy throws his cap into the air.

The old man stands, suddenly frightened.

“Wait,” he says. “Before you wake—where did you put the key?”

But the tower is already folding into sunlight, and somewhere beside your bed, something small and brass falls to the floor.
