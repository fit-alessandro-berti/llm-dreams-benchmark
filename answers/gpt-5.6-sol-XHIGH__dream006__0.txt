I try again. A second moth emerges, then a third. Soon every word I mean to say is circling my head in silence. I can recognize them by their markings: one has the face of my childhood home on its wings, another carries the pattern of a map, another is entirely black except for a tiny golden door. I reach for that one, but it settles on the highest spotlight, beyond my grasp.

The audience begins to cough politely. Under the sound of the coughing I hear whispering, thousands of voices repeating my name with different pronunciations. I look down at my notes. The pages are blank, but they are warm and faintly pulsing, like something asleep. When I turn the first page, I find a staircase cut into the paper. It descends farther than the thickness of the lectern should allow.

Someone in the front row calls, “Go on.”

So I put one foot onto the paper stair. The stage tilts. The lectern rises above me like a building, and I find myself walking downward while still somehow standing in full view of the crowd. Their faces follow me over the edge. From below, the auditorium looks like a night sky made of open eyes.

At the bottom of the staircase is a narrow corridor lined with red curtains. Behind each curtain, I can hear applause. I pull one aside and see myself onstage as a child, delivering the speech perfectly in a language I no longer understand. Behind another, I am very old and have forgotten why I came, yet the crowd is weeping with gratitude. Behind a third there is only an empty suit speaking in my voice.

I close the curtain quickly, but the suit says, “You are late.”

All the curtains draw themselves back at once. Hundreds of versions of the stage stretch away in every direction, and on every stage a different me turns toward me. Some are confident, some terrified, some made of wood or glass. One has a nest where its mouth should be. Another is holding the golden door I saw on the moth’s wing.

I ask what I am supposed to say.

They answer together, but their voices arrive as a gust of wind. The curtains billow upward, revealing that there are no walls, only a vast field under a violet sky. Rows of audience seats have been planted in the soil like crops. Each chair contains someone I know, though many of them are people I have not met yet. My mother is sitting beside an old man who has my eyes. A woman in a blue coat holds an infant wrapped in one of my blank pages. Far in the back, I see my own empty chair.

The moths pour out of the corridor and spread across the sky. For a moment they form a sentence too large for me to read. The crowd turns in their seats to watch it pass overhead. Then the golden door appears upright in the field, unsupported, its brass handle gleaming.

I understand that my speech is on the other side.

As I walk toward it, the audience starts applauding. At first the sound is soft and encouraging, but it grows until the earth shakes beneath my feet. The rows of chairs sink slowly into the ground. People clap as the soil reaches their knees, their chests, their chins. I want to tell them to stop, but more moths fly from my mouth. I run from row to row, trying to pull them free, and each person calmly hands me a small object before disappearing: a key, a button, a wet stone, a baby tooth, a train ticket printed with tomorrow’s date.

At last only the empty chair remains. On its seat is the microphone from the stage, still switched on. I lift it, and from somewhere beneath the field I hear the audience breathing.

The door is waiting. None of the keys I have been given fit its lock, so I knock.

The door opens inward onto the original stage.

The auditorium is empty now. Dust floats through the spotlights, and every seat is folded shut. I step to the lectern. My notes are waiting in a neat stack, covered from edge to edge in my own handwriting. Yet the speech is not a speech at all. It is a list of things I have failed to notice: the last time someone waved from a window, the sound snow made against a coat sleeve, the moment a friend decided not to ask for help. The list continues in smaller and smaller writing until it becomes a single dark line.

I bring the microphone close and begin reading aloud.

With each sentence, one seat opens. The people rise from the floor one by one, brushing soil from their clothes. They do not applaud. They listen. I keep reading even when the lights go out, even when the stage dissolves beneath me and I am standing in darkness with nothing but the paper in my hands.

Near the end, I find one final sentence written in gold: “Tell them what you saw when you were not looking.”

I raise my eyes. The darkness is filled with moths, each holding a tiny light between its wings. Together they illuminate the audience, but now everyone has my face. Thousands of my own faces look back at me—young, old, sleeping, wounded, laughing—and I realize they have been waiting not for an answer but for me to notice that they are there.

I open my mouth. This time my voice comes easily, though I cannot hear the words. The audience can. One by one, they stand, turn into white moths, and rise through the ceiling.

When the last one has gone, the golden door closes somewhere behind me. In the sudden quiet, I look down and see that I am seated in the front row. Onstage, the microphone is alone.

It leans toward the empty auditorium and whispers my name correctly.
