You are walking through a peaceful forest when suddenly the weather changes dramatically. You feel a storm approaching and the birds vanish all at once, as though someone has closed a door on their singing. The silence they leave behind is enormous. Ferns turn their pale undersides toward the sky. The path, which had been warm and powdered with sunlight, darkens beneath your feet until it looks less like earth than a strip of deep water.

You hurry, though you cannot remember where you meant to go. Thunder rolls somewhere below the ground. Each time it sounds, the trees shudder and draw their roots up for an instant, like animals trying not to be touched. A wind rushes through the forest carrying the smell of rain, iron, and extinguished candles. Then the first drops fall—large, warm drops that strike the leaves with the slow ticking of many clocks.

Ahead, between two white birches, a house appears. You are certain it was not there before. It is narrow and tall, with a red door and dozens of small windows glowing amber through the rain. Smoke streams from its chimney, but instead of rising it curls downward, slipping beneath the door. When lightning opens the sky, you see that the house has no roof. Its upper rooms continue into the clouds.

You knock. The red door swings inward before your hand touches it.

Inside is the hallway of the house where you grew up, though the wallpaper is made of living moss. Your wet footprints shine briefly on the floorboards, then fill with tiny silver fish. At the far end of the hall, your grandmother is sitting at a table, or someone who has borrowed her shape. She is much younger than you remember and is carefully folding maps into paper birds.

“You took your time,” she says.

You want to ask what she means, but the storm strikes the house with such force that all the windows fly open. Rain comes in sideways. The paper birds wake in her hands and flutter around the room, beating their wings against the ceiling. One lands on your shoulder. Across its folded back, in your own handwriting, are the words: DO NOT LET THEM RING THE BELL.

From somewhere high above comes the dragging clang of a bell.

Your grandmother looks toward the staircase. “Too late,” she whispers, but she is smiling.

The stairs lengthen as you climb them. Each landing belongs to a different place: a school corridor flooded to the ankles, an empty railway platform under a green moon, a kitchen where a kettle boils without making steam. On every landing stands a person with their back to you. You know each of them intimately, but whenever you try to speak a name, thunder fills your mouth.

The paper bird tugs at your collar, urging you upward.

At last you reach a round room open to the sky. The storm turns overhead like the dark iris of a gigantic eye. In the center hangs a bronze bell, suspended from nothing. Its rope trails across the floor and disappears into a small trapdoor. Lightning crawls over the bell’s surface, illuminating hundreds of names scratched into the metal. Near the bottom you find yours.

The rope jerks.

You grab it with both hands. Something below pulls back. The bell sways above you, gathering its voice. Through the open walls you can see the whole forest bending in circles around the house. Trees tear loose and rise into the clouds, roots spread like black antlers. The paper bird on your shoulder unfolds itself in the wind. For a moment it is only a map again, and you glimpse a single red line leading from the house to a mark in the center of your chest.

Then the trapdoor bursts open.

Instead of darkness beneath it, there is a bright summer afternoon. A little child is standing in tall grass, holding the other end of the rope. You recognize the yellow coat, the muddy knees, the stubborn expression. It is you, years before you learned to be afraid of storms.

“Let go,” the child calls.

“The bell will ring.”

“That’s what bells are for.”

Your hands are aching. The wind strips leaves, photographs, and forgotten words from the air around you. The child laughs and releases the rope.

You fall backward, still clutching it. The bell swings once across the sky and rings.

The sound is not loud. It is deep and gentle, and it passes through everything. The storm freezes. Every raindrop hangs motionless, each containing a tiny upside-down forest. The lifted trees pause among the clouds. Far below, all the figures on the landings turn around at the same time, and you see that none of them has a face. They are only coats hanging in the shape of people.

Then the bell cracks from top to bottom. Dawn pours out through the split.

The house dissolves around you in a flurry of moss and paper. You are standing once more on the forest path. The sky is clear, and sunlight steams from the wet ground. Birds begin singing one by one, cautiously at first, as if testing whether the world will hold.

In your closed hand is a small brass key. You do not know what it opens. As you slip it into your pocket, you hear a faint bell ringing from somewhere inside the nearest tree. You press your ear to the bark and hear the child on the other side, laughing and running farther away.

You follow, leaving the path behind.
