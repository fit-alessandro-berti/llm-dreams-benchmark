You find yourself in a vast, unfamiliar city, and you realize you have an important meeting in an hour but no idea where it is. You start to walk, because standing still feels like the one thing you are not allowed to do.

The streets are wide and the buildings are tall and the color of wet sand. Every window is the same window. You check your pocket for the invitation and find a folded piece of paper, and when you open it the words are there but they slide off the page before you can finish reading them. You catch the word "fourth" and the word "before" and something that might be a name or might be the name of a street. You fold the paper again. It is warm, as though someone has just been holding it.

There is a clock on a tower ahead of you. It says ten past. You don't know ten past what. You decide it means you have fifty minutes, and this decision feels like a small victory.

A man is selling oranges from a cart. You ask him where the meeting is. He does not ask which meeting. He points down a side street with a hand that is missing its little finger and says, "You'll know it by the doors." You thank him and he gives you an orange and you put it in your coat pocket where it sits heavy and cold against your hip like a second, slower heart.

The side street narrows as you go. The buildings lean in. Laundry hangs between them, sheets and shirts, and among the shirts you see one of your own, a blue one you lost years ago, and you feel a rush of tenderness for it and keep walking. You do not have time to climb up and get it. You will come back for it. You know you will not come back for it.

The street empties into a square with a fountain that has no water in it. Instead, the basin is full of keys. Thousands of keys, brass and steel and one that is made of glass. Around the square are eight identical doors, tall and green, each with a brass number. The numbers are all four. You understand this is the place the orange seller meant. You do not understand which door.

You try the first one. It opens onto a staircase going down. At the bottom is a room with a long table and eleven chairs, and in ten of the chairs sit people in gray suits with their hands folded, looking at the empty chair. You know the empty chair is yours. You also know, with complete certainty, that this is the wrong meeting. You apologize. Nobody moves. One of them, a woman with your mother's haircut, says, "You're early," and it sounds like a warning.

You climb back up. The clock on the tower now says twenty to. Twenty to what, you still don't know. You have lost time somewhere in the stairs.

You try another door. It opens onto the same square you are standing in, seen from the other side, and you can see yourself standing there with your hand on a door, and the other you turns and looks at you and mouths something. You think it's "hurry." You think it might be "sorry." You close the door.

You take a key from the fountain because it seems to be what the fountain is for. It is the glass one. It is colder than the orange. You try it in the third door and the lock doesn't take it, the key just passes through the metal as if the metal were the thing that wasn't real.

You sit on the edge of the fountain. Your feet hurt in the specific way they hurt when you have been walking in the wrong shoes, and you look down and you are wearing your father's shoes, brown and enormous, laced with string. This explains a great deal.

A child crosses the square pulling a wagon. In the wagon is a telephone, the old kind, black, with a cord that trails behind on the cobblestones and disappears down the side street you came from. The telephone is ringing. The child stops beside you and waits, and the wagon waits, and the phone rings, and you understand you are expected to answer it.

You pick it up. There is a voice on the line and it is your own voice, but older, patient, a little tired.

"You're looking for the meeting," it says.

"Yes."

"It started already."

"I know. I mean, I thought so. Which door?"

"You've been through it," the voice says. "You went through it in the first street. You just kept walking."

You think about the orange seller. You think about the blue shirt. You think about how you decided ten past meant fifty minutes.

"What was it about?" you ask. "The meeting."

The line crackles. Somewhere behind the voice you can hear a room, chairs, a window being opened.

"You," it says. "It was about you. They'll say you did fine. They always say that."

The child begins to pull the wagon away and the cord goes taut in your hand and you have to let go or be dragged. The receiver bumps across the stones and out of the square, still talking, too far now to hear.

The clock on the tower has no hands at all. You feel, absurdly, relieved.

You stand up in your father's shoes. You take the orange out of your pocket and it has become, while you weren't paying attention, a small warm sun, and you hold it up and the square is lit from your hand, the green doors and the dry fountain and the keys, all of them, glittering. In the light you can see that the eighth door, the one you never tried, is standing slightly open, and through the gap there is a kitchen, and a kettle, and a chair pulled out from a table as if someone has just gone to get something and will be right back.

You walk toward it. You are not late for it. It is not the kind of thing you can be late for.

Behind you, very faintly, the telephone is still ringing, and then it isn't.
