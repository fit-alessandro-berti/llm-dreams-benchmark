You are participating in a competition where the grand prize is your dream job. As the competition progresses, you face various challenges and

the challenges keep changing shape before you can finish them. The first task is a written exam, but when you look down at the paper the questions are printed in a language you knew as a child and have since forgotten. You answer them anyway, and your hand writes in that language too, fluently, as if your fingers remembered what your mind had lost. The proctor collects the papers with hands that are too long, and smiles, and says, "Excellent. Round two is on the roof."

There is no staircase to the roof. There is an elevator, but the buttons are labeled with years instead of floors. You press the year you graduated and the doors open onto a rooftop garden where the other contestants are already waiting. You recognize some of them. One of them is your old manager. One of them is you, from behind — same coat, same haircut — and you decide very firmly not to look at that one's face.

The second challenge is announced by a voice that comes from the pigeons: build the office you want to work in. Everyone is given a small cardboard box and a pair of scissors. The others begin cutting furiously, producing glass towers, courtyards, atriums with impossible light. Your box, when you open it, contains a smaller box, and inside that one a smaller box again, and you understand — with the certainty that only comes in dreams — that this is the answer, that the job you want has always been a matter of opening things patiently. You raise your hand to say so, but the judges have already moved on.

Round three is dinner. The dining hall is your elementary school cafeteria, except the ceiling is very far away and slightly curved, like the inside of an egg. The interviewers sit across from you and ask questions while you eat: Where do you see yourself in five years? and when you begin to answer, the room ages five years around you — the paint fades, the other contestants' hair grows long, the pigeons on the windowsills turn grey. You learn to answer carefully. You learn to say here, now, doing this, and the room holds still.

Contestants are eliminated between rounds, but no one ever sees them leave. Their chairs are simply empty, and their name cards turn face down on the table like sleeping birds. Each time it happens, the building gets a little smaller. By the final round the whole competition fits inside a single office with one desk, one window, and one chair, and you are the only one left, unless you count the other you, who is standing in the corner, still facing the wall.

The head judge comes in. She is your grandmother, or she has your grandmother's way of setting down a cup of tea, which in the dream is the same thing. She slides a single sheet of paper across the desk. "The final challenge," she says, "is to read your offer letter aloud."

The letter is blank. You look up at her, and she nods at you to go on, so you begin to read from the empty page, and you hear yourself describing the job: the work is difficult in the ways you like and easy in the ways you don't; the window looks out on a sea that wasn't there a moment ago; your colleagues are kind and slightly translucent; the hours are whatever hours you are awake. As you read, the words appear on the paper, a half-second behind your voice, in the handwriting of the child who knew that forgotten language.

"You understand," your grandmother says, "that the position has been yours from the beginning. The competition was only to see how long you would keep competing."

In the corner, the other you finally turns around. You brace yourself, but the face is just your face, tired and relieved, and the other you says, "We can stop now," and sits down in the one chair, and you realize you are the one sitting in the chair, and the office is your bedroom, and the offer letter is the morning light coming in flat across the blanket.

Somewhere below, in the building that no longer exists, the pigeons announce the next round to no one. You do not go. You have the job. You sleep in on your first day, and nobody minds, because the first task — the only task, really — is to rest, and you are already doing it perfectly.
