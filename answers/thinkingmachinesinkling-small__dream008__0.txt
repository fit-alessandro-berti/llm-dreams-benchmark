...and you open your mouth to ask for more time, but your voice comes out as the sound of a fax machine slowly dying in another room.

Your boss nods—only, their face is now the polished sheen of the conference table, and they’re smiling with a mouth that stretches too wide, like a window opening onto a darker office.

The deadline is pinned to your chest with a thumbtack made of frozen time. It reads: *Due: Yesterday at 3:47 PM. Also: Tomorrow, but in a language you haven’t learned yet. Also: Never, because the project was already finished by someone who looks exactly like you, but older and made of office supplies.*

You sit down. Your chair is suddenly a speedboat, and you’re floating in a sea of spilled toner and floating sticky notes. The computer on your desk isn’t a computer anymore; it’s a glass aquarium filled with tiny, anxious versions of yourself who are all frantically typing and then dissolving into mist.

You try to start. You reach for the keyboard, but the keys are tiny doors. You open one: inside is a beach where the sand is shredded contracts. You open another: it’s your childhood bedroom, but the walls are filing cabinets and the bed is a printer jamming with your deepest secrets.

Your boss walks closer—only they are now dragging a chain of duplicate deadlines, each one a smaller, more impossible version of the last, and they’re saying something important but you can only hear it as the ringing of an alarm clock from 1987.

You decide to stop fighting the deadline.

You stand up. You pull a drawer open, and instead of pens, it contains a staircase made of old invoices that climbs up into the ceiling—which is now just a soft, gray sky. You begin to build. Not the project. A bridge of coffee cups and paper clips and stolen minutes from the clock on the wall, which has melted into a puddle of silver water.

You step onto the bridge. The office below shrinks, and you realize you can see yourself still at the desk, still panicking, still wondering when the impossible will turn possible.

The bridge takes you not to the meeting room, but to a door in the back of the photocopier.

You open it.

Behind it is a beach, but the tide is made of glowing spreadsheets, and the moon is the face of your boss, still smiling, waiting not for the project, but for you to realize that the only way to finish is to stop being a worker in the dream and become the thing the dream is about.

You reach out and touch the water.

You wake up with the taste of salt and toner on your tongue, and your hand is still reaching for a keyboard that is now just a pillow, and for a moment—before the alarm makes it real—you can still feel the impossible deadline hovering like a bird made of paper, just outside your window, waiting to be named.
