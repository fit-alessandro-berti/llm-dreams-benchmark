…find the person you borrowed the hour from.

You know this the way you know things here: completely, and without ever having been told. There's a debt. It sits behind your sternum like a swallowed coin. Somewhere in this town of low stone houses and smoke that lies flat against the fields, someone is waiting to be paid back sixty minutes, and until they are, the sun will keep hanging a hand's width above the ridge, refusing to finish setting.

Your shoes are wrong. You look down and they're soft leather, laced with something like grass, and they fit better than your real shoes ever did. This is the first clue that you are not a visitor. Visitors don't fit.

The lane is mud held together by straw. A woman is beating a rug against a wall, and the dust comes off it in the shape of a bird, which flies away. She nods at you. Her mouth says *you're late* a half-second after her lips stop moving, like the sound had to come a long way. You want to ask her what year it is, but you understand, suddenly, that the question would be rude — not because they don't know, but because they *do*, and it's considered vulgar to say it out loud, like mentioning the price of a gift.

You need to hurry. You have no idea where you're going. Both of these are true at once and neither troubles you.

A boy takes your hand and leads you through a doorway that smells of wet wool and apples. Inside is a room you have never seen and have missed your whole life. There's a hearth with a fire that gives light but no heat, and above it, on a nail, hangs a small brass key. You reach for it and it becomes a crust of bread in your palm, warm, still rising slightly, breathing. Of course. You can't pay back an hour with a key. You have to *feed* it to someone.

The boy is gone. In his place, sitting by the fire, is your grandmother, younger than you ever knew her, mucking out a stall that isn't there. She doesn't look up. She says, *You always did leave things out in the rain.*

You try to explain about the modern world — the phones, the small bright rectangles everyone carried like saints' medals, the way you could summon any song ever sung by touching glass. You want to tell her it wasn't all bad. But the words come out in the local tongue, which you speak perfectly, and what you actually say is: *I lost track of the time.*

She laughs. It's the exact laugh. It's the one from the kitchen when you were seven.

And you understand that this is the debt. Not sixty minutes. Just this one. Just standing here in a century that never happened, holding bread that used to be a key, being laughed at by someone who has been dead for eleven years.

You have it now. You've been paid.

Outside, the sun finally goes down, all at once, like a held breath let out — and the whole village turns to look at you, kindly, the way people look at someone who has just arrived somewhere they were always meant to be.

You want to stay. You want it so badly it wakes you.

The room you wake in is dark and full of small green lights, and you lie there with your hand still curved around something warm that isn't there.
