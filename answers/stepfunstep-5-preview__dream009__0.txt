You wake up in a completely different era, with no modern technology around. As you explore your new surroundings, you realize you need to find shelter before the curfew bell rings—though you do not yet know what curfew means, or why the streets are emptying so quickly, or why the gas lamps flicker in a rhythm that feels almost like breathing.

You are standing in a market square built of cobblestones and soot. The air smells of coal smoke, wet wool, and horse manure. There are no engines here, only the creak of wooden wheels and the distant call of a man selling eels. Your clothes feel wrong—too smooth, too bright—and when you touch the wall of a nearby shop, your fingers come away gritty with real grime, not the sanitized dust of a historical reenactment. This is not a village. This is a held breath.

You realize you need to understand the rules before the world notices you do not belong.

A woman in a heavy grey dress hurries past, her face obscured by a bonnet. She does not meet your eyes. Neither does the butcher, nor the child leading a goat by a rope. They move with the precise avoidance of people who have learned that newcomers bring misfortune. Or perhaps they simply know what you are: a smudge of wrongness in the weave of their time.

You pat your pockets, hoping for something familiar, but find only strange coins with the faces of kings who died centuries ago, and a scrap of paper with handwriting you do not recognize—your own, but slanted and tired. On it, three words are repeated: *Don’t follow the bell.*

The bell begins to toll.

Panic, cold and liquid, floods your chest. You realize you need to hide, and more than hide—you need to find the place where the era thins, where the dream leaks into itself. You duck into an alley that smells of yeast and rust. There, in the dark, you see a door left ajar, lamplight spilling out in a gold rectangle. Inside, shelves climb to a ceiling lost in shadow, and the air is thick with the vanilla scent of old paper.

You step inside. The door closes behind you without a sound.

An old man sits at a desk, though he does not look up from his ledger. "You’re late," he says, as if you have kept this appointment every night for a thousand years. "The previous century was getting crowded."

You realize, finally, that you need to sign your name in his book—not to record your arrival, but to prove you existed—to anchor yourself enough that the waking world will remember to pull you back.

Your hand trembles as you take the offered pen. The nib scratches. The ink is the color of dark arteries. And just as you finish the final letter, you hear, far above the shelves, the electric hum of an alarm clock.

The library dissolves. The smell of coal smoke lingers in your nostrils as your eyes snap open to the grey morning of your own time. But on your nightstand, where there should be nothing, there is a single unfamiliar coin, warm from someone else’s pocket, waiting for the next dream.
