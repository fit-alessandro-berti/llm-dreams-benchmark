You are in the middle of a heated argument with a close friend. The disagreement is about something important to both of you, and you decide to say the one thing you promised yourself you would never say — but when you open your mouth, what comes out instead is a long, low note, like a cello being tuned in another room.

Your friend stops. The note hangs in the air between you, visible now, a thin amber thread stretching from your mouth to their chest. You both look at it. Neither of you wants to be the one to break it.

"You always do this," your friend says, but their voice comes out as thread too, blue-grey, and it winds around your amber one like a vine. You realize the argument has been going on much longer than you thought — the room is full of these threads, layered and knotted near the ceiling, years of them, some so old they've gone brittle and pale. This is the room where all your arguments have been kept. You had forgotten it was here.

You reach up and touch one of the oldest threads. It plays back at once: the two of you, age nine, fighting over which of you saw the dead bird first. The voices are so small. Your friend hears it too, and something in their face comes loose.

"That was about the bird," they say.

"It was never about the bird," you say, and you both know this is true of tonight as well.

The floor tilts, politely, the way floors do when a dream wants you somewhere else. Now you are walking together down the staircase of your childhood house, except the staircase keeps going, past the basement, past the sewer-smell of the earth, down to a landing where there is a small kitchen table with two cups of tea already poured, still steaming, as if someone knew you'd be arriving mid-fight and would need somewhere to finish it.

You sit. The argument is still there — you can feel it in your hands, a heavy wet rope — but down here it seems willing to be set on the table between you and looked at, rather than pulled.

"I'm not saying you're wrong," your friend says. "I'm saying you left."

And you understand, with the sudden total clarity that only comes in dreams and is always gone by morning, that this is the actual sentence, the one underneath all the other sentences, the one the whole evening had been trying and failing to say. You left. Not the decision you argued about, not the money or the move or whatever it was — the words are already dissolving — just: you left, and they had to watch you do it.

"I know," you say. "I didn't know how to stay and still be me."

Your friend nods slowly. Above you, very faintly, you can hear the threads in the argument-room beginning to loosen, sliding off each other with a sound like snow coming off a roof.

The tea, when you drink it, tastes like the summer you were fifteen. Your friend is crying, or you are — in the dream it's the same water. They reach across the table and instead of taking your hand they take the heavy wet rope, and you take the other end, and together, without discussing it, you begin to coil it, hand over hand, the way fishermen do, until it is small and neat and can be put away.

"We're not done arguing," your friend says, and smiles.

"No," you agree. "But we're done fighting."

Then the kitchen fills gently with warm dark water, which does not alarm either of you, and you both float up through the ceiling, through the argument-room where the last threads have all come untied and drift like kelp, up through the roof of the house, into a sky that is holding, very carefully, one enormous amber-colored star. You are still holding the coiled rope. It has become light enough to carry anywhere.

Your friend says something you can't hear, and you say "What?" and they say it again, louder, and it's your name — said the old way, the way from before — and you wake with your own name in your ears and the certain feeling that somewhere, whatever was knotted has begun, at least, to loosen.
