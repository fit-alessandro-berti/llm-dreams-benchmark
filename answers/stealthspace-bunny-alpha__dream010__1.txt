As the competition progresses, you face various challenges and discover that each one is designed around a different kind of courage.

The first trials test what you know. The second tests what you can learn. Later, the questions become less certain—and the other contestants begin to disappear one by one. Some quit. Others stop competing because they realize the prize isn’t the only thing they want.

Near the final round, you’re offered a shortcut: answer exactly what the judges expect, and the job is yours.

But when you imagine yourself living that dream job, you feel only relief that the uncertainty is over. Not joy.

So you tell the truth instead.

You explain that you want the work—but not at the cost of your curiosity, your relationships, or the person you’re becoming to get it.

The judges fall silent.

Then the dream shifts.

You’re no longer in a competition. You’re starting your first day, surrounded by people who need what you can do and problems that cannot be solved by confidence alone. No spotlight. No final score. Just the ordinary, difficult, meaningful work you once dreamed of.

It is harder than winning was supposed to be.

It is better, too.

The prize was never really the job, you realize. It was the courage to choose a worthwhile path—and keep choosing it on the mornings when nothing feels magical at all.

Somewhere, an alarm begins to ring.

But for one more moment, you let yourself stay in the dream.
