You are about to give a speech to a large audience. As you step onto the stage, you see the crowd looking expectantly at you, and you begin to speak, and the first word comes out as a small brass key.

It falls from your mouth and rings once against the boards of the stage. Nobody laughs. A woman in the third row leans forward to see where it landed, the way you would watch a coin roll under a table. You bend to pick it up and when you stand again the lights have changed, they are warmer now, the color of a kitchen at night, and you realize you have no notes. You never had notes. You had a folded sheet of paper in your jacket pocket and you check the pocket and find only a second key, colder than the first.

The audience is patient. That is what unsettles you most. Their patience is enormous and soft, like a snowfall. You clear your throat and try again and this time the words come out as words, but they are not in the order you meant. "Thank you," you say, "for the river." You had meant to say for coming. You had meant to say good evening. Instead you have thanked them for a river and several of them nod, as if that is exactly what they were owed.

Behind you the screen that should be showing your first slide is showing a hallway. It is the hallway of the house you grew up in, the one with the runner carpet that always bunched near the bathroom door, and the camera, if it is a camera, is moving slowly down it. You want to turn around and look at it properly but you are aware that turning your back on an audience is a kind of failure, so you watch it in the reflection of the water glass on the podium, small and upside down, the hallway approaching a door.

"I was asked to talk about the future," you say, and this comes out correctly, which gives you courage. "About where we are going." Out in the dark a man raises his hand. You did not say there would be questions but you point at him anyway because pointing is something a speaker does. He stands. He is your father, or he has your father's coat, and he says, quite kindly, "Which one of us is asleep?"

You say you don't know. You say it into the microphone and it comes back at you from the walls, you don't know, you don't know, and the audience takes this as a thesis. They begin to write it down. Thousands of pens on thousands of pages, a sound like rain beginning on a roof, and you understand that whatever you say now will be kept.

So you try to say something true. You open your mouth and describe the runner carpet in the hallway, how it bunched, how your mother would kick it flat with the side of her foot without breaking stride, and how you can still do that motion in your own house though the carpet there is different and never bunches. The audience writes this down too. On the screen behind you the hallway door has opened and the room beyond it is this room, the auditorium, seen from the back, and there is a small figure at the podium, and it is you, and it is bending down to pick something up.

The keys in your hand are warm now. Both of them. You hold them up so the audience can see and you say, "These are for you," and you mean it, though you have no idea what they open. People begin to come forward. Not in a rush, in a line, the way people come forward for communion or to sign a book. The woman from the third row is first. You press one key into her palm and she closes her fingers over it and says, "It was under the stage the whole time," and goes back to her seat.

The line is very long. You give away the second key and then you find a third in your sleeve and a fourth behind your ear like a magician's coin and you keep giving them away and each person tells you one thing as they take it. A man says the lights in the hallway used to hum. A girl says the river was cold but not too cold. Someone says, low, near your ear, you're doing fine, and you don't see who.

At some point you notice you are no longer standing on the stage. You are in the line. You have been in the line for a while. Up at the podium someone is giving out keys, someone with your shoulders, and the screen behind them shows the hallway again, empty now, the carpet flat, a door at the end of it standing open with morning coming through.

When you reach the front the speaker puts a key in your hand without looking at you. It is the first one, the brass one. You can tell by the warmth. You are meant to say something in return, everyone else did, and what you say is, "I was supposed to give a speech."

"You did," the speaker says. "Everyone wrote it down."

And you turn to go back to your seat but there are no seats now, only the hallway, and the runner bunched near the bathroom door, and you kick it flat with the side of your foot without breaking stride, and the door at the end is open, and you are almost, almost awake.
