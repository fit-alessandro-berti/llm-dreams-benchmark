Your friend tells you not to do this again.

“Do what?” you ask, and your reflection answers a moment before you do.

“Disappear.”

You wipe the glass with your sleeve. Behind the reflection is a railway platform at night. Rain trembles in the yellow station lights, and a train is waiting with all its doors open. You can hear a conductor calling a destination, but each time he calls it, he uses your friend’s name.

The argument has gone silent behind you. Your friend is holding the small blue cup you both bought years ago at a market by the sea. That was what the fight was about, you suddenly remember—not the cup itself, but who had dropped it, who had promised to keep it safe, who had been careless with all the fragile things that came afterward. Yet the cup is whole in your friend’s hands. A thin crack runs through the room instead, beginning beneath their feet and climbing the wall toward the ceiling.

“If you leave now,” your friend says, “you’ll forget why you were angry.”

This seems more frightening than remaining angry forever.

You step through the mirror.

Rain lands warmly on your face. The platform is crowded with people wearing coats made from old maps. None of the maps show the same country. As they pass, roads and rivers slide across their shoulders. You search for a familiar route, but the names have all been replaced by apologies: I Should Have Called, I Didn’t Mean It, Please Come Back.

The whistle blows. Your friend has followed you through the glass, still carrying the blue cup. They look younger here, almost as they did when you first met, except their hair is full of snow. Neither of you mentions it.

You board the train from opposite doors and somehow arrive in the same compartment. There are two seats and a little table. On the table sits the object you were truly arguing about: a sealed envelope addressed in both your handwritings. You know that inside it is the memory each of you insists belongs only to you.

The train begins moving before you can decide whether to open it.

Outside the window, the city passes in the wrong order. You see the hospital before the birthday, the funeral before the introduction, the empty apartment before the keys are handed over. In every scene, you and your friend stand at a distance from yourselves, waving frantically, trying to warn the people you used to be. Those earlier selves never look up.

Your friend reaches for the envelope. You seize the other end.

“You always make everything yours,” they say.

“You always give things away and blame me for keeping them.”

As you pull, the envelope stretches like fabric. The compartment stretches with it. Soon you are standing at opposite ends of a train car miles long, the envelope a white road between you. Your friend becomes a tiny dark figure at the horizon. Their voice reaches you through the cup, which is suddenly in your hands.

“I wasn’t asking you to agree with me,” the cup says. “I was asking you to remember me correctly.”

You look into it. The cup is full of seawater. Far below its rim is the market where you bought it, each stall bright beneath striped awnings. You and your friend move through the miniature crowd together, laughing over something you can no longer hear. Then the younger version of you pauses and looks straight up through the water.

“It was already cracked,” they say.

The train jolts. The cup slips from your fingers.

It falls for a very long time.

You dive after it, expecting the hard floor, but plunge into the sea instead. The train continues overhead along tracks laid on the surface of the water, its windows glowing like a procession of moons. Beneath you, thousands of blue cups drift downward. Inside each one is a different version of the argument. In one, you apologize immediately. In another, your friend leaves. In another, neither of you can speak, and flowers grow from your mouths until the whole room is a garden.

You swim deeper, following the cup you dropped. Pressure closes around your ribs. At the bottom of the sea stands the room where the argument began, perfectly dry and lit by morning. Your friend is seated at the table alone. They have grown very old while waiting.

When you enter, water pours from your pockets but vanishes before reaching the floor.

“I came back,” you say.

“No,” your friend replies gently. “You came forward.”

They slide the sealed envelope across the table. It is small again. The anger in you has also become small—not gone, but reduced to a hard bright seed. You realize you could swallow it and carry it forever, or plant it and see what grows.

You tear open the envelope.

There is no letter inside. There is only a narrow doorway, folded impossibly flat. When you unfold it, sunlight spills across the table. Beyond it lies the shore from years ago. The two of you are there, walking side by side with your shoes in your hands. Between your footprints, tiny green shoots push out of the wet sand.

Your friend offers you the blue cup. The crack in it shines gold now, branching like a river on a map.

“I am still angry,” you tell them.

“So am I.”

This time the words do not shake the room. They settle between you like two stones placed carefully on a grave.

You both step through the little door. On the beach, the younger versions of you turn around and wait. The sea is withdrawing all the way to the horizon, revealing lost keys, coins, photographs, and hundreds of unopened doors embedded in the sand. Somewhere very far away, a train whistle sounds.

You begin walking toward yourselves. With every step, you remember something differently, but your friend remains beside you. Just before you reach the place where the four of you will meet, the blue cup fills with rain, and from the bright seed of your anger a single white tree begins to grow.
