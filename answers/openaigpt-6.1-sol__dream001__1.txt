But the building moves.

Not quickly—just a few feet to the left whenever you look away. By the time you reach the corner, it has slipped behind a row of narrow houses, and your watch says you have forty-three minutes.

You stop a woman carrying a glass bowl full of keys.

“Do you know where my meeting is?”

She searches your face, then selects a small brass key from the bowl.

“You’ll need this when you remember.”

Before you can ask anything else, she boards a bus with no doors.

The key is warm. Written along its teeth, in letters too small to read until you hold it close, is your childhood address.

You turn around.

There, between a pharmacy and a shop selling rain, stands your old front door.

Inside, the hallway is exactly as you remember it, except that it extends for miles. Every door has a clock above it. Some run backward. One has hands made of grass.

At the end of the hall, a telephone rings.

You run toward it, passing doors behind which people laugh, argue, wash dishes. The hallway gets shorter with each step, until you are standing in your childhood kitchen.

The telephone stops.

At the table sits an older version of you, wearing the coat you lost years ago. Two cups of tea steam between you.

“Am I late?” you ask.

They glance at the empty chair.

“No. Sit down.”

You take the chair, still holding the key. You had planned to explain about the moving building and the woman with the bowl, but now it seems more important to watch your older self pour milk into the tea.

“What’s the meeting about?”

They slide a photograph across the table.

It shows you standing in the unfamiliar city, looking up at the tallest building. In an upper window, too distant for you to have noticed, your older self is watching.

“I wanted to ask,” they say, “why you never looked up a second time.”

Outside the kitchen window, a bus pulls away. You hear keys shifting in a glass bowl.

Your watch begins to ring.
