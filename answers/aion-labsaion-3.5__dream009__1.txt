You wake up in a completely different era, with no modern technology around. As you explore your new surroundings, you realize you need to find water—your throat is dry in that peculiar, urgent way that only happens in dreams, where thirst feels like a message from somewhere far away.

The streets are cobbled and narrow, half-timbered houses leaning overhead until their upper stories nearly touch. The air smells of woodsmoke, horse dung, and something baking—bread, maybe, though sweeter than any bread you've known. A woman in a linen coif carries a wooden yoke with two buckets, and you follow her without deciding to, the way one does in dreams, as if your feet already know the route.

She leads you to a well in the center of the village square. The stone rim is worn smooth by centuries of hands. You drink from the ladle that hangs there on a rope, and the water is impossibly cold, so cold it seems to travel down through your whole body like a silver thread.

When you look up, wiping your mouth, the square has changed. It's the same square—you're certain of it—but now there's a market, and a clock tower where there wasn't one before, and the people's clothes have shifted somehow, become both older and newer at once. A merchant calls out prices for things you don't recognize. A child chases a hoop past your legs, laughing, and the laugh echoes strangely, as if the child is laughing from inside a cathedral.

You reach into your pocket and find something there—a key, or perhaps a small brass gear, warm as if someone just held it. You want to ask someone what it opens, but every time you approach a villager, they smile and turn away, and their faces are difficult to remember even as you're looking at them.

The light begins to change. The sun doesn't set so much as lower itself gently, like something being placed down with care. You realize, with the calm certainty that dreams provide, that you have somewhere to be—an inn, maybe, or a house at the end of the lane where a lamp is already lit in the window, waiting for you, and has been waiting for a very long time.

You start walking toward the lamp. The cobblestones are soft under your feet now, softer than stone should be, and just as you reach the door, your hand on the iron latch, warm and rusted and real—

—you wake, with the smell of woodsmoke still in your nose and the key still warm in your palm, though of course your palm is empty. It takes a long moment to remember which era you belong to. Longer, perhaps, than it should.
