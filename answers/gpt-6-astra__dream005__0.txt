each group seems to be waiting for you to finish a conversation you don’t remember starting.

“Yes, but what happened to the birds?” asks a woman wearing a necklace of tiny silver spoons.

You apologize and move on.

The gathering fills a house much larger inside than out. Beyond the dining room is a train station; beyond the station, a garden where everyone stands ankle-deep in snow, holding warm plates. Somewhere, you hear the laugh of the one person you know. Each time you follow it, someone shuts a door—not to keep you out, but because they haven’t noticed you coming.

You take out your phone. Instead of names, your contacts contain descriptions:

*The person who lent you an umbrella.*

*The person you almost became.*

*Someone who is also looking.*

You tap the last one.

A phone rings beneath the table beside you.

You lift the tablecloth and find a small boy sitting cross-legged, answering a telephone made from two seashells. You recognize his shoes: they are a pair you lost when you were six.

“They’re outside,” he whispers.

“Who?”

He covers the shell with his hand and looks annoyed.

You go through the nearest door and emerge onto the roof. The whole gathering is below you now, visible through the ceiling as though it were still water. People drift from room to room, carrying their glasses carefully. You spot your friend at last, standing alone beside a coat rack.

You call their name.

They look up, smile, and point behind you.

There is a coat rack on the roof, too. Your coat hangs from it, and from the pocket comes the sound of someone knocking.

You reach inside and pull out a door key, warm from another hand.

When you look down again, your friend is wearing their coat. They seem relieved. You know, with the certainty of dreams, that you must get downstairs before they leave.

Behind you, a kettle begins to boil.
