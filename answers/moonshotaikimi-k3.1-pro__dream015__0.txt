It is a foolish decision, which is why it feels right.

You choose the courtyard of a ruined library, where fallen marble columns lie like the bones of enormous animals. The soil is mostly ash and powdered glass, but every morning you carry water to it in a dented helmet. Every afternoon you sift out the debris. Every night you sleep beside the bare earth with one hand on your knife.

You trade your last blanket for an envelope of seeds. The old woman who gives it to you smells faintly of rain.

“Seven seeds,” she says.

But when you open the envelope, there are eight.

You plant them all.

For days, nothing happens. Raiders pass through the streets below. Something howls from the subway tunnels. The sky turns the color of an old bruise. Still, you carry water.

Then, one dawn, a green thread lifts itself from the ash.

People begin arriving. At first, they come to steal. A man with a rifle stands at the courtyard gate, staring at the single sprout. After a long while, he lowers his weapon and offers you a cracked bucket. A girl brings a spoon for digging. An old soldier brings a sack of soil he has carried for years, though he can no longer remember why.

Soon there are twelve gardens. Then twenty.

The plants grow strangely, as plants do in dreams. Tomatoes glow like small lanterns. Beans unfold into pages covered with handwriting. A tree produces blue fruit that tastes, each person insists, like a different memory: a mother’s kitchen, a summer lake, bread eaten before anyone knew the world could end.

No one fences off their plot. No one counts what they take.

On the night the first real rain comes, everyone stands in the courtyard with their faces turned upward. Water streams through your hair and runs cold into your mouth. Around you, the ruined city begins to shimmer. Vines climb the shattered towers. Somewhere, impossibly, a bird sings.

The old woman appears beside you, dry beneath the downpour.

“You see?” she says. “It was never too late. Only too quiet.”

You ask her what will happen now.

She points to your hands.

They are empty, except for a single seed.

“What will you plant tomorrow?”

Before you can answer, you wake.

For a moment, you can still hear the rain. And beneath your fingernails, dark and unmistakable, is soil.
