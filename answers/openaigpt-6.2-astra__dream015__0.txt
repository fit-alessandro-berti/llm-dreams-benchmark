It seems like a foolish use of the last dry room in the train station. People need it to sleep in. So you lay the books flat beneath the cots, spines facing out, and at night anyone who wants a story has to ask a neighbor to roll over.

For weeks, nobody borrows anything. Then a girl with a shaved head asks if you have a book about pears. She has found a tree, she says, and wants to know what to do.

You give her a gardening manual. She shakes her head. “I know how to look after a tree. I want to know when I can eat one.”

There is nothing about that in the manual. But under cot seven is a cookbook, and inside its cover someone has written in pencil: *Press gently beside the stem. If it gives, it’s ready. Don’t squeeze the belly, Arthur.*

The girl goes away satisfied.

Autumn comes. One morning you wake to find a pear on every pillow. At the far end of the room, an old man sits holding his in both hands. You have known him all summer as Seven. Now you watch him put his thumb beside the stem, and before you can stop yourself you say, “Arthur?”

His head comes up.

For the first time since the world ended, you have the peculiar embarrassment of having called a stranger by his first name.

“Sorry,” you say. “I’ve got something of yours.”
