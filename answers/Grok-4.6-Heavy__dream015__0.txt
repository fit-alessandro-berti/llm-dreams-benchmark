You find yourself in a post-apocalyptic world, with resources scarce and dangers everywhere. Despite the hardships, you decide to keep a packet of seeds and walk toward the rumor of a greenhouse that still breathes.

The cities are skeletons of rust and glass. You travel at dusk, when the heat finally lets go and the things that hunt grow slower. In your pack: a dented kettle, a knife, a photograph whose face changes whenever you aren't looking. The seed packet is labeled in your own handwriting — a name you do not remember writing, from a life that may not have been yours.

You do not know if anything will grow. You only know that hoarding the seeds felt like another kind of death.

On the third night you find it: not a fortress, just a cracked glass house leaning against a collapsed overpass. Inside, something green is still insisting. Condensation beads on the panes. You pry the door and step into wet air that smells of soil instead of smoke. The glass reflects a sky greener than the one outside.

You kneel. You press a seed into the dirt with your thumb.

A child appears in the broken doorway with a pocketful of seeds she cannot name. You teach her the names. Raiders pass. Storms pass. You stay. Outside, the world keeps ending. In here, two people kneel in the dirt and plant something they may never eat — because the act of planting is how you refuse to become the wasteland.

The hardships do not leave. They just stop being the only thing that is true.
