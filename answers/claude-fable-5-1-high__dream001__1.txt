You find yourself in a vast, unfamiliar city, and you realize you have an important meeting in an hour but no idea where it is. You start to walk, because walking feels like the thing to do, and because standing still on this corner makes the buildings lean in a little closer than you would like.

The streets are wide and the light is the color of weak tea. Trams pass without stopping, their windows full of people who are all facing the same direction, as if watching something at the front of the car that you can't see. You check your pocket for the invitation and find instead a folded napkin with an address written on it in your own handwriting, though you don't remember writing it. The address reads: 14 Corvid Lane, the room with the green door, don't be late, they already know. You read the last three words several times. You decide not to think about them.

You ask a woman selling oranges where Corvid Lane is. She points with her whole arm, the way people point in dreams, and says, "Past the river, but the river moves." You thank her. She gives you an orange and will not take money for it. It is heavier than an orange should be, and warm, like something that has been held for a long time.

The river, when you find it, is running uphill. This does not seem strange until you are already halfway across the bridge, and by then it is too late to be surprised. On the far side the city changes. The buildings are older, narrower, and all of the street signs are written in a language you can almost read, the letters rearranging themselves just before your eyes settle on them. You have forty minutes. You know this without a watch. The knowledge sits in your chest like a stone you swallowed.

You turn down a street because it feels correct and find that it is full of doors. Not buildings with doors, but doors alone, standing upright in the cobbles with nothing behind them, hundreds of them, in every color except green. You walk among them. Some are ajar. Through one you see a kitchen where a kettle is beginning to whistle. Through another, a classroom you attended when you were nine, the chalkboard still bearing the last problem you never solved. Through a third, a room with a long table and empty chairs and a single glass of water sweating onto a coaster, and you understand, the way you understand things here, that this is the meeting room, and that you are looking at it from the wrong side.

You try to step through. The doorway is a painting of a doorway. Your hand flattens against it.

"You have to go around," says a man sitting on a folding chair between two doors. He is wearing your father's coat, but he is not your father. He is peeling the orange you were given, though you can still feel its weight in your own hand. "Everyone tries to go straight through. That's the mistake."

"How do I go around?"

"The same way you got here." He offers you a segment of orange. It tastes like the smell of rain on hot pavement. "You forgot where you were going. Forget again."

You don't know how to forget on purpose. You tell him so. He nods like this is the correct answer and points, again with his whole arm, down a side street you had not noticed. Thirty minutes.

The side street becomes a stairwell, the stairwell becomes a corridor of an office building where every fluorescent light is a slightly different shade of white. The carpet is patterned with the map of the city you have been walking through, and you can see the river on it, and it is indeed moving, a thin blue thread sliding slowly across the floor ahead of you. You follow it. Your shoes make no sound. Behind the doors on either side you hear meetings in progress, the murmur of agendas, someone laughing too loudly at something that wasn't very funny. Each time you pass a door, a voice inside says your name, casually, as part of a sentence, as if you had just been mentioned in passing. You do not stop. You are learning.

At the end of the corridor there is a lift with no buttons. You step in and the doors close and you rise, or you sink, it's impossible to tell, and for a long moment you are nowhere, just a body in a small bright box, and you think: this is the part where I could wake up. You could. The edges of things are going thin. But you want to know what the meeting is about. You want to know who already knows, and what they know. So you stay.

The doors open onto Corvid Lane. It is a real street, at last, with real crows on the wires, dozens of them, all silent, all watching. Number 14 is a narrow house with a green door, and the green is exactly the green you were expecting, which is somehow the strangest thing that has happened all day. Ten minutes. You climb the steps. You knock.

The door is opened by yourself.

Not a reflection. Not a twin. Just you, in the clothes you are wearing, holding the orange you are holding, looking as startled as you feel. For a moment you both stand there. Then the other you steps aside and gestures you in with a whole arm, and you go, because you are late, because they already know, because there was never any other way this was going to end.

Inside is the long table and the empty chairs and the single sweating glass of water. You sit. The other you sits across from you. Through the window you can see the city spread out below, the river folding back on itself, the field of doors glinting like teeth, the tram full of people all facing forward, and you realize now what they were looking at: they were looking at this window. They were looking at you.

"Well," says the other you, and slides the glass of water across the table. "Shall we begin?"

You reach for it. Your hand closes around cold glass. Somewhere very far away a kettle is beginning to whistle, and the light is coming up, and you have the distinct and terrible sense that the meeting has been going on for a very long time, and that you are the only one who ever arrives, and that you are always, always exactly on time.
