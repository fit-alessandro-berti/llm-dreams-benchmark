…and you decide to stop winning.

Not because you've run out of arguments. You've got a dozen more loaded and lined up, each one true, each one honed over years of bringing this up at 1 a.m. in diners and in the back seat of the car and in the particular silence that falls after a phone call hangs up too early. You've got the receipts. You could finish this in eleven minutes and walk out feeling righteous.

But something in the kitchen has gone thin and airless, and you notice, for the first time in maybe an hour, that the person across the table has stopped talking. They've stopped talking the way a person stops talking when they've begun to listen for something instead.

The kettle has boiled dry and is clicking as it cools. There's a brown ring in the mug they keep turning their hands around — the one with the chip out of the rim, the one they grabbed off the shelf without looking, the way they do. Outside, the porch light is on, and the moths are doing their thing against the glass, patient as creditors.

The subject, again: the house. Their house. Your house. The one with the sagging porch and the door that sticks in August and the upstairs room where the two of you, at nineteen, sat on the floor with a bottle of something warm and made a solemn pact about the shape of your lives. They want to sell it. You want to keep it. Neither of you has said the word *move* yet, but it's in the room, standing behind the refrigerator with its hands over its mouth.

And here's the thing you can feel but can't say: this isn't about the house. This is about the fact that they've already gone — already packed half a life into boxes you weren't allowed to see — and they came over tonight to hand you the last of it, the final version of the question they've been asking for a year: *Are you coming or aren't you?*

Which is why you say, finally:

"Okay. Stop. I'm done arguing."

They blink. Their hands tighten on the chipped mug.

"Tell me what you actually want," you say. "Not the house. Not the price. Not what's fair. What do *you* want?"

And their face does the dream thing — it goes soft at the edges, goes underwater, and for one lurching second they're nineteen, and then they're someone you've never met but would walk into traffic for, and then they're just *them*, all the way through, unwrapped.

"I want to stop being the person who agrees with you," they say. "I've been doing it so long I've forgotten where the wanting ends and the agreeing starts."

The kitchen drains.

The floor becomes a slow slope of black water. The clock on the wall ticks backward with a sound like a coin dropped into a well. Your chair scrapes away from the table and there's no one sitting across from you anymore — there's no table, no mugs, no house — just you, in your coat, holding a chipped mug with both hands, saying, in a voice that is unbearably familiar:

*You don't listen. You have never once listened.*

And you realize you've been saying it to yourself. Both of you have the same stubborn jaw. Both of you have the same habit of going quiet instead of saying the true thing, and the whole fight, every fight, has been you — you — trying to get a person who isn't in the room to answer you.

The porch light flickers. The moths scatter.

You wake up.

Your phone is ringing on the nightstand, the screen bright and terrible in the dark. Their name. The real one. They called at 11:40 and let it ring eleven times and now they're calling again, and you have no memory of the argument you were in, or which version of it was true, or what either of you were actually fighting for.

But you remember the rule. The one you set, in the dream, in the flood and the backward clock: *whatever they want, say yes to the real thing.*

You pick up.

You don't say hello. You say, "Okay. Talk. I'm listening."
