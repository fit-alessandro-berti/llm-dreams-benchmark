**you realize you need to be useful before the first frost.**

---

The silence is the first thing that wakes you. Not quiet — *silence*. The particular, enormous silence of a world with no engines in it, no hum of electricity, no distant jet splitting the sky. The only sounds are a rooster somewhere, the creak of a cart, and wind moving through wool.

You're lying on a straw pallet in a room that smells of smoke, tallow, and old wool. Your clothes are gone. Someone has folded a heavy linen shift and a wool dress at the foot of the bed, and they fit you well enough that you have to believe they were made for you.

You go outside. The village spills down a hillside in a way that no modern place ever could — thatched roofs, a stream, a stone church with a bell, fields of something that isn't quite wheat. A woman passes, looks at you, and crosses herself. A boy stops and stares at your hands, which are clean in a way that must look strange.

You pat your pockets. Keys. A dead watch. A folded thing that used to be a phone, black glass, no light. You hold it up to the sun anyway, because hope is a habit.

Nothing.

The realization doesn't arrive all at once. It comes in stages, like cold feet:

First: *water*. Then *food*. Then *shelter*. Then *language* — no, not language, you're starting to pick up words, the words are coming to *you*, carried on gesture and repetition and the way a child names a creek. Then, when the woman brings you broth and a heel of bread and sits with you as though she has all afternoon, when the boy returns to show you the trick of balancing a coin on a horn and laughing at himself:

*Before the first frost, you need to be useful.*

Because no one here has ever had you. There is no room in this village for a stranger who cannot mend, cannot sow, cannot read the strange marks the village scribe makes, cannot do the hundred small invisible things you have never once noticed people doing because machines did them. Winter is six weeks out. The stores are counted. The accounts are short, and everyone knows it.

So you begin.

You learn the names of things by touching them, and the names turn out to be the beginning of everything. You show the shepherd how to cut the wool in strips instead of shearing it whole, and the sheep survive the rot that took last year's flock. You draw, in dirt, the shape of a furrow that will shed water instead of holding it, and the field that was dead in June is green in August. You sit with the village child and teach her the numbers she was too afraid of the village scribe for — one, two, three — and she teaches you how to say *stranger* in a way that doesn't mean *thief*.

They feed you. They give you a corner by the fire. And slowly, the way water finds its level, you become a person who belongs here. The old woman with the crossed fingers calls you *the one who came down*, and no one asks where from, because asking would be unkind.

But nights, when the fire burns low and the bell has long since gone still, you take out the black glass thing and hold it to the firelight. And you think about the word *impossible*.

Then, one night, the girl — the one with the coin trick — crawls under your blanket uninvited, as children do in cold weather, and says something in the new tongue you almost, almost understand.

You catch it. One word, clear as a bell.

*"Home?"*

You don't know how to answer her. You don't know if you can. The glass is dead. The sky is not a door.

But the girl is looking at you with an expression you recognize from every era you've ever lived in: the look of someone who has decided you are theirs to help.

And you understand then that this is how it works. Not a portal. Not a machine. A story, told often enough, from one voice to another, becomes a road. *There was a stranger who came down from the sky, and she knew how to make the field green, and she is sleeping in the corner by the fire, and her name is—*

So you need a name they will keep. You need to be useful enough that the keeping becomes a duty. You need to survive the first frost, and the second, and the long grey middle of the years.

You need to be remembered.

The girl waits, patient as the whole dark countryside.

You tell her your name.

And she says it back to you — badly, the consonants strange and soft — and laughs at herself, and says it again, and again, until it sticks, until it belongs to her, until by spring every child in the valley can say it and half the elders can too.

By the time the bells ring for the spring ploughing, you are no longer a visitor.

You are a story they tell.

And that, it turns out, is the only way anyone has ever come home.
