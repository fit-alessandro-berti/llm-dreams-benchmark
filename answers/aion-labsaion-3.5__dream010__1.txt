As the competition progresses, you face various challenges and each one peels away something you thought was essential.

The first challenge takes place in a hallway lined with telephones. Each one rings with a different voice—your mother's, an old teacher's, a boss you once had, your own voice from years ago asking questions you never answered. To pass, you must pick up every receiver and respond honestly. Some conversations take seconds; one, with a voice that sounds like your own doubt, lasts what feels like decades. When you set down the final phone, the hallway has become a single door.

The other contestants are strange. Sometimes you see them clearly—a woman made of shifting light, a man who seems to be arguing with his own shadow, a child who walks like they carry centuries. Other times you look at them and see only your own face, older or younger, tired or hopeful. The judges sit behind a desk that stretches impossibly upward, and you can never quite see their faces—only that they nod when you speak truthfully and remain perfectly still when you perform.

The second challenge asks you to build a bridge across a chasm using only your failures. You start with the obvious ones—the rejections, the mistakes, the moments of cowardice—and to your surprise, they hold weight. They are sturdy. It's the polished achievements that crumble when you try to use them, turning to glitter and dust in your hands. Halfway across, you realize you've been carrying these failures for years anyway; here, at least, they serve a purpose.

By the third challenge, most contestants have vanished—or perhaps they've simply become indistinguishable from the walls, which now breathe softly. The final test is a single question, written on a card that smells like your childhood home: *Describe the dream job.*

You open your mouth to say the title you've rehearsed your whole life, and nothing comes. The words have no weight here. You try again. Still nothing. The judges lean forward, waiting. And then, from somewhere deeper than rehearsed ambition, you hear yourself say: "I don't know its name. I only know how it feels. It feels like being fully used—like everything I am is finally being spent on something that doesn't require me to be anyone else."

The judges nod, once.

The grand prize is handed to you in a small box. Inside is a key with no lock, a mirror that shows not your face but your posture when you're absorbed in something you love, and a blank business card. On the back, in handwriting you recognize as your own but have never seen before, it says: *This position has always been yours. The competition was never against them. It was against the version of you who believed you had to win something to be allowed to begin.*

You wake up.

The morning light is ordinary. Your life, on its surface, looks unchanged. But you notice your hands are different—steadier, somehow—or maybe it's just that you're holding them differently. And somewhere behind your ribs, a door you forgot you built has quietly unlocked itself, and you can hear, faintly, the sound of your own footsteps walking toward something that doesn't have a name yet but has, apparently, been waiting a very long time for you to arrive.
