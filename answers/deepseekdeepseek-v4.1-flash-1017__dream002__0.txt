You feel a storm approaching and the forest holds its breath. The wind begins to move strangely—upward instead of sideways—lifting leaves into a slow green spiral. The sky bruises purple, and the first drop of rain hangs in the air in front of you, trembling like a glass bead. When it finally falls, it lands on the moss and rings like a tiny bell.

You start to run, but the roots shift under your feet, arranging themselves into shallow stairs. Lightning flashes, and for one white instant the forest is frozen: every leaf, every bird, every raindrop suspended. In the distance stands a small cabin made of black glass. Thunder shakes the world back into motion, and you reach the door just as the storm begins to sing—a low, humming sound that feels almost like a voice.

Inside, it is summer daylight. The cabin is your old childhood bedroom, dust motes drifting through warm air, except the window looks out onto the same darkening forest. A stag waits there, its antlers tangled with tiny lanterns. It turns its great head toward you and says, in a voice like rain on canvas, “You didn’t need to run. The storm was only asking to come in.”

You open the window. The wind slips past you, gentle now, and the rain turns to silver moths that gather around the stag’s antlers. The forest outside begins to glow softly, tree by tree, as if the storm were lighting every path home at once.

Then, far away, an alarm clock rings—muffled, underwater, impossible. The stag lowers its head. “Remember this part,” it says.

You wake with the smell of rain in the room, though the window is closed, and for a moment you can still see a single silver moth resting on the curtain.
