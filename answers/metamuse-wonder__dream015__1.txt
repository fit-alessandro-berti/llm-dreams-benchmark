You find yourself in a post-apocalyptic world, with resources scarce and dangers everywhere. Despite the hardships, you decide to not just survive — but to rebuild something worth surviving for.

You start with the rooftop of a burned-out library. The city below is quiet in that strange dream-way, where distant fires flicker but never spread, and the wind carries the smell of rain and rust. You haul up broken buckets, cracked planters, anything that can hold soil. Your hands are blistered. Your water ration is half a bottle a day.

Still, you plant seeds.

At first, no one understands. A scavenger named Mara watches you from the stairwell for three days before she finally speaks. "Nothing grows here anymore," she says.

"Then we'll be the first to be disappointed trying," you tell her, and hand her a trowel.

By the second week of the dream, the sprouts come — impossible, luminous green things pushing through ash-gray dirt. Tomatoes. Beans. Sunflowers taller than you are, turning their faces not toward the dimmed sun, but toward each other, like they're listening.

Word spreads the way things do in dreams: instantly, and without anyone speaking. A boy arrives carrying a violin with only two strings. An old man brings a box of books he saved from the flooding. A woman who hasn't spoken since the collapse begins humming while she waters the rows.

You build more than a garden. You build a table in the middle of it, from a fallen door. Every evening, whoever found something that day shares it — a can of peaches, a story, a battery that still holds a little charge so you can listen to static that almost sounds like music.

One night, raiders appear at the edge of the roof. In the logic of nightmares, they should attack. Your heart pounds. You have no weapons.

Instead, you do the bravest, stupidest thing you can think of. You invite them up.

They hesitate. One climbs the ladder, expecting a trap. You hand him a warm tomato, still dusty from the vine. He stares at it like it's a relic from another planet. He takes a bite, and begins to cry.

By the end of the dream, the rooftop is no longer just yours. It's a light. From above — because suddenly you *are* above, floating as dreamers do — you can see other rooftops across the ruined city beginning to glow, one by one. Other gardens. Other tables. Other people who decided the end of the world wasn't the end of *everything*.

You wake up with the phantom taste of tomato on your tongue, and dirt under your fingernails that isn't really there.

And for the whole morning, the real world feels a little less fragile — because you remember what you chose to do when everything was gone.

You chose to plant something anyway.
