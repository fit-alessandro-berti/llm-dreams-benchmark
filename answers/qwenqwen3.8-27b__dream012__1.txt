“Hold the line!” you finally manage, though you are no longer standing. You are sailing through the masts, through the rigging, through the storm itself. The ship is no longer a ship; it is a ribcage of wood and rope, floating on an ocean that keeps changing direction every time you blink.

Your crew stares at you, faces worn smooth like stones, their eyes wide with the need to be led. One of them steps forward, but where his feet touch the deck, flowers bloom—white, impossible flowers, swaying in wind that is not there.

“You’ll find the calm,” he says.

But you know now that the calm is not ahead. It is below.

The sea suddenly stills.

The storm does not stop; it goes quiet, sinking beneath the surface like a held breath. Below the water, lights appear—small blue flames floating in the dark, drifting upward through the waves. They are the dreams of ships that have never made port, and they rise through the hull with a sound like glass bells.

Your crew looks down, afraid.

You take one hand off the wheel.

The helm turns by itself.

The ship begins to sink—slowly, gently, like a ship made of moonlight—and the sea closes over you not with violence, but with welcome. The crew does not scream. They stand shoulder to shoulder, their faces lit from below, as the blue flames rise higher.

You are no longer captain.

You are the sea.
