You wake up in a completely different era, with no modern technology around. As you explore your new surroundings, you realize you need to find out **when** you are—and how to survive long enough to return home.

You step outside the small timber house where you awoke and find a muddy village road crowded with horses, wooden carts, and people dressed in rough wool. Smoke rises from stone chimneys. There are no cars, no power lines, and no familiar signs—only a church bell tolling somewhere beyond the rooftops.

When you ask a passerby what year it is, he stares at you suspiciously.

“The Year of Our Lord, 1348,” he finally says.

The date sends a chill through you. You remember enough history to know what is coming—or perhaps has already begun. Then you notice red marks painted on several doors and hear villagers whispering about a sickness spreading from the southern ports.

You reach instinctively for your phone, but your pocket is empty except for one impossible object: a small silver compass you have never seen before. Its needle does not point north. Instead, it swings toward the forest outside the village.

With nowhere else to turn, you follow it.

Deep among the trees, the compass leads you to the ruins of a stone tower. Inside, carved into the floor, is a circle filled with symbols that resemble both ancient writing and the icons from a computer screen. At the center stands an old woman who looks as though she has been waiting for you.

“You are not the first to fall through time,” she says.

She explains that the tower opens only during moments when history is about to change. To return home, you must recover three objects: something that represents the past, something that could alter the future, and something you are willing to leave behind forever.

You find the first object in the village—a family’s handwritten record of births, deaths, and hopes. The second is a bundle of medicinal notes you write from everything you can remember about hygiene, clean water, and disease prevention. But the third object is more difficult.

At sunset, as the tower begins to glow, you finally understand. You remove the silver compass from your pocket. It brought you here, and as long as you keep it, part of you will always be searching for another life, another time, another chance.

You place it in the circle.

The stones tremble. Light fills the tower, and the sounds of the medieval world dissolve into the hum of electricity.

You wake in your own bed, your phone glowing beside you.

For a moment, you believe it was only a dream. Then you notice mud on your shoes—and on the nightstand lies a fragile piece of parchment bearing a message in faded ink:

**The future remembered us.**
