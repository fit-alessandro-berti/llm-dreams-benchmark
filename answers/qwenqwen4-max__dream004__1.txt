Everything else slides — the hills go from vermilion to a green that tastes like copper, the trees unfold into staircases and fold back into birds — but the canvas stands in the middle of the churn like a held breath. Its whiteness isn't a color. It's the pause before someone says your name.

There's no brush. There's no paint. But when I lift my hand toward it, something gathers at my fingertips, heavy and warm, the way a word gathers before you speak it. I touch the surface and the mark I leave is a sound — a low bell, the note a stairwell makes at night. It hangs there, visible, ringing, slightly too blue.

So I keep going. I paint with the noises I know. A screen door. Rain on a car roof. My mother's laugh from another room, which comes out ochre and spreads faster than I intended, running down the canvas and pooling at the bottom edge where it becomes a small weather system, complete with a horizon.

I decide to paint the landscape. I want to pin one version of it down, to make something here stop changing. But my hand knows a different subject. It draws a door — an ordinary door, cheap hollow-core, with a brass knob gone dull, the exact door of the bedroom I slept in when I was nine. I did not choose it. The canvas seems to have been waiting for it.

The painted knob turns.

Of course it does. That's how things work here. I lean close to see through the crack and the crack is a room and the room is this one, hills flipping through their colors outside its window, and in the middle of the room stands a canvas, and at the canvas stands a figure with its back to me, hand raised, fingertips heavy and warm.

I want to call out. What comes out is a color, one I've never seen and can't hold — it exists only while I'm making it. The figure hears it. The figure's shoulders go still in the way people do when they recognize a voice they'd stopped expecting.

Then the figure turns, and it is me, except younger, except not quite, except wearing an expression I have never worn but have definitely seen — and it lifts its hand and presses its palm flat against the white space beside the door, and I feel the pressure on my own palm, warm, slightly damp, insistent, the way someone wakes you.

The canvas goes soft. It isn't cloth anymore, it's the surface of water held vertically, and my hand is through it up to the wrist, and the cold on the other side is the cold of a bedroom at four in the morning.

I pull back. I don't want to go. There is so much left unpainted; there are sounds I haven't used yet.

But the pull is patient and enormous, and the colors are draining out of the hills like a tide going out flat, and the last thing to go is that one color I made, the nameless one, which stays wet on my fingers.

The ceiling is grey. The light through the blinds is the ordinary gold. Everything holds still, and holds still, and holds still, and I lie there with my hand curled against my chest, keeping it closed a little longer, because something in it is still drying.
