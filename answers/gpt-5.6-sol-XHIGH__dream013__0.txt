You are in a maze filled with complex puzzles. Each puzzle requires intense concentration and logical thinking to solve, and as you move deeper into the maze, you begin to notice that the puzzles are learning how you think.

The first doors are simple. One opens when you arrange seven brass birds from smallest to largest. Another asks you to cross a tiled floor without stepping on a number divisible by the number of letters in your name. You solve these easily, pleased by the crisp click each mechanism makes. Behind you, the walls slide together, erasing the route back.

Farther in, the corridors grow narrow and the puzzles less obedient. A clock with thirteen hands demands the time you were born. A row of glass jars contains different kinds of silence, and you must place them in order from least to most painful. When you hesitate, the jars begin whispering answers in the voices of people you have forgotten. You choose one at random. Somewhere inside the wall, something sighs with relief, and the passage opens.

You tell yourself there must be a center. Mazes always have centers, even if the center is only the reason the maze was built. You begin leaving marks with a piece of chalk you find in your pocket, but whenever you glance back, the marks have changed into tiny equations. Their unknown variables resemble eyes. You try to solve one and discover that it describes the exact corridor in which you are standing, including a small figure bent over the wall. The equation ends with an arrow pointing behind you.

You do not turn around.

Instead, you run. The maze responds by running too. Corners unfold before you; staircases roll upward like tongues. The ceiling drops until you are crawling beneath constellations carved into black stone. Each star is a keyhole. Through one you see your childhood kitchen, sunlit and empty. Through another you see yourself asleep in a bed at the bottom of the sea. Through a third, an enormous eye is already looking back.

At last you enter a circular room. There are no doors, only a wooden table and, on it, a perfect model of the maze. A miniature version of you stands inside the model room, leaning over a still smaller maze. When you lift your hand, the tiny figure lifts its hand. When you lean closer, it looks up—not at you, but past you.

Now you turn around.

A white door has appeared in the wall. Written across it is the final puzzle:

WHAT CAN LEAVE THE MAZE WITHOUT FINDING THE EXIT?

Beneath the words is a keyhole shaped like your shadow. You search the room for clues. You count the stones, trace the grain of the table, study the miniature corridors. The tiny you pounds soundlessly on the glass. The harder you think, the smaller the room becomes. The walls press against your shoulders. Your thoughts circle, double back, and meet themselves at dead ends.

Then you hear breathing from inside the model.

You lift its glass roof. The miniature you scrambles out, warm and trembling, and climbs onto your palm. Up close, it is not quite you. It is much older, with chalk dust in the creases of its face. It points toward the question on the door and then touches your forehead.

“A thought,” it says.

The word is almost too quiet to hear, but the maze hears it. Every lock opens at once.

The walls split apart with a sound like thunder underwater. Behind them are not more corridors but thousands of rooms from your life, stacked impossibly high: classrooms, waiting rooms, houses you only visited once, the back seats of cars at night. People stand inside them holding pieces of unfinished conversations. As the maze collapses, the rooms begin falling toward you.

You push the tiny figure through the white doorway. It grows as it crosses the threshold, becoming your own size, while you shrink. For one suspended moment you face each other, identical except that the other you looks certain. Then the door swings shut between you.

You are inside the model now.

Above you, a vast version of the circular room recedes into darkness. The table stretches like a plain. The white door is no bigger than a grain of rice, but it remains open. Beyond it lies a dawn-colored field, and the other you is walking away through tall grass. You call out, yet your voice emerges as the faint ticking of a mechanism.

The maze begins rebuilding itself around you. Brass birds settle onto their perches. Glass jars fill with silence. The thirteen-handed clock starts counting backward. You understand that the final answer was correct, but answers are not the same as escape. A thought can leave the maze, but the thinker must remain behind to think it.

Then the chalk equations on the walls blink awake. One by one, all their eyes turn toward the open door.

You stop trying to solve them. You close your eyes and imagine the field: the wet grass brushing your knees, the pale sun rising, the weightless figure moving farther from the maze. With every detail, the doorway grows larger. Wind enters the model and scatters the puzzle pieces. The walls become paper. The floor becomes a map. You fold the entire maze smaller and smaller until it fits beneath your tongue.

When you open your eyes, you are standing in the field.

There is no sign of the other you, only a trail through the grass leading toward the horizon. In your pocket, the chalk has become a small white key. You do not know what it opens. Behind you, the maze is gone, though its passages still seem to turn quietly inside your head.

Ahead, the trail divides into countless paths. Each one bears a sign with a different answer. You choose the path with no sign at all, and as you walk, the sun rises backward into the night.
