You are about to give a speech to a large audience. As you step onto the stage, you see the crowd looking expectantly at you, and you begin to speak.

The first word leaves your mouth as a moth. It lifts toward the footlights as if it has always known the way. You try again. Another moth. Then a third. The sentence you rehearsed becomes a pale cloud rising into the rigging.

No one looks alarmed. They lean forward, still expectant, as if this is exactly the speech they came for.

You glance down. Your notes are blank except for one line in your own handwriting: *Tell them what you actually remember.*

So you do. The words that stay words are not the ones you prepared. You hear yourself describing a house you have never lived in, a window that always faced the wrong weather, the sound of someone waiting just beyond a door you never opened. Faces in the third row flicker — your mother at forty, a stranger from a train platform, a childhood friend who never grew older than twelve.

The spotlight warms, then cools. You understand you are not giving a speech so much as returning something you borrowed. When you finally stop, the hall is almost empty. One person is still seated, applauding slowly. You cannot tell if they are you.

High above, the moths keep finding the windows, tapping softly at a night that may or may not be there.
