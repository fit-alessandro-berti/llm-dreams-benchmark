Despite the hardships, you decide to follow the thread of smoke on the horizon. Not because you believe it means survivors—experience has taught you that smoke usually means raiders or burning mines—but because you have begun to dream of a door standing upright in the wasteland, and you need to know if it is a memory or a warning.

The first thing you notice is the silence. In a proper apocalypse, you expect wind, the creak of metal, the distant howl of something hungry. But here, the dust hangs suspended like breath on glass. Your boots leave no prints. You carry a canteen that is somehow always half-full and a photograph of a city that no longer exists.

The smoke leads you to the skeleton of a subway station. The stairs descend, though they should ascend. And there, between two rusted turnstiles, stands the door. It is painted a green that hurts your eyes—not emerald, not jade, but the green of hospital corridors and of things growing in the dark. There is no handle, only a small brass keyhole shaped like a keyhole, not like anything else.

You reach into your pocket and find, not your knife, but a key. You do not remember taking it. Perhaps it was in the dead man's satchel by the river, or perhaps you have carried it since before the sky fell. The metal is warm.

When you turn the key, the door does not open onto a room. It opens onto a sky so blue it feels violent, and beneath it, a field of wheat that no one has planted in twenty years. And standing in the field, with her back to you, is someone wearing your coat. Your exact coat, with the tear in the left pocket and the dried blood on the cuff.

She turns. She has your face, but her eyes are the color of the door.

"You're late," she says. "I've been holding the world together while you slept."

And that is when you understand: the apocalypse was never outside. The dangers were only ever the parts of yourself you refused to walk through. You realize you are not breathing. You have not been breathing since the turnstile.

The wheat begins to whisper in your mother's voice. The sky develops cracks like old porcelain. Somewhere far away, an alarm sounds—not a warning, but an awakening.

You step toward the field.

And wake.
