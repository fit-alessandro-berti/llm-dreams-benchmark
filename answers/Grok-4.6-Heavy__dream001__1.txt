You find yourself in a vast, unfamiliar city, and you realize you have an important meeting in an hour but no idea where it is. You start to walk, then run, then walk again, because running only admits you are already late. The streets refuse a grid. Alleys open like drawers and seal behind you. A café with the same empty chairs appears on every third corner. Your phone offers a map of this city that is also not this city; the blue pin drifts two blocks west of your feet. The calendar says forty minutes, then twelve, then an hour that has not begun. The location field reads only HERE.

You stop a woman in a coat the color of wet slate. She smiles as if you have asked this before. “Third left after the river that isn’t there yet,” she says, and hands you a newspaper dated tomorrow. On the front page is a photograph of you arriving somewhere, late, wearing clothes you do not own. When you look up she is a boy holding a clock with no hands.

A tram arrives without tracks. Everyone inside is already dressed for the meeting you cannot find. They slide over and leave a space that is clearly yours. Outside the windows the city folds: alleys become rivers, rivers become hallways of doors numbered in a system you used to understand. One door has your name on it. You do not knock. From inside comes the sound of a meeting already in progress — your own voice, calm, answering questions you have not been asked yet.

You cannot remember who you are meeting, only that it matters, and that you were supposed to bring the one thing you left in the other city, the one you lived in before the streets learned your name. A tower shows three different times depending on which street you approach it from. You climb anyway. The stairs are made of appointment reminders. At the top there is no city at all, only a long table floating over fog. Every chair is empty except the one at the far end, which is waiting. The sky is the exact color of a held breath.
