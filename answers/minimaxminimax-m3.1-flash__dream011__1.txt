# The Exam

The room smelled of dry-erase markers and old carpet. Thirty-one other students, all of us in our twenties, all of us with our coats folded on the backs of our chairs like children at a recital. Fluorescent light with a faint green cast. A clock over the door that was eleven minutes fast, which I noticed because I had been watching it for eleven minutes.

Course 401: Practical Decision-Making. Final examination. Weight: one hundred percent of career placement.

I'd heard the same sentence from three different people in the year I'd spent in the building: *It's only the exam that matters.* I'd assumed it was a joke about how the lectures went.

"Begin when the proctor says," said the woman at the front. She had a stack of papers bound at the corner with string, and she dealt them out with the efficiency of someone who had done this a thousand times. I was in the third row by the window. The paper landed on my desk and I read Question 1 before I'd decided to read anything.

> **1. Give the name of the place you were most frightened as a child, and the name of the one person who made you that way.**

My hand went cold. I looked up. The girl in the front row had her pencil down. Nobody was writing. The boy on my left turned his paper over, then back, as if checking for a second page.

"Is this—" someone started.

"You may answer in any form," the proctor said. Her voice was unbothered. "There is no partial credit. There is no wrong answer. You have four hours."

That last part should have been reassuring. It wasn't.

I looked at Question 2.

> *Why did you stop calling me on Sundays?*

That was my mother's handwriting. Not her printed name — her actual hand, the way she dragged the tail off a *y* when she was upset, the way she wrote on the little yellow pad by the telephone in the kitchen where I used to sit and pretend to do homework. I hadn't spoken to her in eleven months. I sat in a room with a green fluorescent light and a dead woman asked me why I had stopped calling her on Sundays, and the only person alive who knew that question was me.

Down the row, somebody made a sound like a hiccup that got stuck.

"Question 3," I said, out loud, because I needed to hear something ordinary.

> **3. Define instrumental reason and give one example of its application to a career choice.**
