speak—but instead of words, a small blue bird flies from your mouth.

It circles the microphone once and settles on your shoulder. Nobody in the audience seems surprised. In fact, they all take out notebooks.

You try again.

“The reason we’re gathered here—”

Three more birds burst into the air, carrying the unfinished sentence between them like a ribbon. They fly toward the ceiling, which is no longer a ceiling but a cloudy afternoon sky.

A woman in the front row raises her hand.

“Could you repeat that last part?” she asks.

You open your mouth, and rain begins to fall.

Not heavy rain. Just enough to bead on the microphone and darken the shoulders of your jacket. The audience tilts their faces upward. Someone laughs softly. Someone else closes their notebook, satisfied.

You look down at the speech you prepared. The pages are covered with a map of your childhood neighborhood. A red dot marks the house where you used to live, and as you watch, it moves out through the front door.

You know, somehow, that the dot is you.

The blue bird on your shoulder leans close to your ear.

“You can still catch up,” it says.

At the back of the auditorium, a door opens onto the street from the map. You can see the cracked pavement, the low fence, the tree you remember as enormous.

The audience begins to applaud.

You leave your speech on the podium and walk down the center aisle. As you pass, people tuck things into your hands: a warm stone, a key with no teeth, an orange peeled in one unbroken spiral.

Outside, the rain has stopped. Your younger self is waiting beneath the tree, scuffing one shoe against a root.

“You’re late,” they say.

“I had to give a speech.”

They consider this, then hold out their hand.

“Did you bring anything?”

You give them half the orange, and together you sit on the curb to eat it.
