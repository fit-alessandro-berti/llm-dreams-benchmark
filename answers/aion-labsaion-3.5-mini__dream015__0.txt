The road beneath my boots was cracked asphalt, threaded with weeds that had spent years reclaiming the world. I passed the husk of a gas station, its pumps bent like wilted flowers, and a school bus fused with rust, its yellow paint faded to the color of old bone. My canteen sloshed—half empty. In dreams, you always know exactly how much water you have left.

As I walked, fragments of the old world surfaced like debris in floodwater: a traffic light still cycling green to red for no one, a billboard advertising a vacation to somewhere that no longer existed, a child's bicycle lying in the middle of the road, perfectly upright, as if its rider had simply stepped off and vanished.

The glow grew closer. It was a fire, ringed by figures—survivors, huddled around a meager flame. An old woman saw me first and raised a hand, not in greeting but in warning. Then a man stood, squinting, and called out: "You carrying anything worth killing for?"

"No," I said, and it was true. "Just looking for a reason to keep walking."

He laughed—a dry, cracked sound, like the world itself. "Then sit down. We've got a fire and no answers. Same as everyone left."

I sat. Someone handed me a tin cup of something warm that tasted vaguely of pine needles and hope. The old woman told stories of before—of planes that crossed oceans in hours, of lights that turned night into noon. The younger ones listened like she was describing a religion.

And as the fire burned low, I felt the strange peace that only comes in dreams: the understanding that the world had ended, and yet here we were, telling stories anyway. Perhaps that was the whole point—not rebuilding what was lost, but proving that something in us survived it.

The fire flickered. The faces around it blurred. And somewhere far away—maybe in another world entirely—an alarm clock was ringing.
