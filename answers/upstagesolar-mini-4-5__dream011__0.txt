You reach for the pen, but your hand passes through the paper and the pages drift upward, folding themselves into origami birds. They circle your head, each one beating at a different tempo. The first bird unfolds to show a graph of your life—a line that dips sharply in March, then climbs steadily, then plateaus. The second bird shows your alarm clock at 6:47 a.m. on a Tuesday in 2019. The third bird shows a stranger on a train who says, "You're already doing the work."

The clock on the wall has no numbers, only words: *prepare*, *begin*, *pause*, *continue*, *almost*, *done*.

You turn to the person beside you. It's you, but older, wearing your exam-day clothes. They don't look at the paper. They lean over and whisper, "Section four is the one you forgot to study. Section four is the one you'll remember in ten years."

The words *section four* appear on the paper now, blurred and shifting. Underneath, a single instruction: *Write down what you already know.*

You hesitate. Then you close your eyes, and the exam room falls away—replaced by a quiet kitchen, morning light, and someone at the table who smiles and says, "You don't need to pass. You only need to show up."

The paper turns blank. The clock says *continue*. And somewhere in the dream, your hand finally finds the pen.
