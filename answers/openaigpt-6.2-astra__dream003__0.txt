a small brass moth tucked into one corner. When you brush it with your thumb, it opens its wings. Beneath them, in lettering so tiny you have to hold the box against your nose, are the words: *Knock from the other side.*

You give the lid three tentative taps.

“Come in,” says someone inside.

This strikes you as a perfectly sensible answer until you try to obey it. You turn the box over. You hold it up to the light. At last, feeling a little foolish, you set it on the ground and step onto the lid.

Your foot sinks through as if into warm bathwater.

Inside is your grandmother’s kitchen, exactly as it was except that rain is falling upward outside the window. A little girl sits at the table with her chin in her hand. You recognize the gap in her front teeth before you recognize your own face.

“Took you long enough,” she says. “Did we ever learn to whistle?”

Of all the things you thought she might ask, you haven’t prepared for this one. You pull out the chair beside her and whistle the first tune that comes to mind. She watches your lips. Her first attempt is mostly spit. Her second makes a thin, indignant note, and the two of you laugh so hard your grandmother calls from the pantry to ask what you’ve broken.

“Nothing,” you call back, together.

When you wake, the box is gone. For a moment you lie quite still. Then, just to see, you whistle.
