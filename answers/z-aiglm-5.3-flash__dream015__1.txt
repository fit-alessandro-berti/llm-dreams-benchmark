*Despite the hardships, you decide to* **plant a garden.**

It seems absurd at first—the ground is ash-grey and cracked like old porcelain, and the wind carries the taste of rust. But your hands remember something your mind has forgotten, and you find yourself kneeling in the dirt of what was once a schoolyard, sifting through the debris.

A rusted trowel. Half a watering can. And there, sealed in a glass jar behind the collapsed shelf of a hardware store: seeds. Tomato, basil, sunflower. Someone labeled them long ago in handwriting gone brown with age.

You plant them as the sun bleeds orange through the smoke on the horizon.

Others come. A woman with a scarred face who hasn't spoken in years kneels beside you and begins pulling weeds without a word. A boy brings water he's carried three miles from a well that may not exist anymore—or maybe it does; in this place, you're never certain which memories are real.

Nights are dangerous. You hear *them* moving past the fence line—the ones who decided long ago that kindness was just another resource to consume. Some nights they climb over, and you fight, and sometimes people don't come back.

But the garden grows anyway. That's the strange part. Whole rows vanish overnight, frost arrives out of season, and still something survives—green shoots pushing up through ash like letters from someone who refuses to stop writing.

Then one morning—everything suddenly too vivid, colors saturated, light flowing like honey—you wake *inside* the dream to find sunflowers taller than the fences, taller than the ruins, every face turned toward you. The silent woman is laughing at something. Children you've never met run shrieking with joy between the stalks.

And you understand, the way one understands things only in dreams, that the garden was never really about the food.

Somewhere far away, an alarm begins to ring—

No. Not an alarm. A bird. Just a bird, singing from atop a dead streetlight that has somehow, impossibly, burst into bloom.
