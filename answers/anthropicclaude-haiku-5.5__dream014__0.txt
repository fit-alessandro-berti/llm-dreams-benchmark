The crowd parted like water around a stone, and the nearest of them, a woman with silver threads woven into her coat, caught my sleeve gently. "You look like you've been walking in circles for hours," she said, and her voice had the warmth of a kitchen on a winter morning.

Behind her stood three others: a boy balancing a stack of paper lanterns, an old man with a cane carved like a serpent, and a tall figure whose face I couldn't quite hold onto, though I knew they were smiling.

"Where are you trying to go?" the boy asked.

I opened my mouth to answer and realized I didn't know. The address had been there a moment ago, written on the back of my hand, but now the skin was smooth and blank.

"That's all right," said the old man, tapping his cane twice against the cobblestones. The street answered with a soft chime, like a bell under water, and the buildings on either side leaned inward, their windows lighting up one by one, gold and rose and sea-green. Doors opened where there had been only brick.

"The city does this when someone needs guiding," the silver-threaded woman said. "It's not a maze. It's a conversation. You just have to say something true."

So I told them the truest thing I could find: that I was tired, and that I had been afraid of being forgotten in a place so full of people.

The lanterns in the boy's arms rose into the air, drifting ahead of us like slow fireflies, and the group began to walk, and I found I was walking with them. Each step made the street a little more familiar, as if I had known it all my life and only forgotten the way home. The tall figure fell into step beside me, and at last I saw their face clearly. It was my own, older and calmer, and it nodded as if to say, *you're nearly there.*

The lanterns stopped before a door painted the blue of early morning. The woman pressed my hand. "This one's yours," she said.

I reached for the handle, and the city began, softly, to sing.
