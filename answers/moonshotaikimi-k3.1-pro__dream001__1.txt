You start to ask strangers for directions, but each one points somewhere different. A woman carrying an umbrella full of bees points toward the river. A policeman with no face points at the sky. A small boy simply takes your hand, turns it over, and shows you an address written across your palm in blue ink.

**17 Meridian Street. Top floor.**

You repeat it to a taxi driver, but he shakes his head. “Not while you’re looking at it,” he says, and drives away without wheels.

So you run.

The streets rearrange themselves whenever you turn a corner. Shops become stairwells; stairwells open onto crowded beaches. Every clock shows a different time, except the clocks reflected in windows, which all show that you are already seven minutes late.

At last you find Meridian Street. It is narrower than an alley and stretches vertically up the side of a building. Number 17 waits at the top, its door glowing faintly. You climb, gripping window frames and drainpipes, while below you the city slowly fills with a silver fog.

Inside, there is no elevator, only a reception desk. The receptionist is an old woman knitting something enormous and gray.

“I have a meeting,” you tell her.

She checks a book whose pages whisper as she turns them. “Yes. They’ve been waiting for quite a while.”

“Am I late?”

“That depends,” she says, “on whether you intend to arrive as yourself.”

Before you can ask what she means, the wall behind her opens.

The meeting room contains a long table surrounded by people you almost recognize. One has your childhood haircut. Another wears the coat you lost years ago. At the far end sits someone older than you, their face tired but strangely calm. On the table are objects from your life: a broken watch, an unsent letter, a key whose lock you cannot remember.

The oldest one looks up.

“We were beginning to think you wouldn’t come.”

“I didn’t know where I was going,” you say.

Everyone nods, as though this is the correct answer.

The oldest one pulls out the empty chair beside you and slides a blank sheet of paper across the table. At the top, in your own handwriting, are the words:

**AGENDA: WHAT HAPPENS NEXT**

Somewhere far below, a clock begins to strike. The room trembles. The others start speaking at once, but their voices dissolve into the sound of rain.

Just before you wake, the old woman from reception leans through the doorway and calls after you:

“Don’t worry about the address. Next time, it will find you.”
