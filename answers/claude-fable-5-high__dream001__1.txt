You find yourself in a vast, unfamiliar city, and you realize you have an important meeting in an hour but no idea where it is. You start to walk quickly, because walking quickly feels like the same thing as knowing where you're going.

The streets are wide and clean and lined with buildings made of a pale stone that seems to hum faintly, like a refrigerator in another room. You check your pocket for the address and find a ticket stub, a house key you don't recognize, and a folded piece of paper that says, in your own handwriting, "You'll know it when you see the blue door." This is enormously reassuring for about four seconds.

You ask a woman selling oranges where the meeting is. She weighs an orange in her hand as if the answer has a mass. "Two streets down," she says, "then left at the place where the pharmacy used to be." You thank her and walk on, and you do turn left at a corner that feels vacated, as though a pharmacy had recently been subtracted from it. You are proud of yourself for understanding.

The new street slopes downhill and the buildings grow taller as you descend, leaning inward politely, like people trying to hear you better. Clocks appear on every facade now, which would be helpful except that they all show different times, and one of them shows a small painting of a boat instead of a time. You decide you have forty minutes left. The decision itself seems to make it true.

You realize you don't know what the meeting is about. This bothers you less than it should. What bothers you more is that you can't remember whether you're the one giving the presentation. Your hands feel like they've been holding a folder all morning, a phantom folder, and you keep almost switching it from one hand to the other.

An escalator descends into the sidewalk where no subway entrance should be, and everyone riding it is dressed the way you are, exactly the way you are, and several of them nod at you with the tired solidarity of colleagues. So you get on. The escalator goes down for longer than the city could possibly be deep, past tiled walls displaying advertisements for products you almost remember — a cereal from your childhood, a film that was never made, your own name in a serif font with no explanation.

At the bottom there is a corridor, and at the end of the corridor there is a blue door.

You knew it when you saw it. The note was right. You feel the specific joy of a plan coming together, even though at no point did you have a plan.

The door opens before you knock. Inside is a conference room, long table, water glasses beading with condensation, and every chair is full except one. The people at the table look up. You know none of them, and all of them are familiar — assembled, it seems, from parts: your dentist's eyebrows, a teacher's way of folding her hands, the exact posture of someone you loved once and have not thought about in years.

"You're early," says the man at the head of the table, and everyone smiles with relief, as if your earliness has saved the whole enterprise.

You sit down. The chair fits you the way water fits a stone. Someone slides a folder across the table — the folder, the one your hands remembered — and you open it and find a single page, blank except for a line at the bottom where someone has already signed your name.

"Well," says the man, "shall we begin?"

And you understand, the way you understand things in dreams, without evidence and without doubt, that the meeting was never something you were supposed to find. The meeting was the hour you spent looking. The city arranged itself around your searching like a story arranges itself around a reader. Everyone at the table has just arrived from their own vast, unfamiliar city, their own note in their own handwriting.

You clear your throat to speak, and the room leans in, and the clocks outside all agree at last on a single time, which is now, which was always going to be now —

and somewhere far above, at street level, the woman with the oranges looks up at the sky, and it begins, very softly, to rain.
