The seam of warmth. Just a seam, a hairline of amber light where lid meets base, thin as a scar.

And the patterns — you would swear the patterns shift when you aren't looking directly at them. They crawl like lovers in the dark, rearranging themselves the moment your attention slides off. You try to catch them. You find you've been staring at one corner for a long time without blinking, and your eyes sting with salt, and you don't remember starting to cry.

The box has no keyhole. You check. You check twice, running your thumb around every edge the way you'd check a pocket, and there's nothing — no hole, no slot, no latch, no hinge. Just the seam, and the light in it, and the warm weight of it in your hands, heavier than it should be for its size. Heavier than a box of anything. Heavier like a held breath.

You set it on the floor. You pick it up again. The floor beneath it is not the floor you remember setting it on — the carpet there is a different carpet, the green of a hallway you grew up in, the runner with the worn stripe, but where the runner should be there's the shape of the runner worn into the pile, a ghost of a path that goes toward a door you can't quite see.

Somewhere inside the box, something knocks.

Not loudly. Not desperately. The way a heart knocks, the way a finger taps on a table to say *I'm here, I'm here, are you there.* You hold it against your chest and the knocking stops, and you understand immediately and without effort, the way you understand things in dreams, that the knocking was answering you. That you were knocking too. That you have been knocking for a long time, from the other side of something, for years.

You look at the patterns again. Now that you're not forcing them to be a keyhole, they'd let you see them, and they are not carving. They are handwriting. Your handwriting — the cramped, impatient slant of a school notebook, the margin scrawl of a teenager who wrote in the dark because the light switch didn't work or the light cost money. And the loops of it, the little closed circles of ink, they're not decoration. They're words. Days, maybe. Dates with nothing after them except a small weather symbol — *cloud over sun, cloud over sun, rain, cloud, cloud, a sun so small it's a period.*

You remember the notebook. That's the thing that breaks you open. You remember the kitchen table. You remember a blue plastic cup, the ring of condensation, a radio playing something you can't name but your body can, and a hand — yours, small — writing down the days because you'd decided, with the terrible logic of a child, that if you wrote down enough of them they'd have to keep happening.

And you remember the box. Of course you do. The chocolate box with the picture of the castle on the lid. The one you gave away.

You sit down in the hallway with the green carpet and the runner-worn path and the door you can't see, and you hold the box, and you stop looking for the key.

You're in a dream. Dreams are the one place with no locks, no rules, no physics but the ones you are kind enough to keep. You don't need a key. You never needed a key. You need a *yes*.

So you say it. Not out loud — out loud would be too much like waking. You say it the way you said it then, into the margin of the notebook, in the dark, in the kitchen with the blue cup and the radio: *yes, I'm here, I was here, I know.*

And the lid opens. Not with a click. It simply is open, the way a held breath is released, the way a fist is unclenched after a long time. The amber light spills out and it isn't light, it's warmth, it's the smell of toast and dust and rain on warm pavement, and inside the box there is no key, there is no treasure, there is a small folded room, a kitchen at dusk, a blue cup sweating on the table, a hand writing in the margin, and the hand is writing faster now, filling the page with dates, date after date after date, and you understand that you didn't write them to make the days last.

You wrote them so you'd know where to find yourself.

The knocking begins again, from inside the small room, from the other side of the small room, and this time you lift the box to your ear and you hear it clearly, and you hear what's on the other side of it: your own breathing. You're already here. You've been here the whole time.

You wake up.

Morning, gray, the radiator ticking in the wall like a small persistent heart. Your hands are cupped in front of you as if holding something, and they are warm, and there is nothing in them but the shape of something, which is the shape of everything, which is the shape of a box with no key and a seam of light and a lid that opens the moment you stop hunting for the key.

On the nightstand, there is a small cardboard box with a picture of a castle on the lid.

You've never seen it before.

You open it anyway.
