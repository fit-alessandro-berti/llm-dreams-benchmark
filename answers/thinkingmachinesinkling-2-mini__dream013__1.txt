The walls are not stone; they’re made of your own half-formed thoughts, stacked into corridors labeled with names you almost remember—your childhood room, a street corner, a phone ringing in another life. The first puzzle is a spiral of mirrors, and solving it means admitting what you’re avoiding: an answer you kept secret. The second is a staircase of numbers that spells a countdown, not to danger, but to a moment when you were almost brave enough.

As you move deeper, the puzzles stop being problems to win. They become questions about what you want when no one is grading your effort. A locked door opens when you stop searching for a key and instead describe the feeling behind it; a hallway of echoes responds when you speak aloud; a field of identical statues becomes a crowd reciting the same sentence until it flickers into several different ones.

Then the maze turns—not into a trap, but into a listening chamber. The walls soften, the colors gather, and the next obstacle dissolves into a single luminous question: what are you carrying through the dream? And you realize the maze was never meant to be escaped. It was meant to teach you when to stop avoiding the question, to knock on the door, and to step through lighter—not because the fear has disappeared, but because, for an instant, you can see it clearly enough.
