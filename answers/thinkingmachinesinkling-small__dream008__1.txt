Your boss is waiting, but when you look closer, they are wearing your own face—slightly older, slightly calmer, and holding a clipboard made of grey fog.

You try to ask about the deadline, but your voice comes out as the sound of a printer jamming. The deadline, you see now, is written in wet ink that keeps moving: *Deliver the unbuilt cathedral. By yesterday. At noon.* The clock on the wall is melting, but its hands are still pointing backward.

You sit down. The project is a single sheet of paper, but every time you touch it, it becomes something else—a map of a city that doesn’t exist, a recipe for silence, a blueprint for a stairway that only goes up in dreams.

You start working anyway. Your fingers merge with the keyboard; the keys are little rooms you crawl into. The office begins to breathe. The fluorescent lights become stars that have fallen into a ceiling made of old emails. Your boss nods—except they have turned into a very patient oak tree, and its roots are gently lifting the carpet, and this is fine.

You realize, with a wave of impossible calm, that in dreams, deadlines are just doors that haven’t finished opening. You don’t finish the project; you finish *being* the project. You build a door inside the wall where the conference room should be, and the door opens not onto another office, but onto the smell of rain from your childhood, and the sound of a phone that never rings.

The clock stops. Your boss—now a bird with a briefcase—flies out the window. The impossible deadline passes, but time doesn’t notice; it’s too busy being a river.

You wake up with your hand hovering over your laptop, the echo of urgency still warm in your chest, already dissolving like smoke into the real morning. The real deadline, it turns out, was never the task. It was the moment right before you realized you were dreaming.
