...written in a language you almost recognize. The letters look familiar—shapes you once knew intimately—but they've rearranged themselves overnight into something just beyond your grasp. When you look directly at them, the words shift, like fish scattering in clear water.

You glance up. The clock above the blackboard has no hands, only a slowly darkening face, as if the room itself is running out of time. Around you, the other students write calmly, pens whispering across paper. One of them turns and smiles—it's your third-grade teacher, though she's wearing a graduation gown and has your father's laugh.

Question one reads: *Explain everything.*

You pick up your pen and find it has gone soft, more crayon than pen, leaving waxy smears. You try anyway. You write the first sentence and watch it sink into the page like water into sand. The words vanish. You write faster. The page drinks everything.

Somewhere behind you, a voice—the proctor's? your own?—says gently, *"You should have started studying years ago."* You want to turn around and argue that you did study, that you know this material, but when you look back down, the exam has changed. Now every question is the same question, repeated down the page:

*What were you doing instead?*

And the terrible thing—the thing that makes your chest tighten in that specific dream-way—is that you remember exactly what you were doing instead. All of it. Every golden afternoon. And none of it seems worth trading for this moment: this silent room, this handless clock, this page that refuses to hold a single word.

You raise your hand to ask for another copy of the exam, and as you lift it, you notice your hand has turned translucent. Through it, you can see the desk. Through the desk, the floor. Through the floor, a warm white light rising up to meet you—

—and just before it reaches you, you feel, impossibly, a pang of regret that you never answered question two.
