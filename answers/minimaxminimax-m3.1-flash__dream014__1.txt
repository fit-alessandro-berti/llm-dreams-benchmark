## and you say yes

That's how the dream begins — not with the city, not with the loneliness, but with the word. *Yes.* The strange part of a dream isn't the offer. It's how easily, how *happily*, you answer it.

You are standing at the corner of a street you have never seen and will never see again, which is how you know. The light here is late and gold and slightly too thick, like honey poured over the rooftops. Buses sigh at the curb. A thousand signs flicker in a language of pure suggestion — arrows with no words under them, platforms with no platforms on them. Everyone moves with purpose. You are the only one who has stopped.

And you have stopped for a long time.

Then, from the crowd, a small clearing happens. Not much — the width of a coat sleeve. A woman in a green coat with a string of brass bells in her hand. A boy with a red balloon, breathing it in and out, breathing it in and out. A tall man in a paint-spattered uniform, smelling faintly of turpentine and rain. A girl with a bicycle and a basket of oranges. A dog who has decided, without discussion, that he belongs to you.

None of them asks your name.

None of them asks where you're going — which is the question you have been carrying like a stone in your chest, the one that has made you stop in the first place.

They simply make room.

You walk. This is the dream's oddest gift: the city opens for you the way a hand opens. The green-coat woman sets her bells on a café table and they ring once, quietly, like a knuckle knocked on a door. The boy walks backward in front of you, holding the balloon's string out behind him "so you can see where we came from." The man in paint-stained coveralls tells you he repaints the walls of buildings no one looks at, and that the work is not to be seen but to keep the rain off. The girl trades you an orange. It is cold in your hand. It tastes like the whole afternoon.

And somewhere — you are not sure when this began — you understand that you are not being *led*. You are being *accompanied*. There is a difference, and the difference is the entire point.

"Do you know where we're going?" you ask at last, and the old woman laughs with her whole chest.

"You're not lost," she says. "You're new."

At the river, the traffic is a single long chord of noise, and the moon comes down low enough to sit in the water like a coin in a pocket. The boy points at it, delighted: "It followed you. That means it can't be far."

The dream does something cruel and kind at once. It shows you — quickly, in the way dreams show you — that you have been standing at this corner for years. That the crowd went around you because crowds do. That you mistook the noise of a city for the noise of being alone, when it was only the sound of a great many people each carrying their own small weather.

And then the ring of bells fades, and the four of them begin to blur, and you understand with a lurch that they were never strangers, that they were — what? Your neighbors. Your colleagues. The woman at the bus stop who has been in the same rain as you for eleven months, the kid on the corner with the balloon, the man who paints the walls of the building you wake up in every day. The city has been full of people who would have walked with you if you'd ever let them.

The dream thins toward morning. The gold goes gray, then silver. The bells ring one last time from very far away.

You open your eyes.

Your ceiling. Your ordinary, unmagical ceiling, with its small crack shaped like nothing at all.

And on the other side of the room, still half in sleep, your hand is reaching toward your phone — because *there* is a message, waiting, sent by someone whose name you have looked at a hundred times without ever speaking it. *Hi. Do you want to grab lunch? I know a place.*

You have slept past the point of no. You read it again. And again. The morning is doing something it doesn't usually do: standing in the doorway, patient, with the light behind it.

You think of the orange, cold in your hand. Of a balloon held out backward so you could see where you'd come from. Of a woman saying *you're not lost, you're new.*

And you —

**— you say yes.**
