The canvas is perfectly white—not empty, but waiting. When you step closer, it ripples like water, and your reflection appears wearing a face you almost recognize. The sky above you folds into green triangles, then melts into violet circles. A tree nearby sighs and turns into a staircase.

A voice comes from the canvas, though its lips do not move.

“Paint what you remember,” it says. “But not what happened. What it felt like.”

You reach for a brush, but the nearest one is growing from your wrist. Its bristles are made of tiny silver feathers. You dip it into a puddle beside you and discover the puddle holds blue, then gold, then the smell of warm bread. When you touch the canvas, the first stroke becomes a doorway.

Through it, you see a room from your childhood, except the walls are made of music and the furniture floats politely in midair. Someone is laughing in the next room. You know you should go to them, but the floor keeps rearranging itself into questions.

So instead, you paint a small red moon. It rolls across the canvas and bumps into a black square, which suddenly opens like an eye.

“Careful,” whispers the canvas. “That one is yesterday.”

The eye blinks. Inside it, you see yourself at age seven, holding a cracked crayon, drawing a sun with too many points. You remember being proud of it. You remember no one else seeing it. You paint the sun anyway, larger now, until its rays spill beyond the frame and warm your hands.

All around you, the world begins to settle. The shifting colors choose names. The shapes stop trembling. Even the rain hangs still, like beads on invisible string.

Then the canvas lifts from its frame and wraps itself around you—light as a blanket, endless as a thought. The last thing you see is your own hand finishing one final stroke: a door left open.

When you wake, there is a smear of impossible color on your fingertips, and somewhere in the room, very faintly, a red moon is rolling beneath the bed.
