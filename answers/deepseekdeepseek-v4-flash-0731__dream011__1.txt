the questions are not questions. They are scenes from your life, arranged in the order of every time you traded your heart for approval.

Question 1: you are nine, hiding a drawing because someone laughed.

Question 2: you are sixteen, deleting a voice note recorded at midnight because it sounded too honest.

Question 3: you are here, now, staring at a page that has started to blur into a small door.

The proctor looks like your childhood teacher but has your own eyes. She taps a pencil made of bone and says, “You’ve been preparing for this your whole life.”

You open the door on the page.

On the other side, you are sitting at this same desk, dreaming this same dream, but the paper in your hands says:  
*You passed the moment you stopped performing.*

The bell rings. It sounds like a train leaving without you. Then a softer voice—yours, but younger—whispers,

“Don’t wake up yet. We haven’t reached the beautiful part.”

The exam dissolves into a field of wildflowers, each blossom a question you no longer need to answer.

You lie down in the grass.

The future career was never the point. The point was allowing yourself to be seen, even by you.

And when you finally wake, there is an answer in your mouth that tastes like cold water.

You don’t know the question.

But you know it was yours.
