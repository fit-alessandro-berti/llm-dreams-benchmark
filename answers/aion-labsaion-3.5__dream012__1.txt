"All hands!" your voice cuts through the howl of the wind, though you don't remember opening your mouth. "Secure the mainsail! To the rigging!"

The crew moves as one, scrambling across a deck that tilts at impossible angles. You notice, in the way one only notices things in dreams, that the deck tilts but you never slide. The storm wants you to fall, but the ship holds you like a promise.

Lightning splits the sky, and in that white instant you see the faces of your crew — your third-grade teacher, your grandfather who died before you were born, a stranger you passed on the street last Tuesday. They all wear the same expression: trust, absolute and unearned.

"Captain," says the old man at the bow, water streaming down his face though no rain touches him, "which way?"

You look at the compass, but the needle spins wildly, pointing at everything at once — at the storm, at the sky, at you. So you do the only thing a captain can do when the instruments fail.

You point straight into the heart of it.

"Together!" you shout, and the word comes out golden, an actual rope of light that each crew member grabs hold of. The wave rises before you — taller than the mast, taller than the sky, a wall of black-green water crowned with white fire — and you steer into it, not because you're brave, but because there is nowhere else to go but through.

And here is the strange part, the part you'll try to remember when you wake: the wave isn't cold. When it crashes over the deck it's warm as bathwater, soft as a blanket, and it doesn't smash the ship — it lifts it. The whole vessel rises on the swell as if it has finally remembered how to fly. The crew cheers. The storm, offended, begins to shrink.

The sea calms. The clouds part like curtains drawn by invisible hands. And the water, which should be gray and churning, is instead full of stars — not reflections, but actual stars, as though the ocean and the night sky traded places while you weren't looking.

"Captain," your grandfather says, clapping a hand on your shoulder, "you brought us through."

And just as you open your mouth to answer — to say you didn't, that it was all of them, that you don't know how, that you're not really a captain at all — you feel the pillow against your cheek, hear the ordinary sounds of your own bedroom, and the dream lets you go the way all dreams do: gently, and slightly before you're ready.
