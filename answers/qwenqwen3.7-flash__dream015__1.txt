...decide to plant a tree.

Not just any sapling. It's a gnarled little thing you pried from the skeleton of a collapsed conservatory, its roots tangled in rotting burlap, leaves the color of oxidized pennies. You know the earth here is baked brittle, that the aquifers have long since swallowed themselves, and that the Glass Walkers patrol the canyon ridges, hunting anything that moves. But dreams don't obey supply lines. They answer to gravity of a different kind.

You kneel. Your boots sink into dust that tastes like old iron. With bare hands, you pry open the cracked crust. Every strike of knuckle against stone rings like a struck bell. A vulture circles once, twice, then gives up and settles on a leaning streetlamp. You think: *I am out of my depth*. You think: *Good*.

You carve a hollow. Drop the roots in. Pour the last of your canteen over them, watching the dark stain spread like a confession. Then you pack the soil back down, pat it flat, and press your palm against the center until your breath syncs with the wind.

Waiting in a dream is never idle. It's a form of attention.

The ground shivers. Not an earthquake. Something deeper, like a sigh trapped for decades finally finding a throat. Hairline fractures bloom across the hardpan. Then, without rustling or protest, a stem uncurls. It thickens. Branches split like lightning caught mid-fall. Leaves flush green, then silver, then something between starlight and soap film. Within heartbeats, fruit appears: smooth, teardrop-shaped, hovering an inch above their sockets.

One detaches. Drops into your cupped hands. Warm. Pulsing. You crack the shell.

Inside isn't flesh or juice, but a scene: a subway car rolling through summer, sunlight cutting through dusty windows, a stranger humming off-key. Safety without ownership. Joy without debt. You close your eyes around it, and when you open them, the tree is gone. Only the crater remains, ringed with morning dew that wasn't there a moment ago.

The sky fractures into bruised purple and copper. Far off, a warning horn moans, but the pitch bends, warps, resolves into something like a choir practicing scales. You stand. Brush the ash from your trousers. Your pack is lighter, but your chest feels heavier in the best way.

You step toward the ridge, toward the walkers, toward whatever comes next. You don't know if seeds remember what they were meant to become. But in this dream, planting was never about harvest. It was proof that you still believed in tomorrow enough to waste water on it.
