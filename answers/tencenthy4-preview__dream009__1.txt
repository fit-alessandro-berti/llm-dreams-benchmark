You wake up in a completely different era, with no modern technology around. As you explore your new surroundings, you realize you need to **find a way back — but first, you need to understand where (or when) "back" even is.**

The air smells different here — woodsmoke and horse manure and something green, like crushed herbs. The ground beneath your feet is uneven cobblestone, worn smooth in the center from centuries of footsteps and cart wheels. People pass you in clothing that looks hand-stitched, layered wool and linen in earth tones, and they speak in a cadence you almost recognize but can't quite place — like a song you heard once as a child.

Your hands feel strange. Lighter. No phone. No watch. You pat your pockets out of instinct and find — fabric, coarse and simple. No seams where a pocket for a device should be.

A church bell rings somewhere in the distance, and the sound is enormous in the silence that follows. People around you pause mid-stride, some crossing themselves, others just tilting their heads toward the sound like they're listening for something in it.

You try to remember how you got here. There's a gap where the memory should be — like trying to recall the last few seconds before falling asleep. Was there a door? A light? A voice saying your name?

A woman at a nearby market stall is watching you. Not suspiciously — more like she's trying to place you. "You're not from this parish," she says. It isn't a question.

You open your mouth to respond, to explain, to ask *what year is it* — but your voice catches. Because the real question, the one that sits in your chest like a stone, is: **Are you dreaming someone else's dream, or did you leave your own body somewhere, still asleep, still waiting to be woken up?**

The woman is still watching. Waiting. And somewhere in the distance, that bell, or another one, begins to ring again — but slower this time. Deliberate. Like it's counting.

And you notice, with a chill that has nothing to do with the weather, that you're counting along with it.
