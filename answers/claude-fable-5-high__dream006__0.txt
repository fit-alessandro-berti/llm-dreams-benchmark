You are about to give a speech to a large audience. As you step onto the stage, you see the crowd looking expectantly at you, and you begin to speak — but no sound comes out. You clear your throat and try again, and this time the words emerge, but they are not your words. They are in a language you have never learned, and yet the audience nods along, leaning forward in their seats as though you are saying something profound.

You reach into your jacket for your notes and pull out a handful of leaves instead. Dry, autumn leaves, veined with gold. You spread them on the podium and, strangely, this feels correct. You lift one to the light and the audience murmurs approvingly. The microphone bends toward you like a curious flower.

The hall is larger than it was a moment ago. The back rows have receded into a soft darkness, and you realize the room has no walls anymore — the seats simply continue outward into a field at dusk. Some of the audience members are people you know: your third-grade teacher sits in the front row taking notes, and beside her is someone who might be your grandfather, or might be a man from a photograph you saw once. He smiles at you with great patience.

You keep speaking the unknown language, and now you begin to understand it too, a half-second after each word leaves your mouth. You are telling them about a door. A door you have been carrying with you your whole life, folded up small in your pocket like a handkerchief. You take it out to show them — and there it is, unfolding in your hands, growing until it stands upright on the stage beside you, freestanding, leading nowhere and everywhere.

The audience rises to its feet. You think they are applauding, but they are not: they are turning, all of them, to look at the door. It swings open on its own. Through it you can see the sea — not a picture of the sea, but the sea itself, gray-green and breathing, and the smell of salt rolls out over the podium and the leaves scatter.

One by one, the audience files up onto the stage and passes through the door. Your teacher pauses to tell you that you always did speak well, even when you said nothing at all. Your grandfather squeezes your shoulder as he passes, and his hand is warm and weighs nothing.

When they have all gone through, the hall is empty and small again, just a room with folding chairs. You understand that the speech is over and that it was, everyone agreed, the best you have ever given. You step through the door last, closing it gently behind you.

On the other side there is no sea. There is your childhood kitchen, and it is morning, and someone you love is calling your name from another room, and the light through the window is the exact color of waking up.
