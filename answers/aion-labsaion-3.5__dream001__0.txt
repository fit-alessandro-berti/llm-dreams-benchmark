You start to run, but the city has other plans. The streets rearrange themselves beneath your feet like a shuffled deck of cards — the avenue you were sprinting down is suddenly a narrow alley, then a staircase leading down into a subway station that smells like your grandmother's kitchen.

You check your phone. The screen shows the time as "soon" in swirling letters. The meeting invitation has no address, only a feeling — you somehow *know* this, the way you know things in dreams, with bone-deep certainty and no evidence at all.

A woman with the face of every teacher you've ever had sits at a folding table in the middle of the subway platform, selling maps. "You'll need this," she says, handing you a map that, when you unfold it, is blank except for a red dot labeled YOU ARE HERE, and the dot is moving even though you're standing still.

You follow the dot.

Up an escalator that deposits you on the roof of a cathedral. Across a bridge made of piano keys that plays a melody you recognize but can't place — something from childhood, maybe, or something you've never heard. The buildings grow taller and lean toward you as you pass, eavesdropping.

You ask a man walking six identical dogs where the meeting is. "Everyone's looking for it," he says, not unkindly. "You're early, though. Or very late. It's hard to say." The dogs all turn their heads at once to look at you, and their eyes are the faces of clocks, each showing a different hour.

You start to panic — the classic dream panic, where your legs turn to syrup and the more urgently you need to move, the slower everything becomes. The city notices. The sidewalk tilts helpfully, becoming a slide, then a river current, carrying you through streets that have softened like candle wax.

And then, a door. Ordinary. Beige. Standing alone in a plaza with no building attached to it, just a door and a frame and beyond it, warm light.

You know — with that dream-certainty again — that this is the place.

You open it, and inside is a conference room, and seated around the long table is everyone you've ever known, at every age they've ever been. Your third-grade best friend. Your grandfather, young again. Strangers you've passed in grocery stores and never forgotten.

The seat at the head of the table is empty. A name card waits there, but when you look at it, the letters rearrange themselves into a question you can't quite read.

"Sit down," everyone says in unison, warmly. "You're right on time."

And you realize the meeting was never in the city at all.

You sit.

The alarm goes off.
