...find the watchmaker at the edge of the harbor before the bells strike six.

You don’t know who told you this, or why it feels so urgent, only that a heavy brass key sits warm in the pocket of your wool coat. Around you, the air smells sharply of coal smoke, salt spray, and damp cobblestones. Horse-drawn carts rattle past, their iron wheels sparking against the stone, and merchants shout over baskets of silver fish and dark loaves of bread. No hum of electricity, no glow of glass screens—just the hiss of gas lamps waking up in the thickening fog.

You hurry down an alley that slopes steeply toward the sea. With every turn, the city shifts slightly, as if it hasn’t quite finished deciding what century it belongs to. A stone archway from an ancient ruin opens onto a row of narrow, timber-framed houses leaning over the street like gossiping neighbors. People brush past you in heavy cloaks and wide-brimmed hats; when you try to meet their eyes, their faces blur like wet ink, except for one old woman selling glass jars filled with pale, flickering fireflies.

"You're late," she murmurs without looking up. "The tide is already in the square."

Sure enough, dark water laps at the heels of your leather boots. The ocean isn't rushing in—it's simply rising, quiet and glassy, reflecting the bruised violet sky. You break into a run, though your legs move with that strange, syrupy heaviness, as if you’re wading through knee-deep water even on dry stone.

Ahead, perched on pilings above the swelling harbor, stands a crooked wooden shop. A painted sign swings slowly in the wind, showing a crescent moon tangled in gears. Warm amber light spills from the mullioned windows.

You push the door open. A little brass bell chimes, and the sound echoes far too long, stretching into a low, resonant hum.

Inside, hundreds of clocks line the walls—pendulums swaying out of sync, wooden cuckoos frozen mid-song, astrolabes turning silently in the rafters. Yet the room is completely quiet. Behind the cluttered workbench stands a tall figure in a leather apron, peering through a jeweler's loupe at a tiny, glowing sphere.

When he lifts his head, you feel a jolt of dizzying recognition, though you still can’t place his name.

"Ah," he says softly, holding out an ink-stained hand. "You brought it."

You reach into your coat and place the warm brass key in his palm. He doesn't use it on a door or a lock; instead, he inserts it into the back of a tall grandfather clock standing in the corner, its face completely blank—just polished, empty glass.

As he turns the key, a deep *click* vibrates through the floorboards. The blank glass begins to shimmer, swirling like water, until it clears into a faint, sunlit image of a familiar ceiling, a tangled duvet, and the steady blinking light of a digital clock on a nightstand.

Outside, the harbor bells finally begin to toll—one, two, three—each strike louder and closer, shaking the dust from the rafters, rattling the glass jars, blurring the edges of the wooden walls until the chimes flatten into a sharp, rhythmic beeping, pulling you upward out of the fog.
