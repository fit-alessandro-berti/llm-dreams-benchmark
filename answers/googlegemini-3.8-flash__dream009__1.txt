You stand at the edge of a dense, primeval forest that breathes with a heavy, mossy petrichor. Before you lies a settlement of low-roofed timber and thatch, nestled against a jagged, mist-wrapped coastline. There is no distant drone of highway traffic, no hum of electricity, no airplanes scarring the vast indigo sky. The air is startlingly crisp, smelling purely of woodsmoke, salt brine, and damp earth. 

You look down at yourself. Your modern clothes are gone, replaced by a tunic of coarse, undyed wool bound with a strip of cracked leather. When you reach into your pocket for the instinctual comfort of your phone, your fingers brush only a smooth, carved river stone. 

The sun is dipping below the ocean, dragging long, bleeding shadows across the packed dirt paths. Figures move between the huts—tall, wrapped in furs and woven cloaks, carrying bundles of dried fish and bronze-tipped spears. You need food, you need fire, and most of all, you need to hide your ignorance. 

Heart pounding, you take a hesitant step forward. A burly man carrying an armload of split pine turns his head. His eyes lock onto yours. You brace yourself to plead for mercy or feign muteness, dreading the moment you have to explain that you fell asleep centuries in the future.

He stops, grunts, and tosses a thick log at your chest. 

Your hands fly up and catch it automatically, the rough bark biting into your skin with shocking, vivid reality. 

"Daydreaming again, are you?" the man mutters in a language that should be entirely foreign to you, yet your mind translates it effortlessly, as if you’ve heard his voice for twenty years. "Hurry with the wood. The tide is coming in, and the elders want the beacons lit before the fog rolls thick."

You turn to argue, to say that a mistake has been made, but as you open your mouth, you try to summon the name of your old street. 

It’s gone. 

You try to remember the glow of a computer screen, the face of your best friend, the sound of a siren. They dissolve like mist beneath the sun, leaving behind only the cold wind off the water, the weight of the timber in your arms, and the quiet, inescapable certainty that this is not a dream you are going to wake up from—this is the life you forgot you had.
