...plant a garden inside the hollowed-out skeleton of a grand cathedral.

The sky above is a perpetual bruised purple, and the ground below is buried under centuries of ash. But here, where the stained glass has shattered onto the marble floor, beams of pale, uncorrupted sunlight still manage to cut through the mist. 

Instead of hoarding weapons or hiding in the dark, I spend my days gathering whatever memory of life the earth still holds. I haul rich soil from the dry riverbeds, carrying it up the cracked stone steps in rusted buckets. I trade clean water with passing wanderers not for fuel or steel, but for forgotten seeds they carry in their pockets—creased packets of heirloom tomatoes, wild lavender, and apple pips that have slept through the collapse.

In the dream, time moves with the fluid logic of water. Vines begin to crawl up the fluted pillars, wrapping around the stone saints until they are draped in living green. When the ash storms howl through the ruined city, the thick foliage inside the cathedral filters the air, making it smell sweet and damp—like earth after a summer rain.

Soon, the dangers outside begin to change. The shadows and scavengers who roam the jagged streets are drawn to the scent of blooming jasmine and wet moss. They come with raised guards, expecting a fortress, but find only an unlocked wooden door and rows of green leaves unfurling toward the sky. They drop their defenses at the threshold, sitting quietly among the ferns, remembering what the world used to feel like.

I stand at the center of the nave, handing out ripe, red fruit from the branches. In this ruined world, we are not just surviving anymore; we are remembering how to live, one blossom at a time.
