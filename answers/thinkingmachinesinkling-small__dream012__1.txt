...and you feel the old, heavy weight of command dissolve into something older—an instinct that isn't bravado, but belonging.

You take the wheel. Not with a shout, but with a steadying breath that seems to travel through the planks beneath your boots, branching out to each of your crew. Their faces, lit by frantic lantern light, find yours—not for rescue, but because they recognize the shift. You are no longer asking them to survive the storm; you are showing them that the storm *is* them, too.

The waves rise like cathedral arches of black water. The wind screams in a language that sounds like your own unspoken doubts. And yet, you don't steer around them. You steer *into* their heart. You tell them—your voice carrying clearer than thunder—to tie themselves not to the mast, but to the idea of the ship itself. That they are not passengers of chaos; they are the marrow of this vessel.

As you do, the dream bends. The turbid sea stops being water. It becomes something between memory and glass—each crest holding the face of someone you once loved, or a place you forgot. The crew stops trembling. One by one, they begin to paint, to sing, to weave the storm into something else. The ship doesn’t break upon the waves; it unfolds within them.

When the sky finally tears open, it does not rain. It exhales. And there, at the center of the tempest—where the eye should be—there is no silence, but a door made of your own reflection, waiting for you to choose whether to captain the storm, or to become it.

You choose to keep sailing. And in that choice, the dream wakes—not with terror, but with the strange, lingering feeling that you have already been where you were meant to go.
