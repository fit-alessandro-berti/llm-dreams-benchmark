...and the challenges stop making sense in a way that everyone around you treats as perfectly reasonable.

The first round is a written exam. The paper is warm, like something recently alive. Your pencil writes only in small drawings — a chair, a chair, a chair, forty chairs — and the proctor, who is also your grandmother, though she has never met you, nods and says *good, good, you're the only one who understood the question.*

The second round is held in a hallway. It is the hallway of a school you have never attended, and you know with total certainty which locker is yours, and you cannot open it, and the other contestants are all wearing your coat. They are kind about it. One of them helps you carry the water. There is a great deal of water and no container for it, and you carry it anyway, cupped in your hands, up a staircase that keeps offering one more step just as you reach the top, and the whole time you are thinking: *this is fine, this is the normal part.*

Somewhere in there you look down at your badge to check the job title, because you have begun to suspect you never actually applied. The badge says LIGHTHOUSE KEEPER. You look again: ASSISTANT TO THE SEA. Again, in smaller letters: *the person who stays until everyone else has gone home.* You decide not to look a fourth time.

The other contestants thin out, not by losing but by simply not being in the next room, the way people aren't. A man you were competing against quite fiercely is now a coat rack, and you feel no grief about this, only a mild sense that you owe him an apology.

The final round is a small office with two chairs and a window showing weather from a different season. The interviewer has your face but older, tired in a way you recognize from photographs. She has a folder with nothing in it. She says, "We're very impressed. We just need to know what you actually want."

And you open your mouth and the words are not there. Not forgotten — *not manufactured.* You have all the language for chairs and hallways and the temperature of exam paper, and none for this. You sit there with your hands on your knees, making a shape with your mouth, and the rain outside goes upward, and she waits with terrible patience, and you understand that this was the competition, all of it, the whole time, and you are failing it in the only way that counts.

Then she says, gently, "You start Monday," and stands, and shakes your hand, and you have won.

And the horror of it arrives all at once — *Monday, forever, a job I never named, a room I will not be able to leave* — and you try to ask what the hours are, and the office is a bedroom, and the light is the wrong color, and there is a sound repeating that is not part of the dream.

You wake with the shape of an answer still in your mouth, and nothing in it.
