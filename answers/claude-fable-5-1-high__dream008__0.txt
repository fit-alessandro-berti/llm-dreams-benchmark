You are at your workplace, and suddenly, you are given a project with an impossible deadline. Your boss is waiting for you to start, and you nod, because nodding is what you do here, and you pull the folder toward you across the desk.

The folder is heavier than it should be. When you open it there is only one sheet inside, and the sheet says, in a typeface you have never seen on any office printer: FINISH BEFORE IT IS DUE. There is no date. You look up to ask when it is due and your boss is standing exactly where he was, hands folded, patient, and he says, "Before it is due," as though that were a time.

You turn to your computer. The screen is on but the desktop is not yours. The icons are all the same small grey rectangle, dozens of them, and when you hover over one it says Project. You hover over another. Project. You click one and it opens a spreadsheet where the columns are labelled with hours and the rows are labelled with your name, again and again, down further than you can scroll.

Behind you the office has begun to fill. You didn't hear anyone come in but every desk is occupied now, and everyone is typing. Not the ragged, stop-and-start typing of people thinking. A single steady rhythm, like rain on a tin roof, all of them together. You try to type too, to join the sound, and your fingers land on the keys and the keys go down soft and slow, like pressing into bread. Nothing appears on the screen. You press harder. A letter appears, then dissolves.

"Are you starting?" your boss says. He hasn't moved. His voice arrives from directly beside your ear though he is on the other side of the desk.

"I'm starting," you say.

You decide you need paper. Real paper, a pen. You stand up, and standing takes a long time, as if the chair is reluctant to let you go, and you walk toward the supply cupboard at the end of the row. The row is longer than it was this morning. You pass desk after desk, and each person you pass is you. Not a resemblance. You. Some are younger, wearing the shirt you wore to your interview. One is older and has stopped typing and is just looking at the screen with a very quiet face. None of them look up. The rhythm doesn't break.

The supply cupboard is a door, and then it is a corridor, and then it is the stairwell of the building where you went to school, with the same green paint and the same smell of floor polish. You climb, because down feels like the wrong direction for a deadline. The stairs turn and turn. Each landing has a clock, and each clock shows a different time, and none of the times is later than the last. You are, you realise, going backwards through the afternoon.

At the top there is a small room with a window. Your boss is there. Of course he is there. He is sitting on a child's chair with his knees up near his chest and he does not look ridiculous; he looks like he has always sat this way.

"Have you started?" he says.

"I'm trying to find out what it is," you say. "The project. Nobody told me what it is."

He considers this. Outside the window it is snowing, though it is summer, and the snow is falling upward, very gently, returning to a sky that wants it back.

"It's the thing you've been meaning to do," he says. "You know the one."

And you do know the one. You feel it, all at once, in the chest, the way you feel a name you've forgotten come back to you in the shower. It is not a spreadsheet. It has never been a spreadsheet. It is the letter, or the call, or the room you have not walked into, or the sentence you have been holding in your mouth for years. It is different for every version of you sitting at every desk downstairs, and it is the same.

"That's not a work project," you say.

"No," he agrees, kindly. "But it has a deadline."

You look at the single sheet, which you are still holding, though you don't remember bringing it. FINISH BEFORE IT IS DUE. You understand now that the deadline is not impossible because it is too soon. It is impossible because it is unknown, and because you have been treating unknown as the same thing as far away.

The typing downstairs has stopped. You notice its absence the way you'd notice a fridge going quiet. In the silence you can hear the snow, which is not a sound snow makes, but here it makes a sound like pages being turned.

"I'll need to leave the office," you say.

"You've never been in the office," your boss says, and he stands, and he is your height now, and he opens the window, and the upward snow comes in and does not melt on the floor but rises, past your face, into the ceiling, and through it.

You don't climb out the window. That would be a different dream. Instead you go back down the stairs, and this time the clocks agree with each other, and the corridor is a corridor, and the office is only an office, ordinary and fluorescent and half empty the way it is late in the day. Your desk is there. Your computer shows your own desktop, your own files. The folder is gone.

There is one thing on the desk that wasn't there before: a pen, uncapped, and beside it a blank sheet of the same strange heavy paper. You sit down. The chair lets you.

You don't know what you will write. You pick up the pen anyway, and the moment the nib touches the paper you feel the deadline move, not closer, not farther, just move, the way something does when it stops being a wall and becomes a door.

You start.
