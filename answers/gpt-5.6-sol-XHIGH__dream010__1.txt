You are participating in a competition where the grand prize is your dream job. As the competition progresses, you face various challenges and begin to notice that none of the other contestants ever blink.

The competition takes place in an office tower with no outside walls. Every floor is a different test. On the first, you must answer a telephone before it rings. On the second, you are given a glass briefcase full of seawater and told to carry it upstairs without waking the fish. On the third, the judges ask you to explain the seven-year gap in your resume, although you have no memory of those years and the resume is written in your childhood handwriting.

Somehow, you keep advancing.

At the end of every round, a brass elevator arrives and the losing contestants step inside. They smile with professional graciousness as the doors close. The elevator indicator points down, but the numbers above it rise: 40, 81, 162. Once, just before the doors meet, you glimpse one of the losers standing in a sunny kitchen, older and happier, flour on her hands. Then the elevator seals and drops upward with the sound of distant applause.

Your closest rival is a man named Ellis, who wears your father's green tie and insists you lent it to him tomorrow. Between rounds, he gives you useful advice. Never accept coffee from the judges. Do not sit in the red chair. If anyone asks what you wanted to be when you were nine, lie.

“What did you want to be?” you ask him.

He looks almost surprised. “You,” he says.

The next challenge is held in a vast open-plan office where the desks stretch beyond the horizon. At each desk sits a version of you doing a job you once imagined wanting: architect, marine biologist, orchestra conductor, owner of a small bookshop where it is always raining. Some are exhausted. Some do not look up. One version has built a nest beneath the desk from shredded performance reviews and is sleeping inside it.

The judges give you a silver stamp and say you have ten minutes to decide which versions are redundant.

You walk the aisles, stamp growing heavier in your hand. The architect slides a blueprint toward you. It is a plan of this office, except the room at the center is labeled YOUR ACTUAL LIFE. The conductor raises her baton, and every keyboard begins typing in unison. The marine biologist opens a drawer in which a dark ocean moves under moonlight.

You refuse to stamp anyone.

A siren sounds. The judges confer behind masks shaped like calm human faces. At last, the tallest judge declares that refusal demonstrates leadership. You have won the round.

Only you and Ellis remain.

The final interview is at the top of a spiral staircase made from all the alarm clocks you have ever ignored. Each step rings when you touch it. Ellis climbs beside you, but gradually he becomes younger, then smaller, until he is a boy in an oversized green tie. You recognize him as the child you used to see in the mirror when you tried on your father's clothes.

“There can only be one of us,” he says.

At the top is a quiet room with a single desk and a window overlooking the city. It is the first real window you have seen. Far below, streets curve into cursive sentences, and traffic moves like red blood cells through them. On the desk lies a contract. The title of the position is blank. The salary is listed as ENOUGH. The hours are FROM NOW ON.

The judges sit across from you. Without their masks, they all have your face at different ages.

“One final question,” says the oldest. “Why do you want this job?”

All the answers you rehearsed crowd into your mouth: growth, purpose, impact, lifelong passion. They taste like paper. Behind you, Ellis whispers that there is a correct response, but he has forgotten it.

You look through the window again. In one building, the woman from the elevator is setting bread on her windowsill. In another, someone is teaching a dog to dance. On a rooftop, a person who looks like you is watering a garden in the rain. None of them appears to be competing.

“I don't,” you say.

The tower shudders. The judges lean forward. Cracks race through the desk, the walls, the polished floor. The blank title on the contract fills itself in: PERSON WHO KNOWS WHEN TO LEAVE.

The oldest judge smiles. “Congratulations.”

Confetti begins to fall, but each piece is a tiny resignation letter. You take Ellis by the hand and run. Together you descend through all the former challenges. The fish leap from the briefcase and swim through the air ahead of you. The telephones ring out of sequence. At the endless office, every version of you abandons their desk, and the whole crowd follows, carrying blueprints, batons, books, and small private oceans.

On the ground floor you find a revolving door turning far too quickly. Beyond it is not a street but a green field at dawn. The judges call after you that the benefits are excellent, that the position was created especially for you, that no one has ever refused the grand prize.

You step into the revolving door. For one dizzy rotation you see every possible life waiting in a separate pane of glass. Then Ellis lets go of your hand. He is grown again, standing inside the tower, wearing the green tie.

“Someone has to interview the next one,” he says.

Before you can answer, the door carries you outside.

The field is full of alarm clocks half-buried in the grass. None of them are ringing. The escaped versions of you scatter toward the horizon, becoming birds, strangers, weather. In your pocket you find the silver stamp from the earlier test. Its face no longer says REDUNDANT. It says REAL.

You press it to the pale morning sky. There is a soft official click, and the sun is approved.
