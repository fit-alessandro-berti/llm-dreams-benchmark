The knowledge arrives all at once, as naturally as if you had remembered an appointment. You are standing in a city of sun-baked brick and narrow shaded streets. Merchants unload baskets from donkeys; somewhere nearby, bread is baking, and a smith’s hammer rings against bronze.

At the market, you ask a water seller who dreamed this place. Without surprise, she points to a tower beyond the rooftops. So does a potter. So does a child carrying a cage of green moths.

Inside the tower, an old woman sits beside an oil lamp, writing on a scroll. As you approach, you see that she has recorded everything you have done since waking: the streets you crossed, the dust on your feet, even the question you are about to ask.

“How do I get home?” you whisper.

“You don’t wake up,” she says. “I do.”

Outside, the sky is paling. At the edges of the city, walls and rooftops are becoming translucent. Through them you can see a vast, colorless nothing.

“I’ve dreamed this city for eighty years,” she explains. “Every night I add another street. But dreams end, and I have never been able to make anything remain.”

She offers you the pen. At the bottom of the scroll, after *The stranger reached the tower*, there is one empty line.

“Finish it,” she says.

The first bell of morning sounds. Her hands begin to fade.

You press the pen to the parchment and write:

*When the dreamer woke, the city did not vanish. It folded itself small enough to live inside a memory.*

The old woman reads the sentence and smiles. Then the tower fills with light.

You wake in your own bed, with sunlight on the blanket and a smudge of black ink on your thumb. For a while, you lie still, listening to the ordinary sounds of morning.

And just before the dream slips away, you hear it: distant but unmistakable, the sound of a market opening, a smith lifting his hammer, and an old woman laughing with relief.
