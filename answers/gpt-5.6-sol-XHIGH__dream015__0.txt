They point beyond the barricade, where the old channel lies like a pale scar between the ruined buildings. Shopping carts and the ribs of buses protrude from its dust. At dawn you climb through a gap in the wire with a canteen, a coil of rope, and the small brass key you have carried for so long that you no longer remember what it opens.

The city is quieter outside the walls. Wind worries at plastic caught in the trees. Somewhere high above, a traffic light changes from red to green, though the pole beneath it has fallen across the road. You begin walking along the empty riverbed.

By noon you discover footprints in the dust. They are bare, wet footprints. Each one fills slowly with clear water after you pass it, as if the earth is remembering rain. You kneel and touch one. The water is cold enough to hurt, and beneath its trembling surface you see not your reflection but a kitchen from your childhood: yellow curtains, a bowl of peaches, someone standing with their back to you and humming.

You pull your hand away. The footprint dries instantly.

The tracks lead into a subway entrance half buried under concrete. On the steps sits a fox wearing a red collar. Its fur is dusted white with ash, and one of its eyes is a glass marble. When you approach, it rises and trots underground without looking back. You follow because in the dream you know this is what you came for.

Below, the station is lit by hundreds of candles floating in the air. Their flames lean toward you as you pass. Vines twist through cracked turnstiles, heavy with silver fruit shaped like tiny bells. The fox rings one with its nose. Far down the tunnel, something enormous rings in answer.

A train arrives without sound.

Its doors open onto a carriage crowded with sleeping people. Some wear patched survival gear; others wear evening gowns, hospital robes, school uniforms. Moss grows over their shoes. They breathe together in a slow rhythm that makes the windows fog and clear, fog and clear. There is one empty seat, and on it lies a folded paper map.

The fox jumps onto your lap as soon as you sit. The doors close. The train begins to move.

The map shows the city as it was before the fires, but a blue line has been drawn through streets where the river never ran. At the end of the line someone has written, in your own handwriting, THE RESERVOIR OF THINGS NOT YET LOST.

You try to wake the passenger beside you, an old woman holding a birdcage, but when you touch her shoulder she opens her eyes and sand pours from them. Inside the cage, a tiny thunderstorm flashes.

“You’re late,” she says.

“For what?”

“For deciding.”

The train plunges deeper. Through the windows you glimpse buried rooms sliding past: a library where fish swim between the shelves, a nursery full of gently rocking cradles, a football stadium packed with silent deer. In one tunnel a black ocean presses against the glass. Shapes the size of towers turn in its depths.

At last the train stops beneath a sign bearing your name.

You step onto the platform. The fox remains behind. Just before the doors shut, it drops the red collar at your feet, and you see that the brass key hanging from it is identical to yours.

Beyond the platform stands a blue door set into raw stone. It has two locks.

Both keys turn at once.

On the other side is the river.

It hangs vertically in a vast cavern, flowing upward from darkness into darkness, a wall of luminous water. Suspended within it are seeds, photographs, medicine bottles, bees, teeth, books, and thousands of sleeping animals. You see clouds that have not rained yet and children who have not been born. Near the surface floats the bowl of peaches from the footprint, still bright and unspoiled.

A figure waits at the water’s edge in a coat made of stitched-together maps. Its face changes every time you blink: your mother, a stranger, yourself as an old person, the blank white face of a clock.

“There is enough here to begin again,” the figure says. “But not enough to keep everything.”

It gives you an empty jar. The mouth of the jar is narrow, though you understand that it can hold one thing from the reservoir: rain, grain, a cure, clean soil, the memory of how to build machines. Behind you, very far away, you hear the settlement’s metal gates shudder under an attack. You hear people shouting your name.

You reach toward the water. All the practical choices shine close to your hand. Yet farther in, almost hidden among the drifting relics, you see a small golden sound—the humming from the kitchen, the tune without words. You know that if you take it, everyone will remember something they believed the end of the world had erased. You also know memory cannot fill a stomach.

The cavern begins to collapse. Stones fall upward. The river trembles.

You thrust the jar into the luminous current.

For a moment it contains nothing. Then the little thunderstorm from the old woman’s cage appears inside it, raining onto a single dark seed. The golden humming coils around the seed like a root. Somehow you have chosen three things by choosing one.

The map-faced figure smiles with your oldest face.

You run.

The station is flooding when you return, but the water races beside you rather than behind you. It pours up the stairs into daylight and follows the dry riverbed south. You ride on its crest, clutching the jar, while drowned street signs rise and birds burst from abandoned chimneys. The fox runs along the bank, now red as flame, multiplying into a whole bright pack.

At the settlement, the barricade is surrounded—not by raiders, but by thousands of thirsty people who have followed the sudden smell of rain. Guards raise their weapons. The crowd presses forward. For one terrible second you think the world will end again in the same old way.

Then someone begins to hum.

The tune passes through the crowd. Weapons lower. People who have never met recognize one another. You open the jar and tip the single seed into the mud beside the gate.

A tree erupts from the ground, lifting you gently in its branches. Its roots crack the walls without destroying them. Its leaves become clouds, and rain falls over the city in warm silver sheets. Below, strangers place bowls and helmets and open hands beneath it. The river spreads through every street, reflecting windows in buildings that are no longer ruined.

From high in the tree you can see green moving over the horizon, swift as sunrise.

You look down at your palm. The two brass keys have melted into a tiny compass. Its needle does not point north. It points toward every person singing, turning from voice to voice so quickly that it seems perfectly still.

When you close your hand around it, you wake with the taste of peaches and rain in your mouth, and for several seconds the dark room is full of leaves.
