You are about to take the final exam for a course that determines your future career. As you sit down to start the exam, you notice the questions are written in a language you have never seen — the letters curl and uncurl like ferns, rearranging themselves whenever you look away.

You raise your hand to tell the proctor, but the proctor is your third-grade teacher, and she is much taller than you remember, tall enough that her head brushes the fluorescent lights. She smiles and says, "You studied for this. You've been studying for this your whole life." And somehow that is true and not true at the same time.

You look back down. The exam paper has grown. It hangs off both sides of the desk now, spilling onto the floor like a tablecloth, and the questions keep multiplying in the margins. Around you, the other students are writing furiously. You lean toward the one on your left and see that he is not writing answers at all — he is drawing a house, over and over, the same house with a red door, and he is crying quietly, and you understand without asking that the house is his answer, that everyone here is answering a different exam.

The clock on the wall runs backward, which reassures you at first, until you realize it means the exam has already ended and you are moving away from it.

You decide to write anything. You press your pen to the paper and the pen goes through it, through the desk, and your hand follows, and then you are underneath the exam hall, in a warm dark space like the underside of a stage, where the questions hang down through the floor like roots. Down here you can read them perfectly. They are simple. One says: What did you want, before you were told what to want? Another says: Name the person you were at nine years old. Show your work.

You know these answers. You have always known them. You start speaking them aloud because there is no paper down here, and your voice comes out in that fern-curled language, and it sounds right, it sounds like the first language, the one before school.

Above you, through the floorboards, you hear the proctor announce that time is up. Pencils click down in unison like a hundred small doors closing. But you are still talking, and the roots are listening, drinking it in, and you feel the whole building lean down toward you like something bending to hear.

When you climb back up through your desk, the hall is empty. Your exam paper is the only one left, and it is blank except for a single line at the top, written in your own handwriting, in ordinary letters:

Passed. You may stop preparing now.

You walk out through doors that open onto your childhood street in summer. Somewhere behind you, in the empty hall, the clock is still running backward, giving all of it back — every hour you ever spent afraid of this room.

You wake with your hand cramped, as if it had been writing all night.
