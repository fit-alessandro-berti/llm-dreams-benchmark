walk faster, hoping your feet remember something your mind has forgotten.

The streets have names like Almost and Not Yet. Every clock you pass shows a different time, but all their second hands are moving toward you.

You stop a woman carrying a glass umbrella.

“Do you know where my meeting is?”

She looks relieved. “I was hoping you’d tell me.”

Before you can answer, a bus pulls up beside you. On the front, instead of a destination, it says: **YOU SHOULD HAVE LEFT EARLIER.**

You climb aboard.

Inside, the seats are arranged like a classroom. Your childhood dentist is driving. He hands you a ticket with a small, warm tooth printed on it.

“Last stop,” he says, though the bus hasn’t moved.

Outside the windows, the city folds neatly in half. Buildings bend without breaking. Somewhere, a telephone rings with the sound of your mother calling you in for dinner.

You remember you’re supposed to bring something to the meeting.

You check your pockets. A key. A wet leaf. An envelope addressed in your own handwriting.

Inside is a photograph of a door.

When you look up, the door is standing at the front of the bus.

You know, with the unquestioning certainty of dreams, that opening it will make you late and not opening it will mean you never arrive.

So you turn the handle.

Beyond it is a little kitchen full of afternoon light. Someone has put two cups on the table. Across from you sits an older version of yourself, patiently peeling an orange.

“I’m sorry,” you say. “I couldn’t find the place.”

“I had trouble too,” they say, and brush the peels onto an empty plate.

You sit down, still wearing your coat. There was a question you meant to ask, but now you can only watch the kettle. Through its little window, you see a bus moving slowly along a submerged street.

Your older self slides half the orange across the table.

“Eat,” they say. “You haven’t had lunch.”
