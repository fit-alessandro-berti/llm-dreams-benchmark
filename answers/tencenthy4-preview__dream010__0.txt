You are participating in a competition where the grand prize is your dream job. As the competition progresses, you face various challenges and realize each one is strangely familiar, as if the contest has been built from things you once wanted before you convinced yourself they were impractical.

In the first challenge, you enter a room full of half-finished inventions. The task is not to make something impressive, but to make something kind. You choose a small device that translates hesitation into words. When you finish, it whispers, “Take your time. I’m listening.” The judges—whose faces keep shifting like reflections on water—nod slowly.

Next, you must cross a bridge made of every idea you abandoned. The bridge only holds if you admit what you do not know. So you say aloud, “I’m not ready yet.” The fog lifts. On the other side, earlier versions of yourself are waiting: the one who imagined boldly, the one who was told to be realistic, the one who kept going anyway. They ask if there is room for them in your future. You tell them yes.

For the final task, there is only a blank door. To open it, you must offer something you made without knowing whether it would work. You hold up a lantern built from your failed drafts, awkward beginnings, and stubborn curiosity. It glows faintly at first, then brightly.

The door opens onto a workspace that feels impossible and yet exactly right—warm light, open notebooks, tools waiting, and a quiet sense that your work will matter to someone. A contract appears, but instead of a job title it reads:

**Make what only you can make.  
Begin before you feel ready.  
Leave the world a little easier to navigate.**

You sign it.

A voice that sounds like your own says, “You have won.” Then the dream begins to dissolve, but the door does not disappear. It stays inside you, open just a crack, with light spilling through.
