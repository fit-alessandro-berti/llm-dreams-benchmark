You are at a large social gathering where you only know one person. As you try to find them, you encounter various groups of people and—

**—the room keeps changing its architecture.**

First, you push through a circle of people wearing the faces of your high school teachers, but their bodies are all slightly too tall, jointed wrong like marionettes with extra elbows. They are arguing about a language you almost remember from childhood, syllables that taste like copper pennies. You ask them if they've seen her, and one of them—Ms. Henderson, but younger than she ever was, maybe sixteen—turns to you and smiles with teeth that are piano keys. "She's in the garden," she says, though there is no garden here, only carpet that smells like wet wool.

Then you step through a curtain that wasn't there a moment ago (red velvet, smelling of dust and perfume) and find yourself in a kitchen where everyone is preparing food they cannot eat. A man in a tuxedo is cracking eggs into a bowl, but inside each egg is a tiny, perfect house, complete with chimney smoke. He looks up at you with eyes like cracked mirrors. "Looking for someone?" he asks. You nod. He hands you an egg. "She left through the pantry," he says, "but the pantry is in your left pocket now."

You check your pocket—it's true. The wool lining has become mahogany paneling. You reach inside and your fingers brush against something cold and glass. When you pull it out, it's a snow globe, and inside, the tiny plastic scene is the party you just left, and you can see yourself standing there, looking at the snow globe, your face pressed against the glass from the inside.

The groups multiply. A choir sings in the hallway, but their voices are coming from teacups. A group of children play hopscotch on the ceiling, their laughter dripping down like rain. You realize you have been walking in circles, or perhaps you haven't been walking at all—you're standing in the center of a spiral staircase that descends into a light that looks like dawn but feels like the moment before you fall asleep.

And then you see her.

She is standing by the punch bowl, but the punch bowl is a swimming pool, and she is floating on her back, staring at a ceiling that is actually the sky. She is wearing the blue coat you gave her three winters ago, or perhaps it's the coat she wore when you first met, in a life that hasn't happened yet. She doesn't notice you at first. You call her name, but the sound travels sideways, becoming a question someone else is asking in another room.

You step into the pool—the water is warm, made of honey and static electricity—and swim toward her. As you get closer, she turns, and her face is yours, but softer, as if seen through frosted glass. "I've been waiting," she says, but her mouth doesn't move. The words appear in the air between you, written in condensation.

You reach out to touch her shoulder, and your fingers pass through her, and she is gone, and you are standing alone in a room that is exactly the size of your bedroom, but the walls are made of everyone's voices saying goodbye at once.

The lights go out.

Somewhere, a phone is ringing.

And you wake up with the taste of copper pennies in your mouth, and the distinct, impossible certainty that you were almost there—that if you just remembered the right thing to say, or the right door to open, you would find her again in the next dream, waiting in the garden, or the pantry, or the pocket of your coat, forever almost found.
