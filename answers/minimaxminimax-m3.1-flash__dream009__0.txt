The sentence completes itself in your sleep, and the dream takes it as permission:

*…you realize you need to find the lighthouse keeper's daughter, and give her the key, before she forgets she ever sent you.*

---

You wake in a narrow bed under a quilt that smells of salt and cedar.

The room is whitewashed, low-ceilinged, lit by a single tallow candle that has burned down to a blue-holed stub. No radiator hum. No fridge drone. No blue glow of a screen you left face-down on the nightstand. Instead, a washstand with a pitcher, a wooden chair, and a coat hanging from a nail that fits you *too* well.

You sit up. Your clothes have changed. Linen shirt, high collar, a vest with brass buttons. Your hands are bare, and they are not quite your hands—older, perhaps. Or maybe just tired in a different way.

Outside the window: a cobbled lane. A horse-drawn cart passes, all hoof-clatter and leather creak, driven by a man in a wool cap who does not look up. A woman in layered skirts carries a basket of something red—apples, late-season, though the trees overhead are thick with green. Somewhere a dog barks twice and stops, as if remembering it had better things to do.

No engine noise anywhere. No jet overhead. The silence is so total it feels *curated*.

You find the note where you'd expect a phone: folded into your vest pocket, in handwriting that is unmistakably your own.

> *Find the lighthouse keeper's daughter. Give her the key.*
> *Do not trust the man with the red gloves.*
> *If the bells ring thirteen times, run.*

You turn the note over. On the back, in smaller, shakier letters:

> *I've only got until moonrise. Please hurry. You'll know me.*

---

The key is in your pocket. Cold iron, long-shafted, with a tag shaped like a shell.

The town, when you step into it, does not resolve into any year you can name. The cut of the clothes says one decade; the streetlamps say gaslight, which means before electricity; but the woman at the fishmonger's stall wears a wristwatch, and you see a tin sign nailed above the tavern reading *TELEPHONE* in a serif typeface that would look at home on a 1930s storefront. The air smells of kelp, coal smoke, and bread.

You ask directions. People answer, but their answers have a strange quality—as though they're describing a place that has shifted slightly while they were talking. The lighthouse is "north," then "northwest," then, from an old man who squints at you like he's reading your face, "northwest, but only when the tide's out. Otherwise it's east. Don't let it confuse you."

By the third bell from the church tower—you count, because you are counting now, because of the note—you have a bad feeling.

The bells ring.

Four. Five. The sound is beautiful and it rolls down the wet street and comes back changed.

Six. Seven.

The crowd thins. Shutters close. A woman pulls her child indoors without hurry, the way you do when you know what's coming.

Eight. Nine.

You pass a man on the corner wearing red gloves. He is standing very still, and when he turns his head, there is no face, only the smooth suggestion of one under the streetlight, and he says, pleasantly, "Lovely evening," in your mother's voice.

Ten.

You run.

Eleven. Your lungs burn. The lane narrows, becomes a footpath, becomes slick rock. The town behind you folds in on itself, lanterns going out one by one like eyes closing.

Twelve.

Ahead, on a spit of black stone, a lighthouse. Not burning. Its lamp room is dark, and the door stands open, and standing beside it with her hair whipping in the sea wind is a girl of perhaps sixteen, holding a lantern she doesn't need, watching you come.

Thirteen.

The bell's last note doesn't fade. It just stops, mid-air, like a held breath.

The girl looks at you, and something in her face cracks.

"You're late," she says, and her voice is the voice of the person who wrote the note on the back. "I've been forgetting you for *years*." She holds out her hand. The key is in your other hand; you hadn't noticed pulling it out.

"Say my name," she says. "Quickly. Before I lose it again."

You open your mouth—and the dream, delighted with itself, hands you a name that is not yours, in a language you've never spoken, and the syllables feel like the bottom of the ocean.

You say it.

The lighthouse lamp ignites behind her, and you wake up in your bed, in your room, in your own century, with iron dust on your palm and the taste of salt on your tongue.

And on your nightstand, where the phone should be, there is a folded piece of paper in your handwriting, and it says:

> *Find the lighthouse keeper's daughter. Give her the key.*
> *Do not trust the man with the red gloves.*
> *If the bells ring thirteen times, run.*
>
> *I've only got until moonrise. Please hurry. You'll know me.*

The clock on the wall reads 11:47 p.m.

Moonrise is at 12:19.

Somewhere outside your window, in a town that is definitely not there, a horse-drawn cart creaks past without a sound.

---

*Would you like to go back? The dream is holding the door open, and it doesn't do that twice.*
