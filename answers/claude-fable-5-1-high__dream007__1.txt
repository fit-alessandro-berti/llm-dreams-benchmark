You are in the middle of a heated argument with a close friend. The disagreement is about something important to both of you, and you decide to stop talking and simply show her.

I take her hand. We are standing in her kitchen, except the kitchen has the ceiling of a train station, iron ribs and dirty glass far overhead, and pigeons move in the rafters where the range hood should be. She is still saying my name, the way she says it when she is angry, with the second syllable pressed flat. I pull her toward the back door, and the back door opens onto the street where we grew up, though neither of us grew up on the same street.

"This is what I meant," I say. I am not sure yet what I mean.

The street is wet. It has just rained, or is about to. The houses lean in slightly, listening. I lead her to the third house on the left, the one with the green porch, and I know that inside it is the thing we were arguing about, though when I try to remember the words of the argument they come apart in my hands like wet bread. Something about loyalty. Something about a decision one of us made years ago that the other never forgave. I remember the shape of it, the heat, but not the words.

Inside the green house there is a room full of chairs, all facing one wall. On the wall is a window and beyond the window is the two of us, much younger, sitting on a curb sharing a bag of sour candy. We watch ourselves. The younger versions do not notice. My friend's grip on my hand tightens.

"You always do this," she says. "You always change the subject."

"I'm not changing it," I say. "This is the subject."

The younger me hands the younger her the last candy, and I realize this is what I wanted her to see, and I realize at the same moment that it proves her point and not mine. The candy was the last one. I gave it away. I have always given away the last one and then held it against her.

She lets go of my hand.

Now the chairs are occupied. People I half recognize, teachers, an uncle, a woman from the bakery who has been dead for a long time, all of them turned toward the window, all of them murmuring. They are not taking sides. They are saying, to one another, the exact words of our argument, passing them along the rows like a collection plate. I hear my own sentence come back to me from the mouth of my uncle and it sounds petty. It sounds like a sentence a child would say.

My friend has walked to the front of the room. She puts her palm flat against the window. Through the glass, the younger her looks up, as if she felt something, a coolness, a shadow.

"I'm not going to apologize," she says, without turning around.

"I know," I say.

"But I want you to know I heard you."

The window fogs where her hand is. When she takes it away there is a print, and inside the print, very small, a door.

We go through it, because that is what you do.

On the other side it is night and we are on a roof I have never been on. The city below is made of paper, streetlights showing through it like lamps behind a shade. There is a wind that smells of oranges. We sit down with our backs against a chimney and she takes a bag of candy out of her coat pocket, the same brand, the sour ones dusted white. She holds it out.

I take one. It is not the last one. There are plenty.

"We didn't finish," I say.

"No," she agrees. She looks at the paper city. A building at the edge folds itself neatly in half and is gone. "We won't, probably."

I want to say something that will settle it, some final true sentence, but each time I get close to it the sentence turns over and shows me its underside, which is her sentence, which is just as true. I understand, in the way you understand things in dreams, all at once and without effort, that the argument is not a thing to be won. It is a room we both keep the key to. We will go in there again. We will say the words again, and the dead woman from the bakery will pass them down the row.

The wind picks up. The paper city rustles. Somewhere below us a door I made with my breath is closing.

"Your hair's a mess," she says, and reaches over and fixes it, roughly, the way she would if we were fourteen.

The roof begins to tilt, very slowly, toward the morning. I try to hold on to the chimney, to her sleeve, to the bag of candy, which I do not want to lose. I can feel the dream getting thin. I can hear a bus outside a window that belongs to a room I actually sleep in.

"Call me," she says. She is already becoming the sound of the bus.

"I will," I say, and I mean it, and I wake up with my hand closed on nothing, sour on my tongue, still angry, and no longer sure at whom.
