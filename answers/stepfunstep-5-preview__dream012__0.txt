You grip the wheel until your knuckles turn to bone-white coral, saltwater stinging your eyes as the *Aletheia* pitches hard to port. Another wave crashes over the bow, and for a moment the world is only roar and black water and the screaming of wind through snapped rigging. Your crew’s faces—pale, rain-slicked, desperate—turn toward you like flowers seeking a sun that has drowned.

You open your mouth to shout an order, but the words that come out are not your own. They are the names of stars that no longer exist, syllables that make the storm hesitate. The waves freeze mid-crash, hanging above the deck like sheets of shattered glass, and the sea goes silent except for a low, resonant humming from deep below the keel.

Then you see them. Beneath the frozen waves, colossal shadows move—not fish, but leviathans made of memory and ink, their eyes glowing like lanterns in the abyss. They circle the ship, not as predators, but as mourners. One surfaces, its blowhole exhaling a cloud of butterflies that scatter across the deck and settle on the shoulders of your trembling crew. Where each butterfly lands, a man becomes still. His fear dissolves into wonder.

The ship is no longer wood and iron. It has become a great wooden flute, and the storm is merely breath passing through it. You realize you are not steering a vessel but playing an instrument, and the crew is not a crew but a chorus. As the tempest climbs to its crescendo, you raise your arms, not in surrender but in conducting, and the hurricane rearranges itself into a symphony of light.

The horizon peels open like the pages of a wet book, revealing a calm, golden sea on the other side of the storm. The ship glides forward—not sailing, but falling upward—and the moment your bow crosses the threshold, you feel your body become weightless. You look down at your hands and see they are made of compass needles, all pointing inward.

The last thing you hear is your crew laughing, not with madness, but with recognition, as the dream begins to fold itself around you like the closing of a gentle tide. And just before you wake, you understand: the storm was never trying to sink you. It was trying to teach you how to fly.
