The first round is held in an auditorium where all the seats face away except one—yours. The emcee, whose face shifts like slow-moving clouds, hands you a microphone that weighs exactly as much as your oldest insecurity. “Speak,” they say, “into the silence you refuse to break.” You do. Your voice emerges as a flock of paper cranes that fold themselves into a bridge across the chasm where the audience should be. You cross it, and the floor applauds with your footsteps.

In the semifinals, you are given a glass box and told to fill it with *tomorrow.* You reach inside your chest and pull out hours you have not yet lived—warm, flickering things like fireflies. They spill from your fingers, and for a moment the box holds everything you might become, but the glass begins to crack from the pressure of so much potential. You learn, then, the crucial trick of the competition: you must let a few escape. You must leave room to breathe.

The final challenge is the simplest. You stand before a door with no keyhole. The judges—now only the reflections of everyone you have ever admired, layered like transparencies—say in unison: “The prize is behind this. But to open it, you must become the person who no longer needs to win it.”

In dreams, hesitation moves like a tide; it pulls the whole room sideways. But you remember the bridge of folded voice. You remember the box of borrowed futures. You understand that the competition was never about earning the job. It was about growing the shape of yourself that could hold it.

You reach for the door. There is no click, no tumble of locks. It opens the way a held breath does, finally released.

Beyond it is not an office, or a stage, or a title engraved in gold. It is a clearing at the exact blue hour between dog and wolf. There is a desk made of dawn, a chair that fits the precise curve of your spine, and on the desk: a blank page, a pen, and work that renews itself with every sentence. Meaning that does not require an audience. A purpose so large it has no outside.

You sit down to begin.

And that is when you hear it—not an alarm, exactly, but the sound of a starting gun fired from very far away, or perhaps your own heart, knocking against the inside of the dream, eager to find out what happens next.
