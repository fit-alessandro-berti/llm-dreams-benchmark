...and your boss is smiling. That's the first wrong thing. Your boss never smiles. The smile stretches a little too wide, like it's borrowed from someone with a bigger face.

"The deadline," he says, "is yesterday."

You look down at your hands. You're holding a project brief, except the pages are blank, and when you flip through them they make the sound of shoveling snow. You look up to ask a question, but the office has changed. The cubicles have grown taller, bending overhead like houseplants toward a sun that isn't there. Your coworkers are all still at their desks, typing, but their monitors display only your own reflection, staring back at you with an expression you don't remember making.

You need to start. You sit down, but the chair rolls backward, away from the desk, all the way across the office floor which is now somehow a parking lot, and the chair rolls until it bumps against your car — your car, here, in the office, on the third floor. The trunk is open. Inside is everything you need to complete the project: a box of clocks, all ticking, none showing the same time.

You understand, in the way you understand things in dreams, that if you can set all the clocks to the same time, the deadline will be met. Your boss is standing beside you now, though you never saw him walk over. He's holding a mug that says WORLD'S OKAYEST EMPLOYEE. Steam rises from it. The steam spells words in the air: *faster. faster. faster.*

You start setting the clocks. Your fingers keep slipping — the dials are wet, or your fingers are wet, and when you look at your hands they're pruned, like you've been underwater for a long time. The office lights flicker. Somewhere a phone is ringing. It's your phone, on your desk, but your desk is on the ceiling now, and everyone is still typing, upside down, unbothered.

The phone keeps ringing.

You pick it up — somehow you're at the desk, right side up, no memory of getting there — and a voice says: "This is your nine o'clock reminder for your nine o'clock meeting."

You look at the clock on the wall. It's nine o'clock. It has always been nine o'clock. It will always be nine o'clock. The deadline was yesterday and it is also right now and it is also never.

Your boss leans over the wall of your cubicle — the cubicle is normal-sized again, everything is normal, fluorescent hum, coffee smell, Monday feeling — and he says, gently, almost kindly:

"Wake up. You're going to be late."

And you do.
