Someone in the third row says, “Oh, I know that house.”

You try again. This time it’s the clink of a spoon against the side of a jam jar. A woman near the aisle shuts her eyes. “Keep going,” she says.

So you put your notes down. You give them the bicycle wheel that used to tick once per turn, no matter how often your father bent the fender back. You give them rain starting on the roof of a parked car. For the last part you have to lean close to the microphone: it’s the tiny click the kitchen made when the refrigerator switched off and you and your brother were the only ones still awake.

You had meant to end with something grand. Instead you stand there, listening.

From high up in the balcony comes the sound of a marble rolling across a wooden floor. It travels a little crookedly, just as it always did, and drops through the hole by the radiator.

You can’t see your brother in the dark, but you know where to wave.
