and they are moving.

Not quickly — not like anything alive should move — but slowly, beneath your fingertips, the carved spirals and stars and interlocking vines shift when you aren't quite looking at them. Each time you blink, the pattern has rearranged itself into something almost familiar.

You turn the box over. There is no keyhole. No hinge. No seam at all, really, just dark wood that feels warm, as if someone has been holding it for a very long time before giving it to you.

"Who gave it to me?" you ask, but in the dream, you already know not to expect an answer. The room around you isn't a room anymore. It was, a moment ago, but now the walls have receded into a soft grey distance, like fog over water.

You trace one of the spirals with your thumb.

It clicks.

Not a loud click. A small, intimate sound, like a tooth against a teacup, or a word being remembered.

A line of golden light appears where no seam was before, tracing the new pattern your thumb has made. The other patterns respond, sliding and turning with faint wooden sighs, forming constellations, then faces, then a map of a coastline you have never visited but somehow miss terribly.

You understand, suddenly, with dream-certainty: the box isn't locked to keep you out. It's locked to keep what's inside from getting out before you're ready.

You should be afraid. You aren't.

You press your palm flat against the lid and think of the key — not a metal key, but the feeling of one. Cold. Heavy. The specific sound your childhood house key used to make in the front door on rainy afternoons. The smell of rain through the screen. Someone in the kitchen humming a song you can almost, almost hear.

The box hums back.

The lid opens.

Inside, there is no treasure. No letter, no jewel, no smaller box. Inside is a tiny, perfect night sky, complete with wind. It rushes up around your face smelling of salt and cold stone, and in that sky are thousands of small, glowing threads, each one tied to a moment you had forgotten: you, age seven, laughing with a missing tooth. A stranger holding an elevator for you on the worst day of your year. Your mother's hands braiding something. A dream you had last week, already dissolving.

One thread drifts loose and lands across your wrist, light as cobweb.

You reach to touch it, and you fall — not down, but *in*.

You land barefoot on a beach at low tide, under that same tiny sky now vast above you. The box sits beside you in the sand, now the size of a house, its door standing open. From inside comes the sound of someone calling your name, gently, as if for dinner, as if you have been playing outside too long and the light is getting low.

You know if you go through, you will wake up.

You know if you stay, you will forget why you came.

So you do what dreamers do: you pick up a handful of the glowing sand, close your fist around it tight, and walk through the door to thank whoever has been keeping all your lost things safe for you all this time.

You wake before you see their face.

But your hand is still closed. And when you open it, slowly, in the pale morning light —

there, traced faintly across your palm in gold, is a spiral.

Still moving.
