...and the challenges are all things you have done a thousand times, only slightly wrong.

The first is sorting mail. You know this. You have always known this. But the envelopes are warm, and each one has your handwriting on it, addressed to people whose names you can read but cannot pronounce, and the sorting slots are labeled with feelings instead of departments. *REGRET. LATER. THE OTHER THING.* You sort quickly because the clock on the wall has no hands, only a small mouth, and it is chewing.

Someone behind you is doing very well. You don't turn around. You know it's a person you sat next to in a class you never took, and they are kind, and they are winning, and both of these facts are unbearable.

The second challenge is held outdoors, though you were indoors a moment ago and no one has mentioned the change. A field of low grey grass. A long table. At the table sit the judges: three of them, in chairs of different heights, and a fourth chair, empty, which you understand is yours, which you understand you have been sitting in the whole time.

"Tell us about your greatest weakness," says the one who is also your mother, though she is wearing the badge of a stranger.

You begin to answer and the answer is a good one, rehearsed, polished, and as it leaves your mouth it turns into a small bird and lands on the table and starts eating the bread. Everyone nods. This is apparently the correct response. You feel a surge of pride so intense it wakes something in your chest, and the field tilts slightly, like a boat.

Between rounds there are hallways. The building is growing them the way a plant grows — you can feel it happening, the corridor extending ahead of you at exactly the speed you walk, so that arrival is a thing that can be promised but never delivered. Doors pass. Behind one, someone is laughing at a joke you told years ago. Behind another, your desk from a job you haven't gotten yet, with a mug on it, steam rising, waiting.

You try the handle. Locked. Of course. Of course.

By the final round there are only two of you left, and the other finalist is you, but younger, seventeen maybe, wearing the shirt you wore the day you decided what you wanted to be. They look at you with enormous hope. They have not been tired yet. They have not learned the word *lateral.* They ask you, quietly, without any malice at all:

"Did we get it?"

And here is the thing about the dream: you cannot lie in it. The words won't come. You open your mouth to say *yes* and what comes out is the true answer, which is *I don't remember what it was.* Because the job description has been in your pocket the entire time, folded, and when you unfold it now the paper is blank, and then it is a mirror, and then it is just paper, warm, like the envelopes.

The judges lean forward. The empty chair fills.

"That's the one," they say. "That's the answer."

They hand you the prize and it is a set of keys, ordinary, slightly worn, the kind that have already been used. You know exactly which door they open. You have known the whole time; the knowing was the competition. You stand, and the field is a hallway is a room, and in the room there is work already in progress — half-finished, imperfect, obviously yours — and someone looks up and says, without any surprise, *oh good, you're here, we've been keeping it going.*

And you sit down to a task you were never trained for and your hands know it.

You are still doing it when the light comes through the window and the room begins to thin, and the last thought you have inside the dream is not *I won* but *I was already hired,* and it is such a relief that the relief is what wakes you.

For a few seconds in the dark you can still feel the keys.
