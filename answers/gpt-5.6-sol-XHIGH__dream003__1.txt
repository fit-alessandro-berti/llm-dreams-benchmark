You are given a mysterious locked box with no key in sight. As you examine it closely, you notice intricate patterns and tiny grooves running across its surface like the streets of a city seen from very high above. When you tilt the box, lights move through those streets. They are no brighter than sparks under ash, but each one seems to know where it is going.

No one remembers giving you the box. You are standing in the middle of a railway station with a ceiling so high that clouds have gathered beneath it. Trains arrive without sound, open their doors, and leave empty. On every platform, clocks display a different hour, though all their second hands move together.

You hold the box to your ear. At first you hear only the sea. Then, beneath the waves, someone is knocking.

Three knocks. A pause. Three more.

You knock back with your fingernail, and every clock in the station stops.

The grooves on the lid rearrange themselves. The little illuminated streets bend toward the center, forming the outline of a house. You recognize it as the house where you lived as a child, except for a narrow tower rising from its roof. There was never a tower. Even so, you remember being forbidden to climb it.

A door appears at the far end of the platform. It is the front door of that house, complete with the brass scratch beside the handle. When you open it, you do not enter a hallway but a summer evening. The grass is warm beneath your bare feet. Beyond the garden, the impossible tower leans into a violet sky, its upper window burning gold.

The box grows heavier as you approach. With each step it seems to fill with something that shifts and whispers. You think of sand, then teeth, then folded birds. The knocking continues, no longer inside the box but somewhere above you.

The tower has no entrance. You walk around it until you find a keyhole at the height of your heart. It is exactly the shape of the box.

You press the box against the stone.

For a moment nothing happens. Then the whole tower turns like a key.

The garden rotates around you: trees, walls, moon, and stars sweeping past in a slow circle. A seam opens in the air, and through it you see a spiral staircase descending upward. You begin to climb. With each landing you pass a window looking into a room you once knew. In one, your family is eating breakfast, but every chair is occupied by a much older version of you. In another, rain is falling indoors and gathering on the ceiling. In the third, a small child sits cross-legged on the floor, holding an identical box.

The child looks up before you can pass.

“You took long enough,” they say.

“Where is the key?” you ask.

The child frowns, as if you have misunderstood something simple. “You’re carrying it.”

You look down. The box is gone. In your hands lies a sleeping moth, large and pale, with the same intricate patterns traced across its wings. Between those patterns, the tiny lights still travel. The moth opens its eyes—one on each wing—and the knocking stops.

At the top of the staircase is the golden window. There is no room around it, only the frame hanging in darkness. You unlatch it and climb through.

On the other side, you find the railway station again, but now it is flooded to the level of the platforms. Constellations drift beneath the black water. The silent trains have become long silver fish. Everyone you have ever forgotten is waiting on the opposite platform, their faces clear for only a moment before changing into someone else’s.

One of them raises a hand. You know, with the absolute certainty peculiar to dreams, that this is the person who gave you the box. You also know that if you cross the water and speak to them, you will remember something so important that the life you came from will no longer fit you.

The moth stirs in your palms.

You open your hands, and it flies out over the water. Wherever the tips of its wings touch the surface, doors appear. Hundreds of them rise upright among the drowned tracks: red doors, glass doors, doors covered in ivy, doors with light beneath them. Behind each one you hear a different sound—the clatter of breakfast dishes, applause in an empty theater, wind moving through a room full of paper.

The figure across the water points, not to any of the doors, but to your chest.

There is a keyhole there now.

The moth circles once above your head and folds its wings. It becomes the box again, falling neatly into your hands. The lid is no longer locked. You lift it.

Inside is the station, perfectly small and perfectly complete. Inside the station, you see yourself opening a box. Inside that box is another station, and another you, and another box, descending farther than sight. All the smaller versions of you look up at once.

From immeasurably far below comes the sound of three knocks.

This time you do not answer. You close the lid, set the box on the platform, and step into the dark water. It is warm. The stars beneath your feet rise around you like bubbles, and as you wade toward the waiting figure, the station begins to shrink, walls and clocks and clouds folding inward until everything fits inside the box behind you.

Just before the darkness closes, you hear the lock click open.
