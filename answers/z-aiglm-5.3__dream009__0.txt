Your throat is dry, and the sun here hangs low and strange in a sky that seems too wide. Below the hill sits a village — timber and thatch, smoke curling from chimneys, a church bell striking an hour you can't quite count.

No one stares at your clothes, which is odd until you look down and realize you're wearing wool and linen now. You can't remember when that changed. You decide not to question it.

At the well, a woman lowers her bucket for you without being asked. She has your grandmother's laugh. She asks how the harvest went, as if she's known you all her life, and you hear yourself answer "well enough" in a voice that is yours but somehow older — or younger, it's hard to tell.

What you really need, you decide, is to learn the year. But the blacksmith says "the year of the great flood." The priest says "three winters after your father passed." A barefoot child just laughs: "Now. It's always now."

Time doesn't work here. You stop asking.

That night you sleep in a hayloft that smells of animals and summer, and you realize you haven't thought about your phone once. There's nothing to check, nothing buzzing for your attention, and the silence isn't empty at all — it's full of crickets, of horses breathing in the dark, of wind moving through the fields like a slow tide.

Just before dawn, it comes to you: what you needed all along wasn't water, or the year, or a way back.

You needed to slow down. And here, finally, you have.

You wake with the smell of hay still faint in your nose, and the echo of a bell counting an hour that doesn't exist.
