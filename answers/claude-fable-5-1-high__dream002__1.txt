You are walking through a peaceful forest when suddenly the weather changes dramatically. You feel a storm approaching and the light goes strange first, before anything else does. The green of the leaves turns a deeper, bruised green, the kind you only see underwater, and the birds stop all at once, as if someone has lifted a needle from a record.

You keep walking because your feet seem to know the path even though you have never been here. The pines lean in. Their needles begin to fall, not down but sideways, drifting past your face in slow horizontal lines like the forest is exhaling toward some place behind you. You turn to look and there is no behind you. The path you came along has closed up into moss and fern, seamless, as if it were never there.

The wind arrives. It does not sound like wind. It sounds like a crowd murmuring in another room, hundreds of low voices that stop whenever you try to make out a word. The trees bend and the bending makes a sound too, a creak that is almost a sentence. You think: I should find shelter. And the moment you think it, you see a house.

It is your grandmother's house, though it has no business being here in the forest, and though you have not thought of it in years. The yellow paint, the porch with the third step that always gave a little. The screen door is open and clapping against its frame in the wind, open and shut, open and shut, like it is calling you by name in the only way a door can.

You run. The rain begins as you run, enormous drops, warm, each one landing with the weight of a hand on your shoulder. By the time you reach the porch you are soaked to the skin but not cold. The third step gives under your foot. You knew it would. That small give is the most comforting thing you have ever felt.

Inside, the house is dark and dry and smells of dust and cinnamon. Someone has left a lamp burning in the kitchen. You go toward it, past the coat hooks where a coat you had when you were seven is still hanging, small, patient. In the kitchen the table is set for two. Two bowls, two spoons, a pot on the stove with the lid trembling from the steam beneath it.

Through the window above the sink you watch the storm reach its full strength. The forest is thrashing now. Lightning comes without thunder, or the thunder comes so late and so soft that it feels less like sound and more like a memory of sound. Between the flashes you see that the trees are walking. Not swaying, walking, lifting their roots like skirts and moving off in a slow procession toward the west, and you understand they are leaving because the storm is not weather. The storm is something being remembered by the land, all at once, and the trees do not want to be here for it.

You are not afraid. You sit at the table. You lift the lid from the pot and it is soup, the exact soup, and you ladle some into your bowl and some into the other bowl though there is no one else here.

Then there is. She is sitting across from you, and she is not your grandmother exactly, she is younger than you ever saw her, she is the age she was in the one photograph on the hallway wall, and she is looking at you the way you might look at a letter you wrote to yourself a long time ago and had forgotten sending.

"You came in out of the rain," she says. It is not a question.

"I didn't know where else to go."

"No one ever does." She eats. You eat. The soup tastes like the smell of the house, if that were possible, and in here it is possible. The storm presses against the windows and the windows hold. The lamp does not flicker.

"Is it going to pass?" you ask.

She tips her head at the window as if listening to the murmuring crowd out there, the voices you could never quite make out. "It's not passing," she says. "It's arriving. That's different. Things that arrive stay a while."

You want to ask what it is, what has arrived, but your mouth is full of soup and by the time you swallow the question has changed shape in you, and you ask instead, "Did you know I'd come?"

"I set the table," she says, and that is all.

Outside the last of the trees is gone from the window. There is only rain now and the flat gray light of a sky that has forgotten how to end. And yet in the kitchen it grows warmer. The dust and the cinnamon. The lamp. The spoon in your hand that was always too big for you and now fits.

You notice you have stopped being wet. You notice your grandmother's chair is empty again, but the bowl across from you is empty too, scraped clean, and the spoon is set neatly at the side the way she always set it. You notice the screen door has stopped clapping, and you know without checking that it is closed now, latched, and that the third step outside is dry.

You sit for a long time. The storm goes on arriving. It never quite finishes. Somewhere under the sound of it you hear the crowd of voices settle, one by one, like people finding their seats before a story begins, and you realize that the story is you, sitting here, and that they have been waiting for you to be still enough to start.

So you are still. And the rain, which has been trying to say your name all along, finally says it, and you wake with the word still in the room.
