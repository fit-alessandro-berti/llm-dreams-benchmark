Despite the hardships, you decide to follow the music.

It's faint at first — a crackling guitar riff coming from an old battery-powered radio in your backpack, stuck on the same three-second loop for weeks. But last night in your sleep, you *dreamed inside the dream* that the loop became a full song, coming from the west, past the broken highway.

So you pack what little you have: half a canteen of filtered rainwater, three seeds wrapped in cloth, your father's chipped mug, and you start walking.

The world is grey ash and rusted cars and hollow buildings with windows like empty eyes. The dangers are everywhere — the howling packs at night, the sinkholes, the silent men with masks who take and never give. You learn to move like shadow, sleeping in daylight in the husks of buses, walking at dusk when the sky turns that strange dream-purple.

On the third day, you find the garden. Not yours — someone else's. A rooftop behind a collapsed library, where tin cans and old bathtubs are overflowing with green tomatoes and beans. A little girl waters them with a teacup. She doesn't run from you. She just holds out a tomato and says, "Grandpa says if we grow it, they'll come."

And they do. One by one, drifters like you crawl out of the ash, drawn not by the radio signal you thought you were following, but by the smell of soil and living things.

That night, you all sit around a small fire made from broken bookshelves — but you don't burn the books, you read from them first. You plant your three seeds in a paint bucket. You pour a sip from your father's mug for everyone to share. And for the first time, the radio loop doesn't sound broken. It sounds like a beginning.

You fall asleep in that rooftop garden, safe and full for once, while the stars push through the smoke above you, and you think: this is how the world comes back.
