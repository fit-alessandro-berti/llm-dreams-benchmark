...speak—and the first thing you notice is that your voice isn't your own. It comes out warm and crackling, like a radio announcer from the 1940s. The audience doesn't seem to mind. They lean in.

You look down at your notes and find they're blank. No—wait. They're a map of your childhood neighborhood, with a route drawn in red marker leading from your old house directly to this stage.

So you improvise. You tell them about the map. About the oak tree with the rope swing, the crack in the sidewalk you always jumped over, the way afternoon light came through the kitchen blinds. The audience nods. Some of them are crying. You realize suddenly that you know every one of them—old teachers, a friend from third grade, the mail carrier from when you were nine, and a dog you once loved, sitting politely in the third row with his ears perked.

A hand goes up in the back. A question. You can't hear it no matter how hard you listen. Everyone waits. The stage tilts gently under you, like the deck of a ship, and the curtain ripples though there is no wind.

And then it hits you: you're dreaming. The audience holds its breath, waiting to see what you'll do with this knowledge. The lights are warm on your face. Somewhere far away, faintly, a phone is ringing.

You decide to keep talking.
