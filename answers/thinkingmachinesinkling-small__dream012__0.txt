...and you realize that the rudder in your hands isn’t wood or metal, but something warmer—like a heartbeat you’d forgotten you had.

The crew isn’t just looking at you; they’re holding up mirrors, and in each one you see not the storm, but the parts of yourself you’ve been navigating away from. You stop shouting orders. The tempest is too loud for commands anyway, so instead you sit down on the wet deck and let the rain wash over your face. You tell them, *“We don’t need to outlast this. We need to become it, together.”*

The waves don’t capsize the ship—they lift it. The sky breaks open not into sunlight, but into a color that has no name, and the crew, instead of trembling, begins to sing—not to calm the sea, but to thank it. The masts turn into old tree branches, and the sails become clouds that hold you up rather than drag you down. You are no longer above them, directing; you are in their midst, holding a lantern made of salt and memory.

In the dream, you don’t win the storm. You dissolve into it, and when you wake, your mouth tastes of salt, and you remember that the captain’s truest duty was never to fight the chaos—but to trust the crew enough to be part of it with them.
