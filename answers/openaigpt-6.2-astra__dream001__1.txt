walk faster, as though speed might make up for not having a direction. At the first corner you ask a woman selling newspapers what time it is.

“On this side of the street?” she says.

You cross. It starts to rain.

Under the awning of a closed cinema, you turn out your pockets. In one is a brass key. In the other is a slip of paper in your own handwriting: *I’ll wait by the little door.*

At once you know the door. It was in your grandmother’s kitchen, down beside the stove, too small for any grown-up to use. You used to lie on the linoleum and whisper through its keyhole. You haven’t thought of it in thirty years.

Now the city is full of the smell of toast. You follow it through an arcade, across a bridge, and into a tiled passage where a familiar square of yellow light falls across the floor. There is the stove. There is the little door. On the other side someone is shuffling a deck of cards, badly, dropping half of them.

You kneel and turn the key.

Your grandmother is sitting cross-legged on the floor in her good green dress. She looks about nine.

“You came,” she says.

“I thought I was going to be late.”

“You were. I cheated.”

From under her dress she produces the kitchen clock. Its minute hand is bent. You remember getting blamed for that.

She deals you in. For a while you play according to rules neither of you quite remembers. Then she sets down her cards and asks, with the solemn interest of a child who has been promised a true story, “What am I like when I’m old?”

Outside, a thousand city clocks begin to strike. You shut the little door so you can hear each other.

“Well,” you say, “for one thing, you never get any better at shuffling.”
