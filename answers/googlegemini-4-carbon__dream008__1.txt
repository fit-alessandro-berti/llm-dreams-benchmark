You press them anyway, desperately hoping muscle memory will save you, even though your fingers feel heavy, like they are moving through cold honey. On the monitor, instead of words or spreadsheets, thick green ivy begins growing across the glass from the inside, blocking your view. 

You glance up at your boss to explain, bracing for anger, but they are suddenly seven feet tall and wearing your fourth-grade teacher’s knit cardigan. They don't seem to notice the stones or the ivy. They just tap their wristwatch, which has no numbers—only frantic, spinning compass needles. 

"We need the sky alphabetized by noon," your boss says, their voice echoing as if you are both standing in an empty indoor pool. Somehow, in the heavy, unquestioning logic of the moment, you nod. You know how to do this; you just need the right form.

You frantically pull open your desk drawers looking for a pen, a stapler, *anything* useful, but the drawers are overflowing with dry autumn leaves and tangled cassette tapes. The fluorescent lights overhead begin to hum a lullaby you haven't heard since childhood. With a sudden jolt of fresh panic, you look down and realize you aren't wearing shoes, and the gray office carpet is slowly turning into wet, shifting sand. 

The tide rolls in, swirling around the wheels of your ergonomic chair. Your boss is still waiting, looming like a statue, now holding a clipboard made of melting ice. Driven by sheer desperation, you realize there is only one way to submit the project on time. You pull open the bottom drawer of the nearest filing cabinet, step inside, and slide it shut behind you—instantly finding yourself standing in the cereal aisle of a brightly lit grocery store, holding a single red balloon, having completely forgotten why you were ever in a hurry.
