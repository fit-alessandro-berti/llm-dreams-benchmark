You start to run, though the running is the wrong kind — the pavement gives like the deck of a boat, and each stride carries you a little less far than the last, so that the city seems to be widening around you as you move through it.

A woman is selling apricots from a folding table. You ask her where the meeting is. She says, "Past the water, then left at the sound," and you understand this completely, with a certainty you will not be able to explain later. You thank her. She hands you an apricot with a small brown bruise on it, and you put it in your pocket, and the pocket is deeper than your arm.

There is water. It's a canal the color of weak tea, and along it run the tram tracks, and the tram arrives before you finish thinking that a tram would help. Inside, the seats face inward, and everyone aboard is dressed for the meeting you are going to. Their shoes are all correct. You look at your own shoes and find them fine, which is somehow worse — you had been counting on the shoes to be the problem.

Forty minutes. The clock above the tram door has a minute hand that sweeps normally and an hour hand shaped like a small painted fish, and you watch the fish and feel your chest tighten with a grief that has no object.

You get off at a building that is not the building, but the lobby is your grandmother's kitchen, the linoleum with the burn mark near the stove, and the smell of scorched sugar, and you are so relieved you nearly sit down on the floor. A man at the counter says your name — but says it the way a teacher says it, with a slight rising tone, as if checking that you're the right one. He asks whether you've brought the thing. You reach into your pocket and there is the apricot, and you offer it, and he nods and stamps a form and the form is a page from a book you were reading when you were eleven.

The elevator goes sideways. You feel it in your stomach, the way you feel a car cresting a hill. Through the closing doors you see the canal again, and the apricot woman folding her table, and it is evening now, the light gone amber and long, and you have been in this building for either four minutes or a season.

The room at the end of the hall has a long table and everyone is standing, putting on coats. They're delighted to see you. Someone grips your shoulder and says it went well, all things considered, and someone else says they were sorry about the last part but it had to be done. You have arrived at the end of your own meeting. You will never learn what was decided. There is a chair at the head of the table with your jacket over the back of it, and a glass of water with the lipstick of nobody on the rim, and a single sheet of paper face-down.

You turn the paper over. It says: *Item 4 — whether to keep him.* And underneath, in a handwriting you recognize because it is yours, but neater, more patient, the way you wrote when you still believed things could be fixed by writing them carefully:

*Deferred. He keeps almost arriving.*

You want to argue. You want to say that you ran, that the pavement was against you, that a woman gave you directions in a language you understood perfectly and can no longer speak. But they are filing out past you, and they are kind, and one of them turns off the light, and in the dark the room is the kitchen again, and the burn mark on the linoleum is warm under your palm.

You wake with the taste of scorched sugar and the sense of having been forgiven for something you were never told about, and the feeling stays with you through the shower, through the coffee, until about ten in the morning, when it thins out and is gone, and all day after that you keep touching your pocket.
