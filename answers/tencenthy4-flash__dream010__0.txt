In the first round, you enter a glass maze where every wall reflects a version of you: one confident, one terrified, one older, one who gave up. To move forward, you must choose the reflection you trust least and ask it for directions. It points silently toward a door you had not noticed.

In the second round, you are given a suitcase packed with everything you have ever procrastinated on. The zipper will not open until you admit which item is heaviest. You say the name of an old dream, and the suitcase becomes light enough to carry.

The third challenge takes place on a stage with no audience. You are told to give the presentation of your life, but the slides keep changing: missed trains, abandoned sketches, compliments you forgot, small acts of courage no one clapped for. halfway through, the lamp above you flickers, and you realize the judges are not looking for perfection. They are looking for whether you will keep speaking when the lights go out.

By the final round, the competition has become a vast office made of clouds. Desks float past like islands. Phone calls ring from empty rooms. Everyone around you is dressed for success, but no one remembers what the job is. The head judge, a woman with your childhood handwriting, hands you a contract written in stars.

“Sign it,” she says, “and you will get the position you’ve always wanted.”

You look closer. The title is not written in gold. It is written in your own hand: The Person Who Keeps Beginning.

So you sign.

The clouds part. The judges stand. Somewhere, an alarm clock begins to ring, soft at first, then brighter. You wake with a pen in your hand, a blank page in front of you, and the strange certainty that the application was never to a company at all.

It was to your own life.

And you were hired.
