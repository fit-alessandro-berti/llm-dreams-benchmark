...the air turns the color of old pennies. The birdsong doesn't stop so much as *rewind*, each note sliding back into the throats it came from, until the forest holds its breath.

You look up. The clouds aren't rolling in from the horizon. They're rising up out of the ground, gray mist seeping from between the roots like the trees are exhaling something they've kept inside for centuries. It gathers above the canopy, thickening, and somehow you know the storm has been waiting for you specifically. It isn't angry. It's *impatient*, the way a letter feels when it's been sitting unopened on a table.

The first raindrop hits your wrist, and it's warm. The second tastes like the lemonade your grandmother used to make, though you never had a grandmother who made lemonade, and yet you remember her kitchen perfectly: yellow curtains, a radio playing a song that hasn't been written yet.

You start to run, because that's what you do in storms, but the path under your feet has become soft and elastic, and each step carries you farther than it should. Ten feet. Twenty. You're bounding through the forest in slow, enormous arcs, and the trees lean aside to let you pass, bowing like courtiers. Lightning flickers, but instead of a crack of thunder, you hear a chord, deep and resonant, like a cathedral organ played underwater.

Ahead, there's a house. Of course there's a house. It's the house you lived in when you were seven, except it's built into the trunk of a massive oak, its windows glowing amber. The front door is open. Someone inside is calling your name, but in a voice that sounds like your own, only older, or younger. It's hard to tell.

You step inside, soaked, and the storm stops at the threshold as politely as a guest waiting to be invited in.

The hallway is longer than it should be. Along the walls hang framed photographs of moments you don't remember: you standing on a glacier, you laughing at a table full of strangers, you holding a small wooden box. In each photo, you're looking directly at the camera as if you know someone will be watching later.

At the end of the hall, on a small table, is the wooden box.

You pick it up. It hums faintly, like it has a heartbeat. Outside, the storm presses its face against the windows, and you understand now that it isn't trying to hurt you. It's trying to *see* what's inside, just like you are.

Your thumb finds the latch.

And just as the lid begins to lift, just as a thin line of light spills out, brighter than lightning and warm as that strange rain,

you hear something ringing. Far away at first, then closer.

The forest dissolves at the edges like wet paper. The house folds in on itself. The last thing to fade is the box, still in your hands, still glowing, still unopened.

You wake up with the feeling that you almost remembered something very important, and the faint, impossible taste of lemonade on your tongue.
