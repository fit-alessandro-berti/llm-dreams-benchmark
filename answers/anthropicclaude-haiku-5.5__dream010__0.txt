The stage was made of folded paper, and every step I took unfolded a little more of it, so the floor kept extending ahead of me like a road being drawn in real time. Somewhere behind the velvet curtains, a crowd murmured in a language I almost understood.

The first challenge was a interview conducted by a committee of three identical clocks. Their hands spun in opposite directions, and they asked me questions I couldn't quite hear, only feel, like pressure behind the ears. When I answered, my words came out as small birds that fluttered up into the rafters. One clock nodded. Another frowned. The third simply chimed, and I took that as a pass.

The second challenge was a maze built entirely from my own old handwriting. Every wall was a sentence I had written in school, or in margins of notebooks, or on the backs of envelopes I'd forgotten. Some of them were encouraging. Some were apologies I'd never delivered. I walked through them carefully, reading each one, until I reached the center, where a single unfinished sentence waited on a pedestal. It said *I am finally good at*, and the rest of the line was blank. I held my pen over it, and the pen was already moving, and I woke up a little, but then fell back into the maze to find the ending.

The third challenge was a race against my competitors, who were all versions of me from different years: a nervous teenager, a tired thirty-year-old with coffee on her sleeve, a confident stranger with silver rings. They ran on the ceiling, which was also the floor now, because gravity had been reassigned as a prize. I could not beat them by speed. Instead I stopped and asked the teenager what she had wanted to be, and she told me, and suddenly we were all carrying her answer together, and the stage tilted gently toward us like a boat finding the shore.

At the final round, the judge stepped out of a grandfather clock in a long coat. Its face was my face, but older and kinder, with ink on its fingers.

"The job," it said, "was never a title. It was the thing you kept doing when no one was watching."

Then it handed me a key. The key opened the curtain, and behind it was not an office or a stage but a long table covered in unfinished projects, half-built instruments, sketches of bridges, and a chair with my name carved into the armrest. The crowd, now visible, was made of people I had helped without knowing it: a child who had learned to read from my clumsy flashcards, a man whose bicycle I had once repaired on a rainy street, a dog that had followed me home years ago and never quite left.

They began to applaud, and the sound was like rain on a tin roof, warm and insistent.

I sat in the chair. It fit perfectly. The pen in my hand was already writing, and this time the sentence on the page finished itself: *I am finally good at beginning again.*

And then the dream folded softly, like paper, and I was awake, still hearing the rain.
