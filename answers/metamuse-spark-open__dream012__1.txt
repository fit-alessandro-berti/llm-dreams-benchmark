and you realize you cannot remember how you got there.

The wood of the wheel is warm under your hands, impossibly warm, like skin. The nails that hold the deck together are humming.

Your crew stands around you — but their faces keep shifting. One moment your first mate looks like your best friend from childhood, the next like a stranger you've never met but somehow recognize. No one speaks, but you can feel their question pressing on you: *What do we do, Captain?*

The storm isn't normal.

The waves are too tall, rising like black glass mountains that hang in the air for a second too long before crashing. The lightning doesn't flash — it *creeps* across the sky, slow and branching like veins of light, illuminating the clouds from within. And the rain falls upward from the sea as much as it falls down from the sky.

You shout an order to cut the sails, but your voice comes out muffled, as if underwater. No one moves. They're not being disobedient. They're waiting. Because they know, even if you don't yet, that this storm doesn't care about sails and ropes.

Then you look down.

The sea through the gaps in the deck is not water. It's sky. Deep, clear, star-filled night sky, swirling beneath the ship. You are sailing on a storm above the universe.

And you understand — this is a dream. The thought lands not like a revelation, but like a memory returning.

The wind screams louder, tearing at the mast, begging you to panic, to fight it, to be afraid.

You have two choices, Captain:

**1. Fight the storm:** You can grip the wheel until your knuckles turn white, yell against the wind, and try to force the ship straight. The crew will admire your courage, but the sea will keep rising.

**2. Let go:** You can do what no captain is ever supposed to do.

In the dream, you take a slow breath. The air tastes like salt and electricity. You loosen your grip on the wheel.

You turn to your crew — your friends, your strangers, yourself — and you say, "We don't need to steer."

And you let go.

The instant your hands leave the wood, the wind stops. Not gradually. Instantly. The towering waves freeze in place around you like a cathedral of dark water. Raindrops hang suspended in the air, catching the slow lightning like diamonds.

Perfect silence.

Then, gently, the ship begins to sink — not down, but *into* the sky-sea below. The water doesn't flood the deck. The deck becomes transparent. You and your crew are falling upward through stars.

None of you are afraid anymore. The first mate — who now looks distinctly like you — smiles and says, "Took you long enough to remember you were dreaming."

You wake up slowly, with the faint sensation of swaying, and the sound of your own breathing where the storm used to be.
