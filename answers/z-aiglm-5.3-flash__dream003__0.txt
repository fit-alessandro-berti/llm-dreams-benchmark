And you realize, with a certainty that arrives from nowhere, that you can almost read them. Almost. It feels like remembering a language you only ever knew while falling asleep.

The box is warm. Warmer than wood has any right to be—warm like something small and alive curled up inside. When you press your ear against it, you don't hear ticking or breathing, but a conversation happening just too far away to make out, in voices you almost recognize.

There's no keyhole at first. Then you blink, and there it is in the center, as if it had always been there and simply chosen to let you see it. You search your pockets and find, naturally, everything except a key: a fistful of sand, a photograph of a house you've never lived in, one red balloon string trailing off into somewhere unseen.

It occurs to you—the way things simply occur in dreams—that the box doesn't want a key at all. It wants to be told something true. So you lean down close and whisper a secret you'd forgotten you were keeping.

The lock doesn't click. Instead the whole lid goes soft, like the surface of deep water, and your hand passes through it without resistance.

Inside, there is no bottom. Inside, there is a night sky—stars burning cold and impossibly close—and beneath them, in the middle of an empty field, stands a door. Small. Ordinary. Painted the exact blue of your childhood bedroom.

You're walking toward it before you decide to walk. Somewhere behind you, the box closes itself with a sigh, and you understand—dimly, the way dreams let you understand—that the lock was never keeping you from something. It was keeping something from looking back at you.

Your hand reaches the handle—

—and you wake, palm open on the pillow, holding nothing, missing something.
