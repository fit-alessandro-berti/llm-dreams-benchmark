The buildings here are tall and slightly wrong. Their windows are arranged like the faces of people you almost recognize. You check your phone for the address, but the screen shows only a photograph of your childhood kitchen, and when you swipe, it shows the same kitchen from a different angle. Someone has left the kettle on.

You ask a passerby for directions. She is carrying an armful of clocks, each showing a different time, and she nods as if she's been expecting you. "The meeting," she says. "Of course. It's just past the river, where the library used to be." You have never been here before, yet you feel a pang of grief for the library.

The river, when you reach it, flows uphill, and the bridge across it is made of piano keys. Each step plays a note, and by the middle of the bridge you realize you're playing a song your grandmother used to hum. You slow down to get it right. This seems more important than the meeting, though you can't say why.

On the far bank, the city has rearranged itself. There is a door standing alone in a plaza, with your name on a brass plate, spelled slightly wrong. You look at your watch. The meeting started forty minutes ago, or it starts in forty minutes. The hands won't commit.

You open the door.

Inside is a long table, and seated around it is everyone you've ever meant to call back. They turn to you, not angry, just patient. At the head of the table is an empty chair, and in front of it, a steaming cup of tea.

"You're right on time," says someone with your father's voice and a stranger's face. "We were just about to begin."

You sit down. You realize, with the calm certainty that only dreams allow, that you are the one who called this meeting, and that you have known what it was about all along. You open your mouth to speak, and somewhere far away, a kettle begins to whistle.
