“Thank you for coming,” says a little girl in the last row, exactly as you move your lips.

It takes you a moment to recognize the gap in her front teeth. In every photograph of yourself at that age, you have your mouth clamped shut.

You look down at your notes. They’re in the enormous, careful handwriting you learned in first grade. *When I grow up,* the first page begins. There are ninety pages.

The girl looks as appalled as you feel.

“Shall we skip to the questions?” you ask. This time the voice is yours.

Hers is the only hand that goes up. “Did we ever get a dog?”

You tell her about Arthur: the white sock on his left paw, his fear of the toaster, the way he leans his whole weight against your shins when you come home. As you speak, a familiar scratching starts behind the velvet curtain. The girl hears it too. She comes tearing down the aisle just as Arthur noses his way onto the stage.

He goes straight to her. Of all the strange things that have happened tonight, this is the one that makes perfect sense. He’s never had any trouble recognizing you.

You sit on the edge of the stage while she scratches his belly. After a while someone in the audience clears his throat.

“All right,” the girl tells him, without looking up. “You can have him next.”
