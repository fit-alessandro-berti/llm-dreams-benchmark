The audience doesn’t just watch; they tune in. Each head tilts toward you as if the room were a tuning fork. When you say the first sentence, the sound becomes visible—pale threads of it drifting over the heads, around the balconies, under the chandelier. A few people in the front row laugh, but not in mockery. It sounds like relief.

You feel the words working out of your chest and into the walls, making the room feel older and newer at once. The applause starts, not as noise but as a warm pressure, a tide rising under your feet. Then—suddenly—the sound turns quiet. Every face goes still. Someone in the back reaches toward you, or perhaps simply toward the light.

And you understand that the speech isn’t something you are giving the audience. It’s something you’re inviting the room to remember. You lean forward, your voice softening, and feel the audience lean in too—not just as listeners, but as witnesses to a version of you that has never had to be perfect.

Then the dream softens around the edges, the stage lifting upward into a vast, silver ceiling, and the crowd behind you becomes a shimmer of reflections rather than faces—until, at last, you open your mouth again and begin the first sentence of a speech that only you and the dream can hear.
