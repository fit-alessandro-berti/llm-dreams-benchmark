You are in a world where colors and shapes are constantly changing. As you explore this surreal landscape, you come across a blank canvas and a brush that seems to have been waiting for you, standing upright in the air with no easel, no hand, no reason.

The canvas is the only still thing here. Everything else churns — the hills roll from ochre to violet like slow breathing, the sky folds itself into hexagons and then forgets them, the ground under your feet is sometimes grass, sometimes tile, sometimes the pattern of a tablecloth from a kitchen you knew as a child. But the canvas is white and quiet, and its whiteness feels less like emptiness than like patience.

You pick up the brush. It is heavier than it looks, warm, with a pulse in the handle like a small bird held loosely. There is no paint anywhere, and you understand — the way you understand things in dreams, all at once and without argument — that the paint is supposed to come from the world itself. So you reach out and touch the brush to a passing cloud of orange, and the orange comes away on the bristles, and the cloud continues on, slightly paler, unbothered.

You make the first stroke.

The moment the color touches the canvas, it stops changing. A single line of orange, fixed, permanent, obedient. It is the first permanent thing you have seen in this world, and the landscape notices. The hills slow their breathing. The hexagons in the sky drift closer, leaning in like an audience.

You paint faster. You steal blue from a river that keeps trying to become a staircase, green from a forest that flickers between pine and coral, a deep red from something that might be a sunset or might be someone's memory of one. Each color you take holds still on the canvas. You are not painting a picture so much as building a shelter — a small rectangle of things that stay.

Slowly you realize what you are painting. It is this world, but calmed: the hills as they were one particular second ago, the river as a river, the sky wearing just one shape. A portrait of a moment that never agreed to be a moment.

And the world begins to gather around the canvas to look at itself.

The shifting slows everywhere now. Colors stop mid-change to see what they look like when they commit. A mountain that has been rehearsing a thousand silhouettes settles, shyly, into the one you gave it. The whole restless landscape stands at your shoulder like a crowd around a mirror, and there is a feeling in the air that you can only call relief — the relief of a thing that has finally been seen slowly.

But the brush is growing lighter in your hand. With every stroke, the pulse in the handle fades. You understand this too, the dream-way: stillness is being paid for. Something is being spent.

You look down and see that it is you. Your own edges have started to shimmer. Your hands are turning the colors of everything you've borrowed — orange fingers, a river-blue wrist, one knuckle flickering between pine and coral. You are becoming the changing that the world is giving up.

You could stop. The canvas is nearly full; only one small corner is still white, patient as ever.

Instead you lift the brush to your own chest, take a little of whatever color you are now, and fill in the last corner with it.

The painting is finished. The world is still. And you — you are the only moving thing left in it, drifting up off the ground, shifting hue with every heartbeat, and it does not frighten you at all. Somewhere below, the landscape hangs in its frame, calm at last, and you scatter slowly into the sky like paint into water, becoming the weather of that quiet world, becoming the reason its colors will someday, gently, begin to change again.

You wake with your hands open, as if you had just set something down.
