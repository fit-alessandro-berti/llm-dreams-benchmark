You find yourself in a vast, unfamiliar city, and you realize you have an important meeting in an hour but no idea where it is. You start to ask a woman waiting beneath a clock, but before you can speak she lifts one finger to her lips. All around you, hundreds of people do the same. The city becomes so quiet that you can hear the clocks inside the buildings, each ticking at a different speed.

The woman points to your hand. Written across your palm in blue ink is an address you are certain was not there before: THE LAST FLOOR, MINISTRY OF LOST TIME. Beneath it, in smaller letters, is your own name, though one letter has been crossed out and replaced with a tiny drawing of a door.

You thank her, but when you look up she has become a bronze statue covered in pigeons. One pigeon wears a red conductor's cap. It flies down, seizes the minute hand from the clock above you, and sails into a narrow street. You follow because it seems to know where it is going.

The street lengthens with every step. The buildings lean over you like people trying to hear a secret. Their windows show rooms that cannot possibly fit inside them: a winter forest, a classroom at the bottom of the sea, your childhood kitchen with someone sitting at the table whose back is turned. You want to stop at that window, but the pigeon rings a small bell around its neck and the street begins moving beneath your feet like a conveyor belt.

You pass a bakery selling warm maps. The baker pulls a folded district from the oven and slaps it onto the counter. Streets rise from its crust in golden ridges.

"The ministry?" you ask.

"It was demolished tomorrow," he says. "You'd better hurry."

He tears off a corner of the map and gives it to you. It tastes faintly of rain. As you swallow, the route appears in your memory, not as directions but as a melody. You hum it under your breath and doors unlock as you pass. A bridge unfolds from the wall. A staircase lowers itself out of the sky. The pigeon disappears through a cloud, still carrying the minute hand, and now all the public clocks show only the hour.

There are forty minutes left.

At the top of the staircase you enter a railway station. The trains stand upright in their platforms like silver towers, their windows stacked above you. An announcement says your meeting has been moved to Platform Yesterday. You run past platforms numbered 6, 6, 6, and 8, but there is no 7. At the information desk, a boy with the tired face of an old man asks to see your invitation.

You search your pockets. You find a key, a seashell full of whispering, three white stones, and a photograph of the meeting already in progress. In the photograph, a long table is surrounded by blurred figures. At the far end sits an empty chair with your coat draped over it. On the back someone has written: DO NOT ARRIVE WITHOUT THE QUESTION.

"This will do," the boy says, stamping the photograph. The stamp makes a sound like a distant door closing.

He directs you into an elevator disguised as a telephone booth. There are no buttons, only a black receiver. You pick it up. After several rings, your own voice answers.

"You're late," it says.

"Where am I going?"

"You knew when you woke up."

The elevator drops upward. Your stomach stays behind for a moment, then catches up. Through the glass walls you see the entire city turning slowly below you, its avenues forming the hands of an enormous clock. One avenue points at the station. The other points toward a dark tower at the city's edge. Between them, the rooftops spell a question, but clouds keep passing over the words before you can read them.

The booth stops inside a crowded elevator. Everyone is facing you. They carry briefcases dripping seawater. You recognize none of them, yet each smiles with the intimate sadness of someone who attended your funeral.

"Last floor?" you ask.

They shake their heads.

"There is always another," says a man in a green suit.

The doors open onto an orchard growing inside an office. Desks stand among apple trees. Typewriters chatter by themselves, typing leaves. Secretaries in paper masks collect fallen hours from the grass and seal them in envelopes. Each envelope is addressed to a person who has said, "I wish I had more time."

A receptionist looks at you over the rims of her glasses. Her hair is full of snow.

"Name?"

You tell her.

"That person has not arrived yet."

"But I am that person."

"Then you are much too early."

She opens a ledger. Every page contains the same date, written in your handwriting. "Your meeting is in Room Zero. Follow the red thread. Do not cut it, even if it becomes inconvenient."

A red thread is tied around your ankle. You had not noticed it before. It runs between the trees, under a desk, through a keyhole, and down a hallway lined with umbrellas. You follow it. It tangles around passing strangers, winds through a fountain where the water flows upward, and crosses a ballroom where couples dance without touching. With every turn you lose something: first the key, then the seashell, then the photograph. When you check your palm, the address has faded until only the little door remains.

There are twelve minutes left.

The thread slips beneath a low blue door. You kneel and crawl after it into a room no larger than a cupboard. As soon as you enter, the room expands into a vast circular chamber. The ceiling is night sky. The floor is shallow water. In the center stands the long table from the photograph, surrounded by people whose faces are hidden behind clocks.

The empty chair waits at the far end. Your coat hangs over it, wet from rain that has not begun.

You sit. At once, every clock-face turns toward you.

"You have brought the question," they say together.

It is not a question. It is the melody of the route, still moving inside you. You hum the first note, and the chamber trembles. The people lean closer. You hum the second, and cracks appear in their glass faces. By the third note you remember the words the rooftops were trying to spell.

"What happened to the hour I lost?" you ask.

No one answers. Instead, the water rises around your chair. Beneath its surface you see the missing hour as clearly as a room through an open door. It is morning in your childhood kitchen. Sunlight lies across the table. The person at the window turns around, and you recognize yourself, much younger, waiting with absolute patience for you to come home.

You stand so quickly that the chair falls backward. The clock-faced people begin striking the hour, each at a different moment. The noise shakes stars loose from the ceiling. They splash into the rising water and dart around your legs like silver fish.

"The meeting is adjourned," says the youngest version of your voice.

The red thread pulls tight. It leads across the water and down into the bright kitchen below. You take one step onto the surface. It holds. Then another. Behind you, the ministry folds itself floor by floor, the orchard closing into the elevator, the elevator into the telephone booth, the booth into the stamped photograph, and the photograph into the blue door drawn on your palm.

There is one minute left, though you no longer know until what.

You reach the kitchen window. Your younger self opens it from the other side. For a moment you are face to face, separated only by a thin pane of morning. You want to offer a warning, an apology, a map of everything ahead. Instead you place your palm against the glass. The little blue door opens.

All the clocks in the city stop.

The missing hour rushes through you: long summer light, the smell of toast, rain beginning on a roof, the knowledge that someone is waiting in the next room. It is so ordinary that it breaks your heart. You close your eyes to keep it, but the red thread snaps and becomes the thin red line of dawn beneath your eyelids.

When you open them, you are back beneath the clock in the silent square. The woman is no longer a statue. The pigeon has returned the minute hand. Crowds stream around you, speaking all at once, and every clock shows a different time.

In your hand is a warm envelope addressed to you. Inside is a single blank page and an apple seed. You understand that the meeting has already happened, and that you have missed nothing.

Then somewhere high above the city, an alarm begins to ring. Every window opens. Thousands of white birds pour out and wheel across the sky, arranging themselves briefly into the shape of a door. You step through just as the hour strikes, and wake with your hand still raised, as if knocking.
