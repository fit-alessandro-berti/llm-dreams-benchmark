... walk into the green haze where the old highway used to hum, not toward the ruins but toward the sound—a faint, patient rhythm, like breathing from a buried machine.

The air tastes of ash and rain. Grass leans through cracked asphalt; every shimmer is a stranger. You don’t go because it’s safe—nothing is. You go because “scarce” doesn’t have to mean “dead.” With a dented can, a tattered map, and your sore knees, you follow the rhythm through a canyon of collapsed skyscrapers, pausing whenever something moves in the shadows. The dangers don’t soften; they wait. But the world still offers small decisions: water from the gutter, seeds in a glove, a red ribbon tied to a broken fence that might mean “someone lives here.”

By the time the first birds stutter back into song, you’re kneeling beside a rusted listening dish wired (crudely, beautifully) to a solar panel. It isn’t working perfectly, but it gives back a ghost of broadcast: a voice asking, *Are you still there?* You answer—or maybe the dream answers for you—with the only certainty left: not survival as theft, but survival as a promise. You begin to climb the dish, breath shallow, heart loud, and whisper the next thing you decide to do: *carry hope forward, one stubborn, unfinished signal at a time.*

Want me to keep the dream going, or steer it toward a specific tone—tender, strange, frightening, hopeful?
