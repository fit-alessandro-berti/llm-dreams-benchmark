You are the captain of a ship in the middle of a turbulent sea. Your crew looks to you for guidance as the storm worsens, and you take the wheel with both hands and do not let it go.

Salt slaps your face. The deck falls and returns. Lanterns swing like frantic pendulums. You do not shout first. You listen. The ship has a voice in weather like this: a groan in the keel, a high note in the rigging, the wet slap of canvas that has not yet torn.

“Reef the mainsail. Two men on the pumps. Bow into it.” Your voice comes out lower than you expect, as if the storm has already claimed the upper register. They move too readily. They do not argue. They only wait, faces lit green-white by lightning, as if the sentence itself were a rope.

A wave climbs the starboard rail higher than the wheel. Inside the water, for an instant, there is a harbor you left and a figure on the dock who does not wave. Then it breaks, and there is only black water and the wheel burning cold in your palms.

You keep the bow into the swell. Not because you are certain. Because the alternative is to let the ship turn its flank to the storm, and you have dreamed that ending before.

When the lightning comes again, the compass does not point north. It points at you.
