…written in disappearing ink.

At first, the page looks blank. Then, as your breath fogs the air above the paper, faint lines surface like veins under skin. Each question forms only long enough to be read before fading, forcing you to lean closer and closer until your forehead nearly touches the desk.

The first one says:

**1. What have you been pretending to want?**

You blink. That is not the kind of question that appears on a final exam. You glance around the room, but the other students are already writing furiously, their pens scratching like insects inside the walls. The proctor stands at the front, faceless beneath a soft gray hat, holding a stopwatch that ticks backward.

You look down again. A new question appears.

**2. If you fail, whose disappointment will you hear first?**

Your pencil trembles. You try to write an answer, but the moment the graphite touches the paper, your handwriting changes. It becomes smaller, more precise, and not quite yours. The words write themselves:

*My mother’s. My father’s. My own.*

The room grows colder.

You turn the page, hoping for something technical, something ordinary—a formula, a definition, a diagram you can memorize. Instead, you find a drawing of yourself sitting at this exact desk, seen from above. In the drawing, your chair is slightly crooked. Your shoulders are tense. Your mouth is moving, but there are no words coming out.

Beneath the drawing, the next question forms:

**3. What part of yourself have you been grading too harshly?**

The proctor’s stopwatch gives a loud click. Everyone else stops writing at once. The silence is so sudden it feels heavy, like water filling the room. You look up. Every student has turned to stare at you. Their expressions are not cruel. They are patient. They are waiting.

One of them whispers, “Answer it.”

Another whispers, “You already know.”

Your chest tightens. You look back at the exam, and now the paper is no longer paper. It is a mirror. In its surface, you see yourself—but not the version you expected. This version looks tired, yes, but also determined. There is a small flame burning behind their eyes, steady and stubborn.

The final question appears across the glass:

**4. If your future career could not be a job, what would it be?**

You understand then, in the way one understands things only in dreams, that the exam was never about the course. It was about the story you have been carrying into the future. The room, the proctor, the silent students—all of it leans in, waiting.

You place your pencil against the mirror.

This time, when you write, the words are yours.

*A life I do not have to apologize for.*

The stopwatch stops.

The questions disappear.

The paper turns white again.

And somewhere far away, beyond the walls of the dream, you begin to wake.
