You are in a world where colors and shapes are constantly changing. As you explore this surreal landscape, you come across a blank canvas and it is the only thing that is not moving.

Everything else is in flux. The hills to your left are breathing from ochre into violet, and their ridgelines keep folding themselves into new curves like paper being creased by an invisible hand. The sky is not one color but a slow argument between several. Even the ground under your feet won't hold a shape; when you look down, the path is hexagons, and when you look again it is a spill of overlapping circles, warm as bread. But the canvas is white. Flat, rectangular, quiet. It stands on an easel of plain wood in the middle of a field that cannot decide if it is grass or water, and the whiteness of it hurts a little, the way silence hurts after a long noise.

You walk toward it. The distance changes as you go, stretching and shrinking, so that sometimes you are almost close enough to touch it and sometimes it is a small pale stamp at the far edge of the world. You learn not to look at it directly while you walk. You look to the side, at the melting hills, and let your feet do the finding, and eventually you are there.

There is no brush. You check the easel's little tray and find only a shallow groove filled with something like dust, and when you stir it with your finger the dust has no color at all. It is not gray. It is not white. It is the color of a thing you have forgotten the name of.

You put your finger to the canvas anyway.

Where you touch it, a line appears, and the line is blue. Not any blue you chose; it is the exact blue of a bicycle you had when you were seven, a blue you had not thought about in decades, and seeing it makes your throat tighten. You draw another line and it comes out the green of a hospital corridor. A third is the orange of streetlight through curtains in a room where you once could not sleep. You understand, slowly, that the canvas is not taking colors from the world. The world has too many, and they won't stay still. The canvas is taking colors from you.

You keep going. You cannot help it. A curve becomes the brown of a dog's ear. A cluster of dots becomes the yellow of a raincoat someone wore to meet you at a station. You are not drawing anything in particular, only touching, and the canvas is answering with a color for each touch, and each color arrives with a small weight of memory attached, so that after a while you are standing very still with your finger raised, breathing carefully, like someone carrying too many cups.

Behind you, you notice, the landscape has begun to slow down.

Not stopped. But the hills change more lazily now, lingering on each color before trying the next. The sky has narrowed its argument to two shades and seems close to agreeing. The ground has been hexagons for almost a minute. It is as if the world, seeing that something can hold still, has remembered that it is allowed to.

You look back at the canvas and it is nearly full. Lines and dots and washes, none of it a picture, all of it yours. There is one corner left blank, a small triangle of white in the lower right, and you know without being told that whatever you put there will finish it, and that when it is finished the canvas will stop being the one still thing, because it will simply be a thing, complete, and the stillness will have to go somewhere else.

You hesitate. The wind, which is lilac, moves your hair.

Then you press your whole palm into the empty corner, and the color that comes is one you have never seen before. It has no memory attached. It is not from your past at all. It is warm and it is unnamed and you understand that it is from later, from a room you have not yet stood in, a morning that has not yet happened, and that the canvas has been saving it for you the whole time.

The landscape stops moving entirely. The hills are a deep and final green. The sky is one blue, the bicycle blue, from edge to edge. The ground is grass, only grass, and it smells like grass.

You take your hand away. The print of your palm stays in the corner, in that color you cannot name, and as you stand there looking at it you realize you are not in the field anymore. You are in the canvas, or the canvas has become the field, and the easel is behind you now like a door you have walked through, and ahead of you the world is still, and full of color, and waiting to be lived in rather than watched.

You start walking. Nothing changes as you go, and for the first time since you arrived, that feels like a gift.
