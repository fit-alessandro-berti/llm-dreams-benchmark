...speak, but the words come out as light instead of sound. The audience doesn't seem to mind — their faces tilt upward like flowers turning toward the sun, absorbing each syllable I don't say.

I glance down at my notes and find they've become a flock of small paper birds. They flutter off the podium and settle into the crowd, one landing on each person's shoulder, whispering to them what I meant to say. Somehow, this feels like it's working better than the original plan.

The microphone in my hand has grown warm and soft, like a sleeping animal. I have a vague, dreamy certainty that if I hold it close enough, it will purr.

Somewhere in the third row, I notice my grandmother — though she's been gone for years. She's wearing the yellow dress from the photograph on our kitchen wall, and she gives me a small nod that means *keep going, you're doing fine.* My chest fills with something warm and heavy at the same time.

I try to remember what the speech was supposed to be about. The topic keeps sliding away from me, like reaching for a bar of soap in the bath. Was it about the ocean? About forgiveness? About how to fold a paper crane? The audience nods along as though whatever I'm thinking, they're thinking too.

Then I realize I'm no longer standing on a stage. The stage has become a shoreline, and the audience has become waves, and I'm still speaking — or maybe the sea is speaking through me — and it occurs to me, with perfect dream-logic, that this was always how speeches were meant to work.

I wake up just before the applause, with the strange feeling that somewhere, someone heard exactly what they needed to hear.
