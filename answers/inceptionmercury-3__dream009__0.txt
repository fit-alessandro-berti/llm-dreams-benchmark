You wake up on a dirt road under a sky so wide and blue it feels like a foreign country. There is no hum of electricity, no distant traffic, no glow of a phone. You reach for your pocket and find nothing—no wallet, no keys, no device. Just your own clothes, which are all wrong.

Around you, stone cottages lean into one another. Smoke curls from chimneys. A woman in a woolen dress watches you from a door. A cart creaks past, pulled by a horse, and the driver calls something you almost understand. It smells like woodsmoke, fresh bread, and earth after rain. Crows cry overhead. Somewhere, a dog barks.

You are in another time. Not a ruin of the future, not a museum—a living, breathing past. And as you walk along the muddy lane, past children who stare and then laugh, past the sound of a blacksmith’s hammer, you realize you need to *stop being a ghost*.

These people can see you. They will ask you questions. They will notice your strange ways, your clean hands, your quiet terror. You need to learn the language, the customs, the rhythm of this world. You need to find shelter before nightfall. You need food, water, a story for why you are here.

But as dusk falls and the stars come out—so many stars, like a river of light you have never truly seen—you realize there may be a deeper need. You need to remember who you were before all the noise and screens and urgency. You need to wake up to this moment, even if it is only a dream.

And so you take a breath, and you let go of the world you knew. You walk toward the warmth of a candlelit window, and you knock.

Inside, a voice says, “Come in, stranger. You look like you’ve lost your way.”

You smile, because for the first time, you think you’ve found it.
