You are about to take the final exam for a course that determines your future career. As you sit down to start the exam, you notice the questions are written in your own handwriting.

Not merely handwriting like yours, but yours exactly: the impatient slant, the cramped loops, the small ink blots where you pause too long. At the top of the first page, beneath the university seal, you have written, Please answer honestly this time.

You look up. The examination hall stretches farther than it did when you entered. Rows of desks descend toward a distant blackboard as if the room has been tilted into a valley. Every other student is already writing. Their pens move with a dry, insect sound, although their exam papers appear blank. At the front, the proctor sits behind a clock with no hands.

You raise your arm.

The proctor raises hers at the same instant.

“There’s been a mistake,” you say.

Her lips form the words with you. There’s been a mistake.

Then she points to the page.

Question One: What did you want to become before you learned to answer that question correctly?

You try to remember the official material. There should have been formulas, case studies, diagrams you memorized until dawn. Instead you see the backyard of the house where you grew up, bright after rain. You are eight years old, kneeling in mud and building a hospital for injured moths. You remember believing that if you kept their wings dry enough, they would tell you where night went in the morning.

You write: I wanted to be someone animals were not afraid of.

The moment you put down the period, something taps against the high windows. Hundreds of moths cling to the outside of the glass, their wings opening and closing like small gray lungs.

Question Two: Name the person whose disappointment you have mistaken for your own ambition.

The ink begins to run before you can answer. It streams down the paper and pools on the floor beneath your desk. Reflected in the black puddle is your father, or perhaps your mother—it changes whenever you blink—standing in the doorway on the day you chose your course. They are smiling, proud and frightened, holding out a coat several sizes too large. You remember putting it on. You remember everyone congratulating you on how well it fit.

Around you, chairs scrape back. One by one, the other students stand and walk toward doors that have appeared along the walls. Above each door hangs a brass plaque: SURGEON, JUDGE, ARCHITECT, MINISTER, FAILURE. They enter without looking back. The last door remains unmarked.

You write no name. You draw the coat instead, empty and upright, with its sleeves reaching toward the margins.

The clock gives a loud tick. A single hand sprouts from its face and points at you.

“Half your life remaining,” announces the proctor.

“Half the exam?” you ask.

She shakes her head.

Question Three is printed across two pages: Outside this room is the life you will receive if you pass. Describe it in sufficient detail.

You know this one. You have rehearsed it for years. You write about the office on the upper floor, your name etched into frosted glass, the clean apartment, the calm voice you will use when people ask whether it was worth it. As you write, the wall beside you becomes transparent.

There is the office. There is the glass. Your older self sits behind a desk in a room without windows, signing page after page with the pen you are holding now. The older self looks successful in every visible way, but beneath the desk their feet are sunk ankle-deep in the same childhood mud. Moths lie motionless across the carpet.

Your older self glances up and sees you. For a moment neither of you moves. Then they press both palms against the transparent wall.

On their side of the glass, written backward in the fog of their breath, are the words: FAIL NOW.

You drop the pen.

All the writing in the hall stops.

The proctor rises. She is very tall now, so tall that her head disappears into the darkness above the lamps. “Candidates must answer every question,” she says.

“Who wrote this exam?”

She bends until her face is level with yours. It is your face, aged by a life you cannot recognize.

“You did,” she says. “At the end.”

The final page is heavier than the others. You turn it and find a small door set into the paper, complete with a silver handle. Beneath it is the last question.

What are you willing to lose in order to leave?

Behind you, the entrance to the hall has vanished. The unmarked door is still on the wall, but it has shrunk to the size of a postage stamp. The clock is ticking rapidly now. Each tick removes something from the room: first the windows, then the blackboard, then the rows of empty desks. With another tick, you forget the name of your course. With the next, you forget the career it promised. Panic rises in you, but beneath it is a strange, widening relief.

You pick up the pen. It has become warm and soft, a living thing with a pulse.

You write: I am willing to lose the future that was chosen because it frightened everyone least.

The sentence lifts from the page. The letters flutter around your head and settle among the moths. The tiny silver handle turns.

You open the paper door and find the backyard after rain.

It is night there, though morning light shines along the grass. Your childhood self is waiting beside the little hospital, cupping something in both hands. When you kneel, the child opens their fingers. Inside is the handless clock, no bigger than a coin.

“Did we pass?” the child asks.

You look back through the doorway. The proctor stands alone in the collapsing hall. Her severe expression has disappeared. She looks tired, almost hopeful. Beyond her, your older self is pounding against the glass, but the glass has begun to crack, and through the cracks grow thin green stems.

“I don’t know,” you tell the child.

This seems to be the correct answer.

The coin-clock splits like a seed. Roots thread between the child’s fingers, and a tree rises so quickly that its branches sweep the stars into new arrangements. Every moth takes flight at once. They pour upward through the leaves, carrying scraps of the exam on their backs until the sky is full of pale, unanswered questions.

Somewhere behind you, a bell rings.

You wake at your desk with your cheek against a blank sheet of paper. Morning has filled the room. For several seconds you cannot remember what day it is, only that something important was supposed to happen.

Then you see the admission ticket for the final exam beside your hand.

On its back, in a child’s careful handwriting, is a single line:

There is still time to choose a different door.
