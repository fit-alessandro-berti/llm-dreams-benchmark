You begin to say "Ladies and gentlemen," and the words dissolve before they reach the air.

Not dramatically. They simply thin out, like breath on cold glass, and the microphone in front of you picks up nothing but a small, bewildered hum — the sound of a room holding its breath along with you. The stageboards are warm under your feet, bread-warm, and the lights overhead have the syrupy weight of something poured. Somewhere behind you a page turns itself.

You try again. You open your mouth to say *thank you for being here*, and what comes out instead is a name.

"Marguerite Okonkwo."

The first row, third seat from the left, exhales. You didn't know her, but she has been waiting a long time to be called — you can see it in the way her hands unclasp in her lap, in the small private collapse of her shoulders, as though a knot she didn't know she was holding has been cut. She presses her fingers to her mouth. She is already crying, and she hasn't gotten up.

"Erik Lindqvist."

A man in the second row stands, shakily, holding a hat he no longer needs. He mouths *thank you* and sits back down, and the ripple of it moves through the crowd like wind through wheat.

You keep going. Names, hundreds of them, in a language you have never spoken and understand perfectly. Names from cities you have never visited. A woman who died in 1974 in a town called Velden. A boy of eleven who lost his mother on a Tuesday. A dog. You say the dog's name and the dog is there, in the seventh row, tongue out, thumping its tail, and half the audience laughs, and the laughter is the kind that comes when something breaks open in a good way.

And then you come to the last name on the list — you don't know it's the last, but you do — and it is your own.

You say it. The auditorium goes utterly quiet. Not disappointed. Not even surprised. Simply *finished*, the way a room is quiet when the rain finally stops and everyone can hear the eaves dripping.

You stand there with your mouth still open. Below you, a thousand faces look up at you with the tender, exhausted expression of people who have just been *seen* — and who love you for it, and who are already, terribly, waiting to be dreamed again.

Somewhere behind you the page turns itself one more time.

You wake up with your mouth open. There is a name on the tip of your tongue that you will never be able to say, and you spend the rest of your life trying to remember it — which is, of course, how it works, and which is, of course, the whole point.
