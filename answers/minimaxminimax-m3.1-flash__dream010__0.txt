# The Competition

The brochure said it would be simple. *Four rounds. One prize. Bring only yourself.*

That's what the flyer in the elevator said, right before the elevator kept going down past the basement, past the parking levels, past the place where the earth thickens into something warm and thinking. I should have stepped off at the fourth floor. Instead, the doors opened onto a long white corridor with a skylight the color of a bruise, and a woman in a grey suit was waiting with a clipboard and a brass bell.

"Your number," she said. "Don't be late. The dream doesn't wait for stragglers."

I didn't ask which dream. I already knew the shape of it — I'd been living inside it for years, the way a fish lives inside water and doesn't call it water until someone pulls it out.

---

**Round One: The Waiting Room.**

They put us in a room with twelve chairs arranged in a circle, and no walls — just the sky, and the sky was *low*, and it was the hour before a storm, so everybody could feel it thinking.

The first contestant was a man whose hands were made of stained glass. He stood up when his name was called and he was nervous, and he answered out loud, to all of us, honestly: "I want the job because I've never once in my life been sure that I'm allowed to want things."

The second wanted it to prove something to a father. The third wanted it so her daughter would never hear the word *settle* again. The fourth cried before the bell went and said she wanted it for no reason at all, just to have it, and the room went very quiet, because that was the bravest answer anyone had given.

My turn came and the brass bell made a sound like a spoon in a teacup.

"I want it," I said, "because I've been rehearsing this moment for so long that I've forgotten whether I'm rehearsing or remembering."

The woman wrote something down. She never told us what.

---

**Round Two: The Long Hall.**

We had to get to the far end of a corridor that was longer than the building could hold, and the rules were simple: *walk, don't run; don't touch the walls; if the floor asks you a question, answer it out loud.*

It started as a hallway. By the third minute it was a canyon. By the fifth it was a throat.

Doors opened into versions of my life, all slightly wrong. My childhood kitchen with the mustard-coloured light, but I was nine years old and I was *responsible* for something there. A classroom where I was the one teaching and the students were all sleeping with their eyes open. A kitchen table I remembered from my mother's house, except I was the one who had left, and I was the one still sitting at it, and my mother was packing a bag in the doorway, and she said, *You didn't even try.*

I kept my mouth shut. I walked.

The floor asked me a question then, low and enormous: *Is the person you were trying to become the same as the person you became?*

"Yes," I said, and I was only half lying, and the floor hummed a note that I felt in my teeth.

---

**Round Three: The Group.**

This was the one nobody warned us about. Six of us left the twelve. The survivors sat around a table and were told to build a bridge out of nothing, across nothing, for a town that didn't exist yet — and here's the trick: everyone at the table had to be needed. No unnecessary people. No passengers.

I learned that the second contestant, the one who wanted to prove something to his father, was also the best engineer in the room. He drew load calculations on a napkin with a fountain pen and his hands didn't shake once. The man with stained-glass hands turned out to be the only one who could see where the ground was, because the ground only existed as light.

And I found, to my own surprise, that I was good at one specific thing: I could make people feel like their small piece was the keystone.

We finished at 3 a.m. with a bridge that stood on nothing and held anyway.

---

**Round Four: The Interview.**

The panel was three chairs. The middle one was empty. In it, at a table with a single glass of water, sat a version of me with grey in her hair that I did not have yet.

"You're applying," she said, and it wasn't a question, and it wasn't a dream-within-a-dream either, it was just the tone of someone who had all the time in the world and had chosen to give you some of it.

"Inner-devotion application," confirmed the woman with the clipboard, taking a seat on the left. "That's the correct meeting time."

I looked at the older me. "What's the job?"

"That's the wrong question for the interview you're actually in," she said. "But I'll answer the one you're asking, because you'd have gone blank otherwise and I'd have had to wait."

She turned the glass of water slowly.

"The job is not a position," she said. "It's a Tuesday. It's a thing you do every day, badly at first, and then less badly, and then with something in your hands that you can't explain to anyone. It's small. It's the smallest career there is. You'll never be *famous*, not the way the brochure implied, and you'll be told you're wasting your talent by anyone who asks what you do, and you'll have to say it anyway, and *you'll have to mean it*."

"Why should I want it?"

"You're already awake," she said. "That's the whole audition. Everyone who's ever gotten here was asleep at the start and awake at the end. You just have to keep choosing to be awake, every single Tuesday, for the rest of your life, and it will never once feel like enough."

And then she was gone, and the chairs were empty, and the clipboard woman was writing something down, and outside the tall windows the bruised sky had gone the color of a clear morning.

---

**The Last Part, Which They Didn't Tell Us About.**

There was no handshake. There was no letter. There was just the corridor, and the elevator, and the button marked 4, and the sound of the building settling around me like an old friend getting comfortable.

I woke up at 6:12 on a Thursday, in my apartment, in my life, with the light coming through the blinds in stripes, and it took me a full minute to remember that the competition had been real, and then another full minute to understand that the brochure had been right about the only thing that mattered:

*Bring only yourself.*

And then, because I was already awake, and because it was Thursday, and because Thursday was a Tuesday's cousin — I got up, and I did the small thing.
