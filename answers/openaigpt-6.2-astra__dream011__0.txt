The first one reads, *A woman on the 6:10 train is carrying a birthday cake. Why does she get off three stops early?*

You remember the woman. You were nine, on your way home from the dentist. The cake tilted when the train lurched, and a little pink rose slid down the side of the box. You watched her get off. You have never thought of her since.

You write, *I don’t know.*

From somewhere beneath your desk comes the squeal of train brakes.

The next question asks what your father did with his hands in the hospital corridor. The one after that asks for the name of the boy who ate lunch alone beside the kiln. All around you, pens scratch busily. The clock has begun counting down in years.

You raise your hand. “Am I allowed to speak to the people in the questions?”

The proctor glances at the clock. “You have time for one.”

You almost choose your father. Instead, for reasons you can’t explain, you choose the woman on the train.

At once you are sitting opposite her, your adult knees wedged into the space your nine-year-old legs used to dangle in. The pink rose has just slipped. You ask your question.

“Oh,” she says. “I left my good umbrella at the bakery.”

“That’s all?”

“It was a very good umbrella.” She studies you. “Why? What did you think happened?”

You tell her about the exam. By the time you finish, she has missed her stop. For a moment the two of you stare through the glass at the receding platform. Then she laughs so hard she has to give you the cake to hold.

When you wake, the real exam is in forty minutes. You can’t remember what career it was supposed to qualify you for. You do remember, with perfect clarity, to keep the box level.
