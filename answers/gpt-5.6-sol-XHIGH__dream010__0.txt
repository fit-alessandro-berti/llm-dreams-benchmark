In the first room, the desks are arranged like a chessboard, and you must cross without being noticed by the people answering telephones. Every time a phone rings, one of them slides sideways like a bishop. You step carefully between their long cords while an enormous clock above the elevators counts backward in years instead of seconds.

The second challenge is an interview conducted underwater. You sit at the bottom of a tiled swimming pool in your best clothes, facing three judges with glass fishbowls over their heads. They ask what your greatest weakness is, but when you try to answer, silver bubbles emerge from your mouth. Inside each bubble is a tiny scene from your life: you arriving late to school, you forgetting someone's birthday, you pretending to understand a joke. The judges take notes while brightly colored fish swim through the table. At last you catch one bubble before it escapes and break it open with your finger. You tell them your greatest weakness is that you keep waiting for permission. Somehow your voice is perfectly clear. The pool drains at once.

Only four contestants remain. You recognize none of them, yet each feels strangely familiar. One has your childhood face, one wears the coat you lost years ago, and one casts your shadow instead of their own. The fourth is an old woman carrying a red toolbox. She smiles as if she has been expecting you and whispers that the final challenge is never the one printed in the rules.

A trumpet sounds. A door marked GRAND PRIZE opens onto a railway platform suspended above the clouds. On the opposite platform stands the dream job itself, though it is not a person or a place. It keeps changing shape: first a bright corner office, then a stage before an applauding crowd, then a quiet workshop full of half-finished inventions. Finally it becomes a small golden key floating just beyond the tracks.

The announcer explains that the winner must build a bridge before the last train arrives. Crates of polished materials appear beside each contestant. You receive a box containing only old rejection letters, blunt pencils, and the embarrassing certificates your mother once framed. The others begin constructing magnificent bridges of steel and glass. You try folding the letters into bricks, but the words bleed through the paper and stain your hands blue.

Far down the track, a headlight appears. The platform shakes. Panic moves through the contestants like wind through grass. Then you remember the old woman's warning. Instead of building outward, you kneel and unlock the red toolbox she has quietly left beside you. It contains no tools, only a small door hinge.

You fasten the hinge to the empty air. A narrow wooden door swings open where nothing had been. Beyond it is the same platform, except the competition banners are gone and morning light falls through tall windows. The golden key is already in your hand.

The announcer shouts that this is against the rules. The judges rise from behind their desk, still dripping pool water. The train roars closer, its engine shaped like the enormous backward clock. But the old woman steps between you and them. She opens her toolbox again, climbs inside, and vanishes. One by one, the other contestants abandon their bridges and follow you through the impossible door. The child with your face takes your hand. The one in the lost coat laughs. The one carrying your shadow finally gives it back.

On the other side, there is no prize ceremony. There is only a simple desk with a blank sheet of paper, a lamp, and a nameplate bearing your name in letters that rearrange themselves whenever you blink. You insert the golden key into the desk drawer. Inside is a contract, but every line asks the same question: What would you make if nobody were judging it?

You reach for the bluntest pencil. As soon as its point touches the page, the approaching train becomes the distant rumble of thunder. The office walls unfold into a wide green field, and all the ringing telephones turn into white birds lifting from the grass. You begin to write, although you cannot see the words. You only feel them gathering beneath your hand like a road forming just before your feet.

When you look up, the judges, the contestants, and the prize have disappeared. The child version of you is waiting at the edge of the field, holding the wooden door open. You ask whether you won.

The child shrugs and says, “You started.”

Then the door closes with the soft click of an alarm clock, and you wake with your fist clenched around an invisible key.
