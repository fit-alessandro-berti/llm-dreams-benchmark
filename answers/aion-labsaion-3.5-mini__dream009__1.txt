It's a strange thing, hearing your own name in a voice you don't recognize. You turn toward it and find a woman in a rough woolen dress, her face weathered like old leather, gesturing urgently toward the treeline. She says your name again—or something close to it—and you understand, somehow, the way you understand things in dreams: not through meaning, but through certainty.

Smoke is rising beyond the hills. Not chimney smoke. Something is burning.

She grabs your wrist and pulls, and you run with her through waist-high grass that whispers secrets you almost catch. The air smells of woodsmoke and crushed herbs and something older, something like rain that fell a thousand years ago. Your feet know this ground. That's the impossible part—you've never been here, and yet your body moves like it's coming home.

The village, when you reach it, is half-hidden in a valley: round thatched roofs, a stone well, goats tethered to a post. People are shouting. Children are being herded toward the fields like startled birds. And no one looks at the sky the way you do—no one seems surprised by the low, bruised-purple clouds gathering overhead, or the strange stillness before them.

Only you know what's coming. Only you remember weather reports, satellites, the word *storm* in a language no one here speaks.

You need to warn them. But how do you explain, in gestures and fragments, that the sky is about to open?

You climb onto the well and point at the clouds. You mime rain—fingers fluttering down—and then, arms wide, you mime *everything*. The woman from the field understands first. She barks something and the shouting changes tone. People begin moving with purpose: pulling animals loose, dragging hides over doorways, hurrying toward the stone structure at the village's edge that you now realize is a shelter of some kind.

The first drops fall as the last child disappears inside.

And then—dream logic, sudden and absolute—you're standing alone in the downpour, watching the village through a curtain of rain, and you feel it: not fear, not wonder, but a deep, aching familiarity, like remembering a place you left before you were born.

The woman appears beside you, holding out a cloak. She doesn't try to pull you inside. She just stands there with you, getting soaked, and says your name one more time—softly this time, like a question.

You open your mouth to answer—

—and wake up, with rain still drumming in your ears, though the window shows a dry, ordinary morning. For a moment you can't remember which world you belong to. Then the sound fades, and you're left with only the feeling: that somewhere, in some folded-away century, someone is still waiting for your answer.
