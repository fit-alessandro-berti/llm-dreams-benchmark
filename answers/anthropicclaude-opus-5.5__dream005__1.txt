...each group seems to be having a conversation you were supposed to prepare for.

The first cluster stands near a fountain that pours out warm light instead of water. They turn to you in unison and ask, "So, what's your position on the tides?" You open your mouth to answer, and what comes out is a perfect recitation of your childhood phone number. They nod thoughtfully, as though you've made an excellent point, and one of them writes it down on a napkin that immediately folds itself into a paper crane and flies toward the ceiling, which is much higher than it was a moment ago.

You slip away. The room has become a hallway, though you don't remember walking into one. The walls are lined with coats, hundreds of them, and you know with dream-certainty that one of them belongs to the person you're looking for. If you can find the right coat, you'll find them. You check the pockets of a green wool one and find a ticket stub for a movie you've never seen but somehow remember the ending of.

The hallway opens into a kitchen, where a second group is gathered around an island counter, laughing at a joke you just missed. Everyone here looks faintly familiar: a teacher from third grade, a barista whose name you never learned, someone you sat next to on a long flight years ago. They make room for you as if you've been gone only a minute. "You were just telling us about the house," the barista says. You don't know what house. But you start talking anyway, describing rooms with staircases that lead to other staircases and a door you were always told not to open, and they listen so kindly that you almost forget you're searching for someone.

Then you catch a glimpse of them, the one person you know, through the window. They're outside, in the garden, standing under a tree whose leaves are pages from old letters. You call their name, but the sound comes out as music, a few notes of a song you both used to know. They look up. They smile. They wave you over.

You try to reach the back door, but the party keeps rearranging itself. A third group, all wearing masks of their own faces, asks you to settle an argument about whether the moon is closer on Tuesdays. A waiter offers you a tray of tiny, glowing keys. You take one without thinking, and it's warm in your hand, like it's been waiting for you.

Finally you find the door. It's the one from the house you were describing, the one you were never supposed to open. The key fits.

You step through, and you're in the garden. The noise of the party fades behind you like a radio being turned down. Your friend is sitting on a bench now, under the letter-leaf tree, and they pat the seat beside them.

"I wondered how long it would take you," they say.

"There were so many people," you reply. "I didn't know any of them."

"You knew all of them," they say gently. "You just hadn't met them yet."

You sit. A leaf drifts down and lands in your lap. It's a letter in your own handwriting, addressed to you, but before you can read it, the words begin to blur. The garden brightens, softens, dissolves into the gray light of early morning.

You wake with the feeling of a warm key still pressed into your palm, and the last line of the letter just out of reach.
