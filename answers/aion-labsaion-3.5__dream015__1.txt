...keep walking.

The road ahead is cracked asphalt swallowed by pale green weeds, and the sky hangs low and violet, the way skies do only in dreams. You carry a canvas bag with a dented canteen, a handful of seeds, and a photograph of people whose faces you can't quite remember anymore.

You decide to plant the seeds.

It seems absurd — the world has burned, the soil is gray, and yet your hands move with strange certainty. You kneel in the rubble of what was once a gas station and press each seed into the ash with your thumb. And the moment the last one disappears into the earth, you hear it: a low hum beneath the ground, like the planet clearing its throat after a long silence.

By the time you stand, green shoots are already breaking through. They grow impossibly fast, curling up your arms like they know you, like they've been waiting decades for someone stubborn enough to try. The vines bloom with small white flowers that glow faintly, lighting the road ahead of you in the gathering dark.

You walk through the night following that light. In the distance, you see a flicker — a fire, a real one, tended by hands. Other survivors. You can hear laughter carried on the wind, thin and cautious but real. They've built something out of the wreckage: a windmill from car doors, a garden on a collapsed overpass, a flag made from a faded bedsheet.

As you approach, an old woman looks up from the fire and smiles like she's been expecting you. "You brought seeds," she says. Not a question.

You open your bag to show her, and inside, where the seeds were, there are now dozens of small lights, warm as embers. You hand them out one by one to the people around the fire, and each person tucks one into a pocket, into a lantern, into their chest.

The world outside the firelight is still broken. The dangers are still out there, breathing in the dark. But the circle of light is growing now, seed by seed, person by person, and you understand — the way you only understand things in dreams — that this is how worlds are rebuilt. Not all at once. Not by heroes. But by whoever keeps walking, and keeps planting, anyway.

Then the fire flickers, the old woman's face softens at the edges, and you feel yourself rising out of the dream — but the warmth of the seed in your palm stays with you, all the way up into waking.
