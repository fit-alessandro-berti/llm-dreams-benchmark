Without the constant, invisible hum of electricity you never realized you were always listening to, the air feels vast and startlingly sharp. You hear everything: the groan of wooden cartwheels over packed mud, the hiss of tallow sputtering on an open hearth, and the murmur of voices speaking a language that sounds like river water tumbling over stones. You don't recognize a single word, yet in the strange logic of the moment, you know they are talking about the weather and the harvest.

You wander through narrow lanes flanked by crooked timber-and-plaster houses, the air thick with woodsmoke, damp earth, and crushed rosemary. Every corner you turn, a tall stone tower looms above the thatched roofs. Its face has the circular indentation of a clock, though there are no numbers and no hands—only bare, weathered stone. No matter which direction you walk, the tower seems to stay directly ahead of you.

A sudden certainty settles in your chest: you aren't just lost here. You were supposed to bring something back.

Reaching into your other pocket, your fingers brush against something cold that wasn't there a second ago—a heavy iron key wrapped in a strip of faded linen. As you pull it out, the bustle of the street begins to quiet. People in coarse wool tunics pause beside their baskets and barrels, turning their heads toward you with calm, unblinking expectation, as if they have been waiting all day for someone to step out of the crowd holding that exact key.

The heavy oak door at the base of the tower groans open on its own, revealing a stone spiral staircase bathed in warm, flickering candlelight. Oddly, the stairs don't climb toward the top of the tower; they wind steeply downward into the earth. 

You step over the threshold just as the sky behind you flickers like an old film reel, and you realize you need to reach the bottom before you wake up—or before you forget which century was the dream.
