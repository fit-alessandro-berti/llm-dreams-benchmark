You are the captain of a ship in the middle of a turbulent sea. Your crew looks to you for guidance as the storm worsens, and you realize that every one of them has your face.

They are younger and older versions of you, though none is exactly the age you are now. A small boy in a yellow raincoat clings to the wheel. An old woman with your eyes is tying knots in the lightning. A young man you almost remember stands barefoot on the bowsprit, calling out depths that make no sense.

“Seven fathoms!” he cries. “Three birthdays! One regret and falling!”

The deck pitches beneath you. Beyond the railing, the waves have grown taller than mountains, and in their glassy sides you can see rooms suspended under the water: a kitchen with a kettle boiling, a school corridor lined with wet shoes, a bedroom where someone is sleeping beneath a blue blanket. Each time the ship rises, the rooms sink out of sight. Each time it plunges, they drift close enough that you can see people inside them turn toward the windows.

The crew waits for an order.

You open your mouth, but the storm takes your voice. It whips the words upward in a flock of black letters, which circle the mast and vanish into the clouds. The boy at the wheel stares at you accusingly.

Then the bell below deck begins to ring.

It is not the ship’s bell. It is a doorbell, the two-note chime from a house where you once lived. No one else moves, so you descend the narrow stairs. The passage below is impossibly long. Water runs ankle-deep along the floor, carrying teacups, silver fish, unopened envelopes. The lamps swing although the walls are perfectly still.

At the end of the passage stands your cabin door. Through its round brass porthole you see not the cabin but a calm, sunlit garden. Your mother is there, or someone who has agreed to look like her. She is seated at a white table, carefully folding a map into the shape of a bird.

You knock.

“You’re late,” she says without looking up.

“The ship is sinking.”

“Ships are always sinking,” she replies. “The trick is to decide what they are sinking toward.”

She holds the paper bird to the window. It flaps once, passes through the glass, and lands in your hands. When you unfold it, the map is blank except for a red line leading straight through a place labeled THE EYE.

Another wave strikes. The garden tilts. Trees slide soundlessly across the sky, their roots trailing soil like brown rain. You hurry back to the deck, but the stairs now climb through the center of a vast cathedral. Your crew sit in the pews, soaked and silent, while thunder rolls from the organ pipes. At the altar stands the old woman, still knotting lightning into a rope.

“Captain,” she says, “we can hold the storm or steer through it. Not both.”

You take the rope from her. It is warm and trembling, alive as an artery. Above the cathedral ceiling, you feel the mast bending. Below the floor, something enormous turns in its sleep.

“Let go,” you tell her.

Perhaps you say it to the crew. Perhaps you say it to yourself.

The knots come undone.

At once the cathedral bursts apart into sails, rain, bells, and white birds. You are back at the helm. The boy in the yellow raincoat has vanished, leaving two small wet handprints on the wheel. All around you the crew rush to their stations, their many versions of your face bright with terror. You point toward the darkest wall of cloud.

“There,” you shout, and this time your voice comes out as your own. “Straight through the eye.”

The ship turns. The storm seems to notice.

The sea rises in front of you, a single wave so immense that it contains the whole night. Stars burn inside it. Whales swim between constellations. Near its crest hangs the moon, tangled in a fishing net, and beneath the moon you glimpse the lost rooms again. In the bedroom, the sleeper beneath the blue blanket sits up. It is you, though you cannot see your face. The sleeping you lifts one hand, and the wave lifts with it.

The crew begin to sing. You know the melody but not the words. It is a song you heard before you were born, a work song for hauling the world over the horizon. The old woman braces herself beside you. The barefoot young man laughs into the rain. One by one, all your possible selves take hold of the same rope.

The ship climbs.

For a long time there is nothing but the steep black water and the groaning of timber. Then gravity loosens. The sails fill from below. Your coat streams upward, and the ship leaves the sea entirely, rising along the face of the wave like an elevator made of wood and wind.

At the top, everything stops.

You hover on the crest. On one side is the storm, boiling and endless. On the other is a circular plain of perfectly still water. At its center stands a lighthouse, though there is no island beneath it. Its beam does not rotate across the sea; it points down into the depths.

The ship slides into the calm.

Instantly the crew disappear. Their coats collapse on the deck, empty. The ropes hang slack. Even the rain is gone. You are alone with the soft creak of the wheel and the lighthouse shining downward.

As you draw near, you see a door at the base of the tower, level with the water. A figure waits there holding a lantern. It is the child in the yellow raincoat.

“Are we safe?” he asks.

You look back. The storm circles the calm sea like a wall. Shapes move inside the clouds: all the orders you might have given, all the routes you might have chosen, all the ships that did not reach this place.

“For now,” you say.

The child considers this and nods, as if it is the only honest answer. He raises the lantern. Beneath the ship, the water becomes clear. Far below lies a city on the ocean floor, its streets illuminated, its windows open. People move through the squares without drowning. They look up as your shadow passes over them, and you understand that this is where the sea has been carrying everything: the teacups, the letters, the houses, the forgotten names. Nothing lost, only living at a different depth.

The lighthouse beam touches your ship. The deck turns transparent. The hull dissolves plank by plank into silver fish, and still you remain standing at the wheel, steering something that is no longer there.

“What happens now?” the child asks.

You listen. From very far away comes the ringing of the doorbell again, then the kettle, then the first gull of morning. The calm water is beginning to brighten.

You place both hands on the invisible wheel.

“Now,” you tell him, “we bring everyone home.”

The lighthouse turns its beam upward. The submerged city rises like a reflection. Rooftops break the surface without a splash, followed by chimneys, gardens, and streets streaming with stars. The empty coats on deck fill with wind and stand beside you. One by one, your crew return, no longer wearing your face.

Ahead, where the storm had been, the horizon opens into a narrow golden line. You steer toward it. The ship gathers itself beneath your feet, made now of light, water, and all the hands holding on.

Just before you reach the dawn, you hear the boy laughing from high in the rigging. You look up and see the moon freed from its net, sailing ahead of you like a small white boat.
