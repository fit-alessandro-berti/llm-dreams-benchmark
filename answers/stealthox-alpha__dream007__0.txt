But when you turn toward the door, the door isn't there anymore. Instead there's a window—your childhood bedroom window—and beyond it, the street outside is flooded with warm, golden water that somehow doesn't wet anything it touches. Your friend is still talking behind you, but their voice has become muffled, like they're speaking underwater, and when you look back, their face keeps sliding out of focus, becoming briefly your mother's, then a stranger's, then theirs again.

"I'm not angry about the thing," your friend says, though you can't remember what the thing was anymore. "I'm angry about what it means."

You want to ask what it means, but your mouth is full of something soft—cotton, or petals, or pages torn from a book you once loved. You spit them into your hand and see they're photographs of the two of you, aged decades in an instant, gray-haired and laughing on a porch that doesn't exist yet.

The argument suddenly seems very far away, like it happened to someone else years ago. You realize with dream-certainty that you've had this fight a hundred times before, in a hundred versions of this room, and every time you walked away, and every time the door became a window.

"Stay," your friend says—not pleading, just stating a fact, the way one states that the sky is blue.

And you do stay. You sit down on the floor, which is now carpeted in cool grass, and the argument dissolves into something quieter—not agreement exactly, but the particular peace of two people who understand that some things will never be settled between them, and that this is its own kind of friendship.

Then you wake up, and for a moment you can't remember who you were arguing with, only that you miss them terribly—and that somewhere, in whatever version of the room they're in, they're missing you too.
