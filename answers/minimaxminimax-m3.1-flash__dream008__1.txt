The sentence hangs in the air unfinished, and the dream does the thing dreams do: it fills the silence with something that is almost, but not quite, right.

You are at your desk. Third floor, north window, the radiator that ticks like a very patient animal. Your boss is not in the doorway anymore. That's the first wrong thing. She's standing *behind your chair*, close enough that you can smell her perfume — gardenia, or maybe it's the smell of the office printer overheating — and when you try to turn to look at her, your neck won't move.

"Let's get you started," she says, and her voice is coming from your own mouth.

On the monitor: a project brief. **PROJECT: HOME.** **DELIVERABLE: ONE ROOM.** **DEADLINE: 5:00 PM.** The deadline field is the strange part — you know it should be a date, but the number 5:00 is already behind you, already *elapsed*, and yet the light in the window is the amber of 4:50 in the evening. The clock on the wall agrees with the sky and disagrees with the brief. You try to type a question. The cursor blinks in the field, patient as a metronome, and the words come out:

*What room?*

The cursor considers. Then, in the boss's voice, quietly, from the chair: "The one you keep walking past."

And now you're *moving*. That's the second wrong thing. Your feet have decided without consulting you.

---

The hallway of the third floor is longer than the building has any right to contain. Doors you have never seen are opening as you pass — the janitor's closet, a bathroom with no sign, a conference room where eleven versions of you are sitting around a table with their backs to you, and every one of them is speaking quietly, and every one of them stops when you pass, and every one of them *relaxes*, which somehow is worse.

The office at the end of the hall is labeled with your handwriting. You haven't written it. You write every day. It's the handwriting of someone very young, someone who held the pen in a fist.

Inside: a bed. A window with the same amber light behind it — the light of a day that is *almost* over, that is always almost over. On the bed, folded on top of a blanket, is a set of keys.

"You need to build something," says the boss, from the doorway, and she's not your boss anymore, she's just a shape in the doorway shaped like a person who is being kind to you. "You need to build it out of what's in the house. That's all you have. That's all anyone has. You can't go back for materials, and you can't ask anyone for help, and you can't do it faster than you can do it right."

"How long do I have?" you ask.

"Until you stop needing it," she says, and she is gone, and the door closes, and the sound it makes is the sound of a hand closing around a small thing.

---

The house is not the house. The house is *the room*, and the room is the whole world, and it is warm, and it smells of dust and clean laundry and something faintly like pear soap, and you know — with the absolute certainty that dreams are made of — that you have lived here before.

You begin.

The floorboards are the walls. You don't remember deciding that. You remember deciding that. There's a hammer that is somehow a screwdriver, and a saw that is somehow your own two hands, and you're measuring things with a strip of paper torn from the back of a calendar, and the calendar behind you is flipping, and every page you turn is a year, and every year is the same year.

You build a chair first. It's terrible. You build it anyway.

You build a shelf, and on the shelf you put the keys, and the keys don't open anything in this room, so you leave them there, and later they will be the only thing you have left, and you won't remember that this was the moment.

You build a window frame. You build it in the place where the window already is, and when you fit the last piece of trim, the wall *opens*, and behind it is not outside — behind it is more house, the same hallway, the same door, and standing in it, watching you with enormous, patient love, is someone who has been waiting for you to come down the stairs for a very long time. You don't know their face. Their face is the one thing the dream won't give you. They speak, and the words arrive without sound, the way certain things arrive: *You took your time. That's okay. I have nothing but time and pears.*

You build their room. You build it exactly. Fourteen steps from the front door. The window facing north. The bed with the wrong-colored blanket, because you liked the wrong color, and that was allowed, and it was *theirs to like*.

At 4:59 you nail the last board.

The light in the window doesn't change. That's the moment you understand — the deadline was never about the clock. It was about being *done before the light goes*, meaning: before you lose the memory of what it was like to be in the light with them. The building is the remembering. The room is the keeping. You can't build it out of materials; you're building it out of a life, and the more you build the more you have, and the more you have the more there is to lose, and the doors in the hallway were every door they've ever closed behind you, and the versions of you in the conference room were every time you chose not to come back.

You sit down on the floor of the completed room, in the good light, and the good light is the last of it, and you understand that you *will* be here in the morning and the morning will be fine and you will not remember any of this, and that is the arrangement, and that is the whole mercy of it: you will build it again. You will always build it again. That's not a punishment. That's the *point*.

Someone's hand on the back of your neck. Warm. A thumb moving once, twice, in that small absent rhythm that means *I know you're still here, I know you're trying.*

"You're done," says the boss, kindly. "Do you want to go back down?"

And what you say, in the dream, with your ear against her sleeve, is the only thing you've ever wanted to say to any of them, on any of the days you've been too busy building: *"I know. I know I took too long. I know."*

---

You wake up.

Fourth floor, north window. The radiator ticking like a very patient animal. A project brief in your inbox, and the deadline is real, and it's Thursday, and the work is genuinely impossible, the way work always is — which is to say, it is possible, but only one room at a time, only out of what's in the house, only if you begin before the light goes.

Your hands are warm.

You open a blank document, and you start.
