The architecture swells around you: gothic spires bleed into towering monoliths of mirrored glass, reflecting a sky the color of bruised plums.

You stop a passerby—a woman in an immaculate trench coat carrying a briefcase that clicks rhythmically like a metronome. "Excuse me," you plead, your voice oddly muffled, as though spoken underwater. "I have a meeting at noon. Where am I supposed to be?"

She doesn't break her stride, but as she passes, she looks at you with eyes the color of dawn and answers softly, "You’re already late to the only room that matters, but you’re walking the right way. Just turn at the sound of the cello."

You listen. Beneath the low hum of the city, through the sigh of passing electric trams whose tracks float three inches above the asphalt, you hear it: a single, resonant, mournful chord vibrating through the soles of your shoes. 

You turn the corner, and the crowded city dissolves. 

Suddenly, there are no cars, no pedestrians, no looming skyscrapers—only a solitary, ornate wooden door standing upright in the middle of a vast, wind-swept field of tall, silver grass. Nailed to the center of the door is a simple brass plaque engraved with your own name.

The ticking in your pocket stops completely. 

You take a breath, place your hand on the cold brass knob, and realize that you aren't late at all; the meeting was never with anyone else. You push the door open, step across the threshold, and the entire city dissolves into a sudden, blinding rush of morning light.
