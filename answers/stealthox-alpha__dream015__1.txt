...press on toward the distant glow on the horizon—a light that shouldn't exist, since every power grid died years ago. But dreams don't care about logic, and neither do you anymore.

You walk through streets choked with ash and rusted cars, your boots crunching on glass that glitters like frost. In your dream-hands you carry a canvas bag containing three things: a canteen half-full of water, a photograph of people whose faces keep shifting when you look at them, and a single seed wrapped in cloth. You don't remember where the seed came from, but you know—*know*, the way dreams make you know things—that it's the most important object left on Earth.

A dog appears at your side. Gray-muzzled, ribs showing, but its tail wags when it looks up at you. You never had a dog. Or maybe you did, long ago, before everything. In the dream, both are true.

The dangers come as they must: a collapsed overpass you have to climb through, your palms bleeding on rebar; a group of silhouettes watching you from a rooftop who, mercifully, decide you're not worth the trouble; a night spent hiding in a subway tunnel while something enormous breathes somewhere in the dark, each exhale warm against your neck.

But dreams are generous with time. You walk for what feels like years.

When you finally reach the glow, you find it's not a city or survivors or salvation. It's a greenhouse—impossibly intact, glass roofed, humming with old solar power—and inside, an old woman tending rows of green things. She doesn't seem surprised to see you. She holds out her hand, palm up, expectant.

You unwrap the seed and place it there. She nods once, satisfied, and plants it in a bed of dark soil near the window.

"Good," she says. "Now it can begin again."

You wake with the taste of rain in your mouth and, for reasons you can't explain, dirt under your fingernails.
