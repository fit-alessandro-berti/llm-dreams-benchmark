...and you find that the wheel is warm. Not sun-warm. Blood-warm, like the wrist of someone sleeping. You have been holding it for hours, or for years, and your hands have grown into the spokes the way roots grow into a fence.

You turn to give an order and the crew is closer than they should be — shoulder to shoulder, filling the deck, more of them than the ship could carry. The bosun has your father's hands. The one at the halyard is a woman you sat beside on a bus in 1998 and never spoke to. They are not afraid. That's the terrible part. They are *waiting*, the way children wait at the top of the stairs on a morning they trust you to make good.

You open your mouth and what comes out is: "Hold on to something you love."

It isn't an order. It's nonsense. But they nod, every one of them, solemn as a congregation, and they begin to go below deck to fetch things, and you hear cupboards opening under the water.

The rain stops mid-air. Not falls — *stops*, hanging in a lattice around the mast, ten thousand small glass beads, and you can see through them to a horizon that is much nearer than a horizon has any right to be. It's a hedge. It's the hedge behind your grandmother's house.

The sea flattens into a linoleum floor, yellow, worn pale in a path from the stove to the sink. The ship is still a ship but it is also the kitchen, and the storm turns out to have been a kettle in another room, screaming this whole time, and someone — you — should have taken it off the heat hours ago.

You go to take it off. The corridor to the other room is the length of the ship, then longer. The crew passes you carrying their beloved things: a bicycle bell, a dog, a folded shirt still warm from an iron. Nobody steers. The ship goes on perfectly well without you, which is a relief so enormous it wakes something in your chest.

You reach the stove. You lift the kettle. The scream thins to a whistle, then to breath, then to nothing, and in the sudden quiet you can hear the hull moving through calm black water, and beneath it, very deep, stars — the whole sky again, upside down, unhurried, being sailed over.

You stand there with the kettle in your hand thinking: *I should tell them we've arrived.*

But you don't know where. And the dream lets you keep standing there, warm, forgiven, holding the thing that was making all the noise.
