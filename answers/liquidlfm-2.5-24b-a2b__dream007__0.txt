You decide to stop shouting. You force your mouth shut, silencing the words that felt so necessary moments ago. The tension in the room doesn't dissipate; instead, it crystallizes.

The air grows heavy and cold. On the floor between you, the "important thing" you were arguing about—the core of your disagreement—solidifies into a jagged, towering wall of glass. It reflects both of your faces, but distorted, showing you older, sadder versions of yourselves.

Your friend looks at the wall, then back at you. They don't lower their defenses. They don't offer a quick apology or a compromise. They simply stand there, arms crossed, waiting.

You realize that in this state, you cannot break the glass with your fists or your voice. The only way forward is to change your perspective. You take a step to the side, not to avoid the confrontation, but to look *at* the wall from a different angle. From this new vantage point, the reflection shows that you are both right, and you are both wrong. The anger drains out of your limbs, replaced by a hollow exhaustion.

The dream shifts. The room fades into a soft, white mist. You hear your friend’s voice, distant and muffled, saying, "I don't want to be right, I just want you to know I love you."

You wake up with the lingering sensation of the silence—not the silence of an argument ended, but the silence of a space opened up. The wall of glass is gone, but the view is different now.
