You are at your workplace, and suddenly, you are given a project with an impossible deadline. Your boss is waiting for you to start, and you open your laptop, but the keyboard has too many keys — rows and rows of them, spilling over the edge of the desk like piano keys, and none of them are letters you recognize. You press one anyway and it plays a low note that everyone in the office turns to hear.

"The deadline was yesterday," your boss says, though he only just handed you the folder. The folder is heavy, heavier than paper should be, and when you open it the pages are all blank except for a single line in the middle of each one: DUE YESTERDAY. You flip faster and faster and the words don't change, only the ink gets darker, wetter, until it comes off on your fingers.

You decide you will simply work backwards. This makes perfect sense. If the deadline was yesterday, you will finish it yesterday. You stand up and walk toward the elevator, because everyone knows yesterday is on the floor below.

The elevator has only two buttons: LATER and BEFORE. You press BEFORE. The doors close on your boss's face, which is still saying something, his mouth moving in slow motion like he's underwater. The elevator doesn't descend so much as loosen, like a tooth.

When the doors open, it is the same office but earlier. The light is younger. Your coworkers are all there, but their faces are slightly wrong — they're the faces of people you went to school with, wearing your coworkers' clothes. One of them, who is both Marco from accounting and a boy from your third-grade class, hands you a coffee and says, "You'd better hurry, you haven't been given the project yet."

You sit at your desk in the yesterday-office and begin typing on the too-many-keyed keyboard, and now, somehow, it works — your fingers know where to go, playing the report like a sonata. The words appear on the screen faster than you can think them, and they're brilliant, they're the best work of your life, the sentences arranging themselves into charts and the charts blooming into gardens. You can hear the whole office humming along with the sound of your keys.

You print the finished project. The printer takes a very long time, and while you wait, you notice the office is emptying out, chairs pushed back, screens going dark one by one, the daylight in the windows draining like bathwater. When the printer finally exhales the last warm page, you're alone.

You take the stack of pages — still warm, like bread — and press LATER in the elevator, riding it back up to today to deliver it before it's due, which is now, which is always.

But when the doors open, the office is a field. Grass grows up between the desks. Your boss is there, standing in the tall weeds in his gray suit, and he looks at the pages in your hands with an expression you slowly recognize as your father's expression, from a long time ago, when you showed him a drawing you had made.

"You didn't have to do this," he says gently. "There was never any project."

You look down. The pages in your hands are blank again, all of them, and they're getting lighter, lifting out of your grip one by one, sailing off over the field like gulls. You feel you should chase them, and you take one step, then another, and then you're running through the grass with no shoes on, and the deadline is gone, the office is gone, and you're just running, the way you did when you were small, when running was the whole point of having a body.

Somewhere behind you a phone is ringing on a desk that no longer exists. You decide not to answer it. The sun is enormous and does not move. You run until the alarm folds the field neatly in half, and then in half again, and puts it away.
