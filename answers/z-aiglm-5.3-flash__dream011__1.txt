...written in your own handwriting.

You have never seen these questions before, but the looped letters, the way you cross your sevens — it's unmistakably your hand. The first question reads: *Explain everything.* It is worth 100 points.

The second question: *Define the year you were seven.* The third: *What did you do with the afternoon of June 14th? Show your work.*

Around you the lecture hall is full — though a moment ago it was empty — and everyone is already writing, pens whispering like rain. You reach for your pen and find you're holding a pencil the length of your arm. You write *I don't know* and the words sink into the paper like stones into water, leaving no mark.

The girl beside you — you don't know her, but in the way of dreams you know her completely, you've known her since before either of you existed — murmurs without looking up: "You're supposed to write it in the other language."

Of course. The other language. You knew it once. You can feel it sitting in your mouth like a word on the tip of your tongue, except it's an entire language on the tip of your tongue.

You look at the clock. The clock has no numbers, only a single hand, pointing at your seat.

Then you look down and you're writing — you don't remember starting — pages and pages of the other language pouring out of you, and you understand it perfectly as it leaves you but not a word once it lands on the page. The proctor passes your desk. He is your fourth-grade teacher. He is your father. He is both, the way dream people are both, and he says, kindly, terribly: "Ten minutes left." Somewhere a bell begins to ring, though the clock has no bell.

Ten minutes. Four hundred questions remaining. The pages are multiplying under your hands — flip one over and there are more questions beneath, and beneath those, more pages, a fathomless depth of pages. Your pencil is now very short, almost gone, the way pencils go in dreams. The hall has grown larger while you weren't looking. The proctor is very far away. You write faster, and you realize with sudden terrible clarity that you never attended this class. Not once. All semester. You don't even know what the course is called. You try to read the title on the front of the exam booklet, but the letters swim apart like fish.

The bell is louder now. It's your alarm. You wake — your ceiling, your blanket, gray morning light — and you lie there flooded with relief. It was a dream. You have no exam today.

Then, from the kitchen, your mother calls up the stairs: "You're going to be late!"

And you are at the desk again, booklet open, pencil in hand, and the questions are in your own handwriting, and the first one reads *Explain everything*, and the single hand on the numberless clock is pointing at you.

You wake for real this time — or you decide you have — heart hammering, and you try to remember what course it was, what career it was supposed to decide. You can't remember. You can't remember ever knowing. And beneath the relief, faint as a watermark, one question is still waiting for your answer.
