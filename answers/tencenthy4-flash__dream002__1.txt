You feel a storm approaching and the whole forest holds its breath.

The leaves turn their pale undersides to the sky. The path beneath your feet darkens, though no rain has fallen yet. Somewhere above you, thunder rolls like a great wooden door being shut in a distant house.

Then the wind arrives.

It moves through the trees in a long, low voice, and the branches bend as if they are bowing to someone ancient. You quicken your pace, but the path changes behind you, folding into moss and roots until you can no longer see the way you came. Ahead, a massive oak splits open—not with lightning, but with a soft golden light.

Inside its hollow trunk is a staircase.

 You step into the tree, expecting bark and darkness, but find warm stone walls and tiny windows glowing with blue fire. The staircase spirals downward into a quiet library beneath the forest. Shelves stretch toward a ceiling lost in shadow, filled not with books, but with jars of weather: bottled thunderstorms, folded clouds, a snowstorm glittering like crushed glass, a tiny hurricane turning lazily in a crystal sphere.

At the center of the room stands an old woman made partly of bark and partly of rain. She is pouring something from a silver pitcher into a bowl.

“You’re early,” she says.

“For what?” you ask.

She looks up. Her eyes are green with lightning.

“For the storm that remembers your name.”

Outside, the thunder cracks again, closer now. The windows shiver. The old woman gestures toward a small jar on the table. Inside it, a black cloud pulses like a heartbeat.

“It has been looking for you for years,” she says. “Every time it finds you, you wake before it can speak.”

You reach for the jar. The moment your fingers touch the glass, the cloud presses itself against the inside, forming words in pale script:

*I was never angry. I only wanted to be heard.*

The library goes silent.

Then the storm breaks.

Rain falls upward from the floor. The jars rattle. Books made of wind flap open. The old woman smiles and tips the silver pitcher into the bowl until it overflows, and the water becomes a doorway.

“Listen,” she says.

You lean close.

At first there is only static. Then, beneath it, a voice—your own, but younger—calling from far away. You understand suddenly that the storm is not weather at all, but every unspoken thing you have ever swallowed: every apology, every fear, every Unsaid word waiting in the limbs of the forest.

You open the jar.

The cloud rushes out, wraps around you gently, and whispers your name.

The rain stops.

The golden light fades.

You wake in your bed with the sound of leaves tapping against the window, though the sky is clear. Your hand is closed around a single acorn, warm as if it has just been held by someone else.

And somewhere far off, thunder murmurs softly, like a person turning over in their sleep.
