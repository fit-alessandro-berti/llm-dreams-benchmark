...find the clockmaker before the bells stop ringing.

I don't know how I know this. It's simply *true*, the way things are true in dreams. The knowledge sits in my chest like a swallowed stone.

The street beneath my feet is cobbled and slick with something that might be rain or might be honey. It shines amber under the gas lamps, though it's the middle of the afternoon. Women in long wool skirts pass me without looking, carrying baskets of bread that steam and whisper. A horse clops by pulling an empty cart, and when I glance at the driver's seat, there's only a folded coat sitting upright, reins looped neatly around one sleeve.

I reach into my pocket for my phone, the reflex of a lifetime, and find instead a brass key, warm as if someone has just been holding it. Etched along its shaft, in tiny looping script: *For the third door.*

The bells are ringing from a tower I can't see. Each toll lands a little softer than the last.

I ask a boy selling newspapers where the clockmaker lives. He looks at me with eyes far too old for his face and says, "Which one? The one who fixes time, or the one who sells it?" Before I can answer, he points down an alley that wasn't there a moment ago. The newspapers in his arms are all dated tomorrow.

The alley narrows until the walls brush my shoulders. I pass a first door, painted red, behind which I hear my grandmother humming a song I'd forgotten I knew. I want badly to open it, but the key grows cold in my hand. I pass a second door, made of rough driftwood, and smell the sea, though we are nowhere near the sea, and hear my own voice, younger, calling someone's name.

The third door is small, so small I have to kneel. The key fits perfectly.

Inside, the room is enormous, far larger than the building could possibly hold. Thousands of clocks cover every wall, every surface, hanging from the ceiling on silver threads. None of them tick in unison. At a workbench in the center sits an old woman with a jeweler's loupe over one eye, tinkering with a pocket watch no bigger than an acorn.

She doesn't look up. "You're late," she says. "Or early. It's hard to tell with you."

"The bells," I say. "They're stopping."

"They always stop. That's what bells do." She finally lifts her head, and I realize, with the dizzying calm of dreams, that she has my face, just weathered by decades I haven't lived yet. "The question isn't how to keep them ringing. The question is what you'll do when it's quiet."

She holds out the tiny watch. Its hands are spinning backward.

"This one's yours. It's been running wrong since you started waiting for your life to begin."

I take it. It's heavier than it looks, heavy as every postponed thing, every *someday*, every *when I'm ready*. The final bell tolls, so faint now it's almost just a memory of sound.

And then silence.

In the silence, the watch in my palm gives a single small click. The hands stop, shudder, and begin to move forward, slowly and steadily, in rhythm with my heartbeat.

"There," she says, turning back to her work. "Now go on. You've got a whole morning waiting."

The room dissolves like sugar in warm water. The clocks, the alley, the honey-slick streets all melt into soft gray light...

...and I wake to the sound of birds, sunlight across my blanket, and the strange, lingering sense that I've been given something back.
