# The Tower on Whitmore Hill

The generator died eleven days ago, and with it went the last of the hot water, so you now wake in the dark with your breath fogging the inside of your sleeping bag and the cold pressing in like a hand on your chest.

You lie still and listen to the settlement breathe. The pipes tick. Someone's shelter tarp crackles in the wind like a sail. Far off, north of the ridge, the dogs are running again — you can hear them, four or five of them, working the landfill slope. You count the sounds without wanting to. It's become a habit. Counting things tells you the world is still there.

Your name is a thing you gave yourself, since the old one belonged to someone with a job and a mother's kitchen and a life that made no sense anymore. *Wren.* Small bird. Fast. You liked the implication.

You are thirty-one years old, or thirty-four, or twenty-nine. You stopped counting when the calendar ran out of months, and then when the year ran out, and now you keep time in the old way: *rains, freezes, the long grey, the burning season.* It's been nine of those since the grid died. Nine years since the lights went out everywhere at once, not in a storm but in a silence so total the whole world seemed to hold its breath and never exhale. Nobody knows why. Some say it was the cold. Some say it was the wars. Some say it was just the way electricity was always going to end, one tired wire at a time.

You've stopped needing the reason. The reason is *now.*

---

## The Sump

You live in a half-flooded storm drain at the bottom of the Whitmore valley, in the space where the runoff goes when it comes. There's a settlement of maybe two hundred people in the pipe network and the maintenance sheds around it: forty-three families, a trade post, a water station, a schoolroom where a woman named Ovi teaches children letters from a book she memorized and recites like songs.

It's a good place, honestly. It's the best place you've ever lived. There is a rule here, unstated, unwritten but absolute:

**Nothing leaves the valley.**

Everyone who goes up past the ridge doesn't come back. Not bandits. Not the dogs. Something worse — the *emptiness.* The long grey roads where the power stations stand with their arms raised, dead and grey and patient, and the wind that comes down off them smelling like rust and old rain. The world up there is unclaimed and unclaiming, and it takes people the way the sea takes shoes.

You came down into the Sump on foot, eight years ago, starving, and Ovi gave you a job with the water filters and never asked where you'd been. That's the thing about the Sump: nobody asks. You owe nothing. You belong to no ledger. You can die here, and if you do, no one will write it down.

It is the safest place on earth.

---

## The Decision

This morning — the third day of the hard cold, the generator dead, the pipes ticking, the dogs running on the far slope — you sit on the concrete lip above the drainage channel and you make a decision.

You decide to climb Whitmore Hill.

There is a radio tower on the top of Whitmore Hill. A hundred and forty feet of rusted lattice, and on the third platform there's a transmitter that might still work, if the batteries hold, if the antenna's intact, if there's anything left to talk to. Once, a long time ago, there were people in this valley who traded with the coast. There was a hospital with working generators. There was a school with a roof. There was a whole world, humming.

You want to find out if any of it is still there.

Not for you. You've already made peace with the Sump; you're good at peace. It's Ovi. It's the schoolroom. It's the way kids here are learning letters they will never use to write to anyone, in a valley where the radio has been a piece of folklore for nine years, and if there *is* someone out there — if there's a coast, if there's a hospital with a generator, if there's a settlement that has kept its lights on — then a child in the Sump might get antibiotics for the ear infection that has been going around for a month. Might get a doctor. Might get a *future* that extends past the ridge.

You sit there with the cold in your chest and you think about how stupid this is. Which is fine. Stupid is what you have. You've got nothing but stupid and a coil of wire.

---

## The Climb

The hill is not that tall. It's just that nothing on it is friendly.

You go up at dusk, when the dogs sleep, carrying a jerry can of fuel, a coil of copper wire, a length of climbing rope, a pry bar, and a wind-up radio you found in a drawer four years ago and have never once managed to power.

The valley floor gives you to the scrub, and the scrub gives you to the dead forest, and the dead forest gives you to the old road. The asphalt is heaving now, buckled into ridges where the frost has been working for nine years, and you move up it like a man crossing a frozen sea.

There's a car on the shoulder, nose-down in a ditch, doors open, the seats gone to leather and mice. You don't look inside. Everybody looks inside. That's how you learn to be hungry.

Two hours in, you hear something behind you and you stop and stand very still with the pry bar in your fist, and it goes past — a coyote, thin as a wire, trotting along the road like it owns the place. Which, in fact, it does.

By the time you see the first power station, your legs are shaking. It's the Whitmore Substation, a grey concrete box with a switchyard full of skeletal pylons, and the smell comes down off it exactly the way you remember: rust, ozone, old rain. Your stomach turns. You keep going. You've come this far; turning around now would mean you came this far for nothing.

**Nothing has ever been for nothing. That's the whole lesson of the Sump.**

You spend the night in the substation control building, in a nest of shredded insulation, and you don't sleep. You watch the moon come up over the hill, and the tower's shadow lies across the ground like a fallen ladder, and you think that if you die here, nobody at the Sump will even know you were born.

---

## The Platform

The tower is in better shape than you hoped. The lattice is corroded but standing. Someone has been up here before — there's a bolt pattern in the concrete at the base, and a ladder, and at the third platform the hatch is open.

The generator is a hand-crank, military surplus, chained to the platform with the chain rusted into a single solid link. You take the pry bar and you set to work, and the sound of it — that *chunk chunk chunk* of iron on iron — carries out across the valley, out over the whole dead world, and you find you don't care. You are making noise. For the first time in nine years, you are making noise on purpose.

Forty minutes. The link gives. The crank turns.

The transmitter wakes up in stages: a hiss, then a carrier, then that deep hum that means power is moving through the wires toward the antenna. The signal goes out over the hill like heat.

And you stand there with the hand on the switch and you realize you have no idea who to call. You don't have a frequency. You don't have a call sign. You don't know the number of anyone's house. You have spent nine years learning the precise, careful, necessary skill of *not reaching out.*

You look at the hand-crank. You look at the antenna, sixty feet of rusted aluminum pointing at nothing in particular.

So you do the only thing you can do. You pick up the old radio. You wind the handle until the dial lights up amber. You turn it slow, through the dead bands, the hisses, the ghost-whispers of long-dead stations, the flat dead air where the cities used to be.

And on 89.3, out of nowhere, out of the static, so faint you almost talk over it:

*"—...repeat, this is Coast Guard Station Nine, Whitmore sector, to any station receiving, do you receive—"*

You stop breathing.

*"—we are operating a generator station and we have medical supplies and we are attempting to re-establish relay to the coastal grid. To any station receiving. To any station receiving."*

---

## After

It takes you four days to get back down.

You sleep in the substation, and you leave the generator running with the fuel you brought, and you climb the tower twice a day to keep the carrier on the air, and you talk — you *talk* — for six hours a day, giving them your coordinates, describing the valley, the Sump, the two hundred people in the drainage pipes who have been waiting nine years for a boat or a doctor or a *word.*

They send a boat. It takes eleven weeks to come up the coast and around the point, and it comes, four of them, sunburned and astonished, with crates of antibiotics and a wind turbine repair manual and a working radio.

Ovi cries, which she has not done in front of the children before, and she is embarrassed about it, and she laughs at herself, and the kids think something wonderful has happened and are disappointed when they find out what it actually was: a boat, and medicine, and a future that now extends past the ridge.

The thing you were sure of — that the world would take you the way the sea takes shoes — turns out to be false. The world took nine years, and it let you keep your name.

You tell the children about it. You tell them it was stupid, that you nearly died on a hill for nothing, that you spent four days in a cold concrete box talking to a radio like an idiot.

You tell them it was the best thing you ever did.

And then you teach them how to work the crank.
