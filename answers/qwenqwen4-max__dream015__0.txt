...you decide to plant an orchard.

Not because you're hungry. You are — hunger has become a piece of furniture in the house of you, something you walk around without seeing — but because you found a seed catalog in the glovebox of a burned-out sedan, and the descriptions are written in a language so hopeful it makes you furious. *Reliable producer. Excellent keeper. Bears heavily in the fourth year.*

The fourth year. Nobody has said a number that large out loud in a long time.

So you dig in the median of the highway, where the runoff gathers and the ash has gone soft and black as potting soil. You dig with a hubcap. The traffic lights still cycle overhead for cars that aren't coming, green yellow red, patient as a heartbeat, and you plant twelve small dry nothings in a row and tamp the dirt down with your thumbs.

There's a girl on the overpass with a bucket. She's been there three days. On the fourth she comes down and pours water on your hands as much as on the roots, and neither of you says anything, because talking costs something you can't spare.

Then the dream does what dreams do: the trees come up while you watch, wrong-fast, unfolding like paper flowers dropped in a glass. The pears are heavy and gold and when you bite one it tastes like the kitchen of a house you lived in before you were born — linoleum, sunlight, someone in the next room humming.

A man comes over the guardrail with a rifle and asks what you think you're doing.

*Making a place worth arriving at,* you say.

And then you are the girl on the overpass. You are holding the bucket. Below you, someone with your hands is kneeling in the dirt, and you understand — with the total, unarguable certainty of dreams — that you have to be the one who goes down and brings the water.

You start down the slope. The wind smells like rain.

Somewhere a fan is turning.
