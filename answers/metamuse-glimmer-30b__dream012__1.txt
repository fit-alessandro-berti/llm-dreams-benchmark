You are the captain of a ship in the middle of a turbulent sea. Your crew looks to you for guidance as the storm worsens, and you realize you have no voice.

You open your mouth to shout an order, to tell them to reef the sails or batten down, but only a rush of wind comes out. The words are there, perfect and urgent, right behind your teeth, and they never make it past your throat.

The wheel in your hands is slick with water, or maybe sweat. It turns, but not because you're turning it. The ship is steering itself, lurching hard to starboard as a wall of black water rises up. The crew — you know their faces, but you can't remember their names — are pressed against the rail, eyes wide, waiting for you to do something.

You try to stand taller. Your coat is heavy, the captain's coat you were given when you were twelve in a school play. The brass buttons are warm. Below deck, you hear a low knocking, like someone tapping on the hull from the inside.

Then the storm changes. The rain stops falling down and starts falling *up*, lifting off the deck in silver sheets. The sea around you turns flat and glassy, and you can see straight through it to the streetlights of a city you recognize, far below.

One of the crew, a boy with your own face, tugs at your sleeve. "Captain," he says, "it's time to dock."

You look down at the wheel and realize it's not a ship's wheel at all. It's the steering wheel of your old car, and your feet aren't on the deck, they're tangled in bedsheets.

The thunder is your alarm clock.
