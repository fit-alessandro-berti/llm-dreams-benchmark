start to hear someone following you. Their footsteps stop whenever yours do. At the next junction, while you work out which of six brass levers to pull, you hear them sit down with a little sigh.

“Are you stuck too?” you call.

“I’m waiting for you,” says a child.

Around the corner sits a small girl in your old yellow raincoat. One of the toggles is missing. You remember chewing it off on the bus.

“What happens if I pull the wrong lever?” you ask.

“I don’t know,” she says. “You never have.”

You pull the nearest one. Somewhere behind the wall a bell gives a rather ordinary ding. A narrow cupboard swings open. Inside are a broom, a dustpan, and a pair of muddy boots.

The girl looks delighted. “All this time there’s been a cupboard?”

At the next puzzle you deliberately put the red stone in the blue hollow. A window opens onto a rainy afternoon. You can smell the pavement. At the next, you give a stone lion the wrong answer to its riddle. It rolls onto its back and lets you scratch its chin.

By now you’re running. The girl is a little ahead of you, yellow coat flashing at the turns. At the very center of the maze you find a kitchen. Rain ticks against the window. There’s a crossword on the table in your father’s handwriting, and two slices of bread waiting in the toaster.

You know this morning. In a minute you’ll miss the bus. Your father will drive you to school, still in his slippers, and the two of you will laugh about it all the way there.

From the hall he calls, “Either of you know a five-letter word for hurry?”

You and the girl look at each other.

“Toast,” you call back.

After a moment you hear his pencil moving.
