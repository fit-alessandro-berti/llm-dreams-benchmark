...you decide to plant a garden.

The seeds are already in your pocket, which surprises you, though in the dream it doesn't—dreams are generous that way. You kneel in the ash-soft earth beneath a fallen overpass and press them in one at a time, and the sky overhead is the exact color of a bruise healing.

When you look up again, the garden is grown. This also doesn't surprise you. Sunflowers stand tall as streetlights. Tomatoes glow faintly, like paper lanterns. And there are footprints threading through the rows that aren't yours.

You follow them—you were kneeling a moment ago, and now you're walking; the dream doesn't explain the seam, and neither do you. The footprints lead through a city gone soft with ivy, past a rusted-out car where a fox sits watching you with unreadable patience. In the way of dreams, you understand the fox perfectly. It's telling you: *others came. Others saw.*

You find them at dusk, a handful of strangers at a fire built inside a grand piano someone dragged onto a rooftop. One of them plucks the strings that haven't burned yet, and the notes come out warped and warm, like the piano is dreaming too. They make room for you without asking your name. A woman with gray in her beard hands you a tin cup of something that tastes like every kitchen from your childhood, all at once.

"We kept the fire going," she says, "in case someone was coming."

You tell them about the garden. They already know. Somehow they already know.

Morning arrives sideways, the way it does in dreams, and you wake with the phantom feeling of dirt under your fingernails. You check. Nothing there. But all day you keep looking at your hands, remembering what it felt like to press something small and alive into broken ground—and to believe, without a shred of doubt, that it would grow.
