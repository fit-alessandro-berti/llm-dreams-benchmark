You are feeling lost and alone in a bustling city. Suddenly, a group of kind strangers offers to help you find your way, and you feel something loosen in your chest, like a knot untying itself without your permission.

There are five of them, or maybe seven — the number keeps shifting when you try to count. A woman in a yellow raincoat takes your left hand as if she has always held it. An old man with a paper map that shows no streets, only weather, walks ahead of you, folding and unfolding it, saying, "It's this way, it's always been this way." The others hum quietly, a tune you knew as a child but had filed away somewhere unreachable, and now it comes back to you complete, every note.

The city rearranges itself to make room for you. Streets that were walls become doorways. The traffic parts like a slow gray river, and the crowds — the same crowds that an hour ago pushed past you as if you were made of fog — now turn their faces toward you and nod, one by one, as though you are a small parade.

"Where are we going?" you ask.

"Home," says the woman in the raincoat, and you want to tell her that you don't live here, that your home is far away, in another city or perhaps another life, but the word feels true anyway, so you keep walking.

You pass a bakery where the bread in the window is shaped like the houses of everyone you've ever loved. You pass a fountain where the water flows upward, gathering into clouds that drift off carrying small silver fish. The old man consults his weather map and announces, "Kindness ahead, with scattered forgiveness by evening," and the group laughs, and you laugh too, and your laugh sounds younger than you remember it.

The buildings begin to lean in, gently, like they are listening. You realize the strangers are telling stories about you — not to you, about you — small true things you never told anyone. How you once gave up your seat and pretended you were getting off anyway. How you talk to houseplants when you think no one is listening. How lonely you have been, and for how long. You wait to feel exposed, but instead you feel accounted for, the way a coat feels when someone hangs it up carefully instead of tossing it on a chair.

"How do you know all this?" you ask.

"We were lost once too," says a boy with pockets full of streetlight. "Everyone in this city was. That's how the city works. You get found, and then you become one of the finders."

The street tilts upward and becomes a staircase, and the staircase becomes the fire escape of a building you have never seen but recognize completely. At the top there is a door the color of the first door you ever had a key to. The woman in the yellow raincoat lets go of your hand, and you feel the shape of her grip stay behind, warm, like a stone kept in a pocket.

"You'll be all right now," she says.

"Will I see you again?"

"Every time someone looks lost," she says, "check your own hands."

You look down. Your hands are full of small lights, soft as moths. When you look up, the strangers are already dissolving into the crowd, becoming the crowd, and the crowd is beautiful now — every hurried person is carrying someone's found way home in their pockets and doesn't know it yet.

You open the door. Inside is not a room but the whole city again, seen from above, glowing like a circuit of veins, and you understand that you were never lost, only unheld, and that these are different things.

Somewhere below, a person stands alone at an intersection, turning in slow circles the way you did.

You take the stairs down two at a time, your hands still glowing, and the dream lets you reach them just as you wake — your arm stretched out across the bed, offering directions to the dark.
