...open your mouth to shout an order, only to realize you don't actually know how to sail a ship.

The salt spray stings your eyes, tasting strangely of cold rain and static. You grip the wooden helm, expecting to feel the violent strain of the rudder fighting the current, but the wheel spins effortlessly beneath your palms, clicking softly like the dial of an old safe. Around you, the deck pitches steeply. When you glance back at your crew, their rain-slicked faces refuse to stay still—one is a stranger with eyes like sea glass, another is someone you vaguely remember from years ago, wearing a heavy winter coat completely unsuited for the ocean. Yet they all stare at you with absolute, unwavering trust.

Ahead of the bow, a wave rises impossibly high, dark as obsidian and laced with glowing white foam. It hangs over the ship like a cliff of moving glass, refusing to break. You look up to check the rigging, only to find that the sails are no longer canvas—they are enormous, billowing sheets of handwritten paper, the ink bleeding into dark rivers that run down the masts.

A familiar, heavy dread tightens in your chest. You try to yell *hard to starboard*, or *drop the anchor*, anything to prove you belong at the helm, but the howling wind steals your voice before it can even leave your throat. 

So, guided by a sudden, inexplicable instinct, you let go of the wheel completely.

You step back and raise your hands. The crew doesn't panic; they simply watch, swaying in quiet unison with the tilting deck. The massive black wave curls over the ship, blotting out the lightning-streaked sky and plunging everything into a deep, translucent green twilight. You brace yourself for the crushing weight of the water.

Instead, the sound cuts out entirely.

The roar of the tempest drops into a soft, distant hum, like the quiet buzz of a house late at night. The ship doesn't sink; it tilts gently upward and begins to glide smoothly up the sheer face of the wave, defying gravity as easily as a leaf caught in a warm draft. Around you, the falling rain slows and hangs suspended in the air like thousands of shimmering silver beads.

When the bow finally breaches the crest, the ocean is gone.

The ship slips seamlessly out of the dark water and into a vast, silent expanse of twilight sky. Below the keel, instead of waves, stretches an endless, slow-moving sea of clouds, glowing softly from within like paper lanterns. The wind turns warm and gentle. The crew's heavy coats dry in an instant, and someone near the bow begins playing a slow, half-remembered melody on an accordion you hadn't noticed before.

You look down at the brass table in front of you, where your nautical chart is pinned. The coastlines, depths, and compass roses have all faded away, replaced by the faint, shifting floor plan of a house you used to live in. You still don't know how to steer, and you have no idea where the ship is going—only that the storm is behind you now, and you're somehow heading home.
