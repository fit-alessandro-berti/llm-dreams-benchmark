You are the captain of a ship in the middle of a turbulent sea. Your crew looks to you for guidance as the storm worsens, and you open your mouth to give an order and find that you do not know the names of anything.

Not the sails. Not the ropes. You know that the thing tied off to the left of the wheel is important, that it is holding something up, but the word for it has gone the way of words in dreams, and the crew is still looking at you. There are eleven of them. You count them twice. The second time there are nine, and nobody seems to have noticed the difference, so you decide not to mention it.

"Hold her steady," you say, because that is a thing captains say, and it works. The men move. The ship leans into a wave the size of a church and comes down the other side with a sound like a door slamming somewhere very far below you. Water comes over the rail, black and then white and then black again. It is warm. That is the first thing that tells you something is wrong with the sea. Storm water should be cold, and this is the temperature of a bath someone has left and forgotten.

The first mate comes up beside you. You know he is the first mate because he is wearing your father's coat. He has your father's way of standing too, slightly turned, as if he is always about to leave a room. He does not have your father's face. He has no face you can settle on; every time you look at him it has been rearranged slightly, like furniture in a house you visit only at holidays.

"She wants to know where we're going," he says.

"Who does?"

He nods down at the deck, meaning the ship, and you understand that he is serious, that in this sea the ship is a woman with wants, and you owe her an answer.

You look for the compass. There is a compass. The needle is not pointing anywhere; it is turning, slow and patient, like the hand of a clock that has decided to count something other than time. You watch it go around once, and in the time it takes you have the strong feeling that you have been on this ship for years, that you have had this conversation with this man before, in this weather, and that each time you have failed to answer.

"Home," you say.

The first mate does not laugh, exactly, but the coat moves the way it did when your father laughed. "Which one," he says.

The storm gets worse to give you time to think about that.

Lightning shows you the sea, and the sea is not empty. There are other ships out there. Dozens. They ride the same waves you ride, at the same angle, their masts tilting when yours tilts, and on each deck there is a small figure at the wheel, and you know without needing to be told that every one of them is you. Some of the ships are in better shape. One of them has already lost its mast and is turning broadside to the weather, and you watch the captain of that ship, who is you, stand very still at the useless wheel with his hands at his sides, and you feel sorry for him in a distant, administrative way, the way you feel sorry for people in newspapers.

"Don't look at them," says the first mate. "They'll look back."

You look away. Too late. Across the water, one of the other captains has turned his head. You cannot see his eyes but you can feel the attention arrive, like a hand on the back of your neck.

Below deck, someone is singing. It is a song you knew as a child, but the words have been replaced with instructions. Reef the main. Bear away. Count your men again. You go down the ladder to find the singer and the ladder goes on much longer than the ship is deep. The rungs are wet. Then they are dry. Then they are carpeted, and you are on the stairs of a house, and the storm is a sound in the walls, the sound a house makes in wind when you are small and cannot sleep.

In the kitchen at the bottom of the stairs a woman is sitting at a table with a map. The map is of the sea you are sailing. She is drawing on it with a pencil, very carefully, a line that starts at a small ink ship and wanders out into the blue, doubles back, crosses itself, and stops. Where it stops she has written a word and covered it with her hand.

"You're doing fine," she says, without looking up.

"I don't know the names of anything."

"Nobody does. That's not what the names are for." She turns the pencil around and erases a piece of the line, a loop where the ship had gone in a circle for what looks like days. "There. You never did that."

You want to ask her what the word is under her hand. You know that if you ask she will lift her hand and show you, and that this will end something. So you do not ask. You stand there in the kitchen with the sea in the walls, and she draws, and every mark she makes you feel in your knees, a shift of the deck.

"They're waiting for you," she says. "Go on up."

The stairs are a ladder again before you reach the top. The deck, when you come out onto it, is quieter. The rain has become the kind that falls straight down. The crew is at the rail, all of them, and you count and get eleven, and then you count again and get eleven, and the relief of that is so large you have to hold the wheel to stay upright.

The other ships are gone. Or, not gone: they have come alongside, all of them, and folded into yours, the way a hand of cards is squared up before it is put away. You can feel them in the timbers. The ship is heavier and steadier and has more history than it did an hour ago, and when it takes the next wave it does so like it has taken that wave before, many times, in many versions, and has finally found the angle.

The first mate hands you the compass. The needle has stopped. It is pointing at him, and then when he steps aside it is pointing past him, at a patch of the horizon where the cloud is thinner and the dark is a slightly different dark.

"That'll be it," he says.

"Is it?"

He shrugs your father's shoulders. "It's where the needle stopped. That's all a heading ever is."

You bring the wheel around. The ship answers with the whole weight of the fleet she has swallowed, slow and certain, and the bow comes up out of the trough and points at the thin place in the sky. Behind you the storm is still going, and it will go for a long time, but it is behind you now, which is a direction, which is more than you had.

You notice you have said the word for the rope. You do not remember saying it, but the crew is hauling on it, and the sail it holds is filling, and the name is in the air the way the sound of a bell is in the air after the bell has stopped.

You do not remember, either, what the woman wrote on the map. But the ship seems to. You let her have it. You hold the wheel and you watch the thin place widen, very slowly, into something you could almost call morning, and you keep your hands where they are, and you do not look back to count the ships.
