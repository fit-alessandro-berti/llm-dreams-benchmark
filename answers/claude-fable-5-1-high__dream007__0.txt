You are in the middle of a heated argument with a close friend. The disagreement is about something important to both of you, and you decide to stop talking and simply show them.

You take their wrist, the way you used to when you were both small and crossing a road, and you lead them out of the kitchen. Except the kitchen door does not open onto the hallway anymore. It opens onto the beach where you spent the summer you were nineteen, and the sand is the exact grey of the countertop you were just leaning against. Your friend does not seem surprised. They are still talking, still making their point, but the wind takes every third word and you can only hear the shape of the sentence, its rise and fall, like a song you know without knowing the lyrics.

"You never listen," they say, and this time the wind lets the whole thing through.

"I am listening," you say. "That's what this is."

You want to show them the thing you cannot say. You walk them down to where the water comes in, and you point, and they look, and there is nothing there but water. You feel the familiar frustration climb your throat. It was here. You were sure it was here. The argument had felt so clear a minute ago, so obviously right, and now it has turned into a low tide with nothing on it.

Your friend sits down in the wet sand in their good trousers. This is unlike them. You sit down too.

"Say it again," they say. "The thing you were saying inside."

You open your mouth and what comes out is not words but a small paper boat. It sits on your tongue for a moment and then you lift it off with two fingers and set it on the water. It is folded from a page you recognize, a letter, your handwriting from years ago, and it floats out a few feet and turns in a slow circle and comes back. Your friend picks it up, unfolds it, reads it. Their face does something you have never seen it do.

"Oh," they say. "You meant this."

"Yes."

"You should have just said."

"I was saying."

And you both laugh, the way you laugh after a fight when you are too tired to keep fighting and the laughing is really a kind of apology neither of you wants to make first. The sound of it goes out across the water and does not come back.

Then the tide starts to go the wrong way. It draws off the beach fast, faster than any tide, and where it pulls away it leaves things. Not shells. Rooms. Your friend's first apartment, the one with the radiator that clanked. The back seat of a car at three in the morning outside a hospital. A table in a bar with two coasters torn into confetti. Each room is only a few inches deep, lying flat on the sand like a photograph with walls, and you can walk between them and look down into them, and you understand that this is what the argument was actually about. Not the thing itself. All of this. Every place where one of you had been right and had not said so, or had been wrong and had not said so either.

Your friend crouches at the edge of the hospital car and reaches down and touches the seat. Their hand comes away wet.

"I didn't know you kept these," they say.

"I didn't know I did."

You keep walking. The rooms get older. There is one you don't recognize, a bedroom with a window and a night-light shaped like a moon, and a child in the bed who is your friend before you knew them, and you realize you are looking at something that was never yours to keep and yet here it is, laid out on the sand for you with the rest. You feel you should look away. You don't. Your friend stands next to you and looks too, and does not seem to mind, and the child in the bed turns over and says a word in their sleep that you both hear clearly. It is your name.

"That's not possible," you say.

"It's a dream," your friend says, reasonably. "It doesn't have to be."

The water begins to come back now. It fills the rooms one by one, the way a flood fills a house, from the bottom up, without hurry. The radiator goes under. The bar table. The car. You want to save something and you crouch down and reach for the night-light but the water is already over it and your hand goes through the surface and finds only sand, ordinary, cool, a little coarse.

You stand up. Your friend has your other wrist now, and you are not sure when that happened, or who is leading who.

"So were you right?" they ask.

You think about it honestly, for what feels like the first time all night. "I don't remember what I was saying."

"Me neither."

"It was important though."

"It was," they agree. "It still is. That's why it's out there." They nod at the water, which is calm now, and full, and in it you can see, very far down, a faint grid of lights, like a town at night seen from a plane. All the rooms, still lit.

You walk back up the beach together. The kitchen door is where you left it, standing by itself in the sand, no wall around it, a rectangle of yellow light. Through it you can hear the kettle you put on before the argument started, just now beginning to whistle, which means none of this took any time at all.

At the door your friend stops. "Next time," they say, "just hand me the boat first."

"Next time," you say, "just read it."

You go inside. The kettle is screaming. You turn it off, and in the silence after, you realize you have both already forgiven each other, and that neither of you is going to say so, and that this is fine, and that it has always been fine, and that this is what it means to be known by someone. You pour two cups. Your friend sits at the counter. The window over the sink shows the hallway, not the beach, and you're a little sad about that, and then you're not, because the hallway has a light on at the end of it that you don't remember leaving on, and you have a feeling that if you walked down there the floor would be a little wet, and a little sandy, and that would be all right too.
