(You find yourself in a post-apocalyptic world, with resources scarce and dangers everywhere. Despite the hardships, you decide to...)

...crawl to the edge of the dried-up creek bed where the cracked earth still holds a thin, hidden line of moisture beneath the dust. You haven’t slept well—wind rattled the broken tin roof of the half-collapsed barn last night, and the scent of burnt metal lingers in your throat—but your hands know the place. Last week, you found a single green shoot pushing through a fissure where a rain barrel once sat. It’s small, trembling, leaves curled tight against the heat. No water yet today, just the ghost of last week’s dew.

You don’t have a bucket, no rope, no fancy tool. You use the smooth, shard-edge of the broken coffee mug you salvaged from the rubble—carefully, slowly—to scrape away just enough loose soil around the base without disturbing the fragile roots. You press your forehead to the cool earth for a breath, listening: the high buzz of distant winged scavengers (not the big ones, just the thin-winged ones that mean no harm), the dry sigh of wind through broken glass, the faint, almost imperceptible *drip*… from somewhere *under* the ground, not above.

Then, with knuckles white, you tilt the mug just so, not pouring—*guiding*. A single, deliberate stream of the last precious sip from your canteen (half full, shared yesterday with the woman who lost her dog near the ridge) slides down the inside of the mug’s rim, trickling *into* the scraped circle around the shoot. Not much. Just enough to kiss the roots. You watch the tiny drop vanish into the soil, then another, then a third. You don’t cheer. You don’t think about survival *today*. You just watch the shoot’s leaves uncurl, millimeter by millimeter, as if remembering how to breathe.

A shadow flickers at the tree line—could be a fox, could be the edge of your own shadow—but you don’t flinch. You keep your eyes on the shoot. The wind hushes for a second. The tiny green tip lifts, just slightly, toward the weak morning sun breaking over the burnt-out skyline.

You don’t fix the world. You don’t find the hidden vault or the working generator. You just… tend the one thing that asked for nothing but a little space and a little water. Your shoulders drop, not with exhaustion, but with a quiet, wordless softness. No grand plan forms. No promise echoes. Just this: *I saw it. I gave it what it needed, right here, right now. It didn’t die.*

You sit back on your heels, the mug empty but clean in your hand, the dust on your knees cool against your skin. The sky is the color of faded ash and pale lavender. Somewhere, a bird calls—one clear note, not a warning. You breathe out slowly, long, and for the first time all week, you don’t think about *what’s missing*. You think only of the shoot, and the tiny drop of water that found its way home.

*(The dream holds for a few more seconds—the light shifts, the shoot’s leaves tremble again—then, softly, the image dissolves back into the quiet hum of the empty street outside the barn, but the feeling of tending lingers in your chest like a small, warm stone. You wake, not with a gasp, but with the quiet certainty that somewhere, under the cracked earth, something green is still trying.)*
