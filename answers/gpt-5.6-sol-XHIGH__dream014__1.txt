You are feeling lost and alone in a bustling city. Suddenly, a group of kind strangers offers to help you find your way, and you follow them beneath an awning striped the color of rain.

There are five of them, though whenever you try to count their faces there are six. An old woman carries a birdcage with no bird inside it. A boy in a red coat holds a folded map. Two sisters walk hand in hand, whispering the names of streets just before you reach them. The last stranger is a tall man with silver spectacles who keeps glancing behind you as if making sure your loneliness is coming too.

“Where are you trying to go?” the boy asks.

You open your mouth, but the name of the place has vanished. You remember only a yellow door, the smell of toast, and someone waiting beside a window. This seems like enough. The strangers nod gravely, as though you have given them a precise address.

They lead you through the crowd. Everyone else is hurrying in the opposite direction, carrying clocks, suitcases, armfuls of white flowers. The buildings lean over the avenue and exchange balconies high above your head. Traffic lights bloom green, then blue. Each time you look back, the street you have crossed rolls itself up like a carpet, hiding the route behind you.

At the entrance to the underground, the old woman opens her empty cage. A small wind flies out. It circles your head once, warm and feathery, then rushes down the stairs. You follow.

No trains run below the city. Instead, a black river passes between tiled platforms. Paper boats drift on it, each carrying a candle and a tiny sleeping passenger. The sisters find a boat large enough for all of you. The tall man pushes away from the platform with his umbrella, and the current takes you into a tunnel filled with the muffled sounds of rooms overhead: cutlery ringing, babies crying, radios announcing tomorrow’s weather in forgotten languages.

“Have I been here before?” you ask.

“Everyone has,” says one sister.

“But nobody remembers the same way twice,” says the other.

The boy unfolds his map. It is blank except for a small blue dot that pulses like a heartbeat. You realize the dot is not showing where you are. It is showing where you were happiest. As the boat moves, the dot keeps sliding toward the edge of the page.

You reach for it. The paper gives beneath your finger, and for an instant your hand passes into sunlight. You feel grass, the rough bark of a tree, a dog’s nose pressed eagerly against your palm. Then the map snaps shut, and the tunnel is dark again.

“We’re close,” the strangers say together.

The river spills them into a station beneath a vast glass roof. Rain is falling upward from the floor, each drop rising into the night. Beyond the gates stands the yellow door from your memory, alone in the middle of the concourse. Warm light shines beneath it. You hear someone moving on the other side, setting down two cups.

Relief surges through you so suddenly that you begin to laugh. You hurry toward the door, but the strangers remain by the river. When you turn to thank them, you see that each one is holding something you lost while wandering: the old woman has your courage tucked beneath her arm; the sisters share your name between them like a scarf; the tall man carries a small suitcase full of all the mornings you thought would never come. The boy holds the empty birdcage.

“Aren’t you coming?” you ask.

They shake their heads.

“We only know the way as far as the door.”

You try the handle. It will not move. There is no keyhole, only a shallow hollow shaped exactly like your hand. When you press your palm into it, the city falls silent. The upward rain hangs motionless. Far above, every lit window turns toward you.

The voice behind the door says, “You took so long.”

You know the voice. It belongs to the person you were before you became lost.

Fear makes you pull your hand away. At once the yellow paint begins to peel, revealing dozens of other doors beneath it: the door of your childhood bedroom, the door of a house you never lived in, a hospital door, a garden gate, the blue door of a train. They flicker one over another, all waiting for you to choose.

The strangers do not urge you. They simply stand nearby, kind and patient, while the station begins to fill with the same hurried people from the streets above. One by one, they set down their clocks and flowers. Some seem just as lost as you were. A woman looks at you with tears in her eyes and asks whether you know the way out.

You look at the blank map still tucked under the boy’s arm. Now you understand why it could never tell you where to go.

You take your courage from the old woman, your name from the sisters, and the suitcase of mornings from the tall man. Then you open the birdcage. The warm little wind returns, but this time it does not fly toward the yellow door. It passes through the station, stirring coats and hair, and slips into a narrow passage you had not noticed before. Along its walls, lamps awaken one after another.

“This way,” you tell the weeping woman.

Others turn to listen. The strangers smile. The boy gives you the map, and as soon as you hold it, new lines begin to draw themselves across the paper—not a single route, but hundreds, crossing and joining like veins in a leaf. The blue dot divides into many lights. None is far away.

You lead the crowd into the passage. Behind you, the yellow door opens at last, spilling the smell of toast and morning through the station. You do not need to turn around to know that no one is waiting there anymore. Whoever it was has stepped quietly into you.

The passage climbs. With every step, the suitcase grows lighter. At the top is no grand destination, only a small street washed clean by rain. Dawn rests on the rooftops. Shops are lifting their shutters, pigeons are arguing on a statue, and somewhere a kettle begins to sing.

The people disperse, each following a different line on the map. When you look for the kind strangers, they have disappeared into the waking city. In the empty birdcage, however, you find a folded scrap of paper.

It reads: YOU ARE HERE.

For the first time, the words feel less like a location than a promise. You put the note in your pocket and walk on as the streets unfold beneath your feet, making room for you wherever you choose to go.
