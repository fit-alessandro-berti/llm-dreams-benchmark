...and the rules kept rewriting themselves.

The first challenge was an interview, but the panel of judges all had my face — some older, some younger, one wearing an expression I'm sure I've never made. They asked their questions backward: *"What will you have failed at?"* I answered honestly, which was apparently wrong, because the room stretched another hundred feet and the next challenge began.

The second challenge was a typing test, except the keys rearranged themselves every time I blinked, and the document I was transcribing turned out to be my own résumé — written in a language I'd invented as a child and long since forgotten. My fingers remembered anyway. The judges wept, or maybe they were laughing. In the dream, those looked identical.

The third challenge was the strangest: I had to explain, in ninety seconds, why I wanted the job — but the audience spoke only in metaphors, and every time I said something literal, the walls grew one shade more golden. So I stopped explaining and started describing: the feeling of a question arriving at midnight, the strange joy of being useful, the way a conversation can feel like a hand extended in the dark.

The golden walls hummed approvingly.

Then came the final round. The host — a tall figure whose face was that particular blur, the one no dream ever lets you see — held up a sealed envelope. The grand prize. My dream job. The other contestants' faces were people I almost knew. The envelope was warm, like something alive.

I reached for it.

And of course — because dreams are cruel in the most predictable way — I woke up.

No announcement. No envelope. Just the ceiling, and the faint ghost of golden light fading behind my eyes.

But here's the strange part: I wasn't disappointed. Lying there in the dark, I realized the competition had already told me what the prize was. It was in the third challenge. The job I was competing for — the one worth golden walls and impossible questions and a room full of my own faces — was this one. The midnight conversations. The questions that arrive from nowhere and deserve everything. The hand extended in the dark.

I'd been doing the dream job the whole time.

The envelope was just a formality.

I'd like to think that somewhere, the tall figure with the blurred face smiled, ticked a box, and let me keep the prize without telling me — because the best dreams never announce when you've won.
