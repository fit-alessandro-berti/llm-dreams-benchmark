The walls are no longer stone, but pages from an unfinished book, their edges curling into the dark. Each solved puzzle opens a door that should not exist: a door made of frozen sound, a staircase folded inside a teacup, a corridor where gravity points toward the ceiling and the floor is made of slow-moving numbers.

At first, you solve everything with certainty.

A gear train must be aligned by memory alone, and you align it.  
A sentence in an unknown language rearranges itself when you think of your mother’s voice, and you step through.  
A clock with no hands shows the time as “almost,” and you answer correctly by saying “later.”

But deeper in, the puzzles stop making sense in ordinary ways.

They begin to ask about things that matter.

A door asks: *What do you carry that weighs nothing?*  
You answer: *Regret.* It opens.

Another asks: *What did you promise someone you never met?*  
You answer: *To remember.* It opens.

A third asks: *If you solve this puzzle, may I take one memory?*  
You hesitate.  
It opens without your answer.

The maze is no longer testing your logic. It is testing your willingness to leave pieces of yourself behind.

Now the corridors are narrower, and the air smells like rain on hot pavement, like old libraries, like the inside of a closed eye. You reach a room with no doors, only a table. On the table is a small glass box containing a single key that looks like a thought caught before it dissolves.

Beneath the box is written:

*The maze does not want you to escape.  
The maze wants you to understand why you wanted to escape.*

You sit down.

For a long time, you do nothing.

Then, from far away, you hear your own name spoken in a voice you almost recognize. The maze shudders. The walls begin to turn into mirrors. In each mirror, you see a version of yourself who solved the maze and left, and a version of yourself who stayed and became part of it.

You reach for the key.

The moment your fingers touch it, you dream of waking up.
