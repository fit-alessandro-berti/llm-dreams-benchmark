

…suddenly the challenge is not a test of skill at all, but a test of remembering.

A long corridor appears before you, its floor made of shimmering glass. Below the glass, your own life plays out in slow motion: a childhood room, a rainy street, a door you almost knocked on, a conversation you never had. Each memory flickers like a light about to go out.

A voice without a face says, “To earn the dream job, you must give up the dream that made you hungry for it.”

You step forward. The first challenge is a staircase with no top. As you climb, the rungs turn into old versions of yourself—versions that gave up, versions that stayed safe, versions that were too afraid to want more. You walk over them gently, and each one becomes a soft white light.

The second challenge is a room filled with doors labeled with every job you once thought you could not have. You open one and find yourself inside a version of your life where you never applied. It looks comfortable. Safe. Almost beautiful. But the light is dim, and your own footsteps echo as if you are already disappearing.

You close the door.

The third challenge is the hardest: a mirror.

In the mirror, the dream job is already yours. You are sitting in the right chair, wearing the right title, receiving the respect, recognition, and success. It should feel like victory. Instead, it feels hollow, because you realize you have been competing not for the job itself, but for the proof that you are finally enough.

The glass begins to crack.

A new challenge appears: a blank page.

It floats in the center of the room, and on it is a single line:

*What would you do if the grand prize were never given?*

Before you can answer, the dream shifts.

The competition hall disappears. In its place is a small desk in a quiet room, and for the first time, no one is watching. You sit down. You begin to work—not for the prize, not for the applause, not for the title, but because the work itself feels like coming home.

The door behind you clicks shut.

Then you understand:

the dream job was never the grand prize.

It was the life you started building the moment you stopped competing for permission to want it.
