...not made of words at all, but of tiny, shimmering puddles of water resting on the paper. 

When you lean in closer to read Question 1, you see your own reflection staring back, except you are seven years old and missing your two front teeth. A voice echoes from the ceiling—it sounds like your high school gym teacher speaking through a seashell—announcing, *"You have forty-five minutes. Remember to show your grief."*

Panic bubbles in your chest. You look around the vast auditorium to see how everyone else is handling this, only to realize the room has stretched infinitely upward into a dense canopy of redwood trees. Your classmates don't seem bothered at all. The girl to your left is furiously knitting a sweater out of yellow caution tape, and the guy in front of you is calmly pouring sand into a briefcase. 

You look down at your #2 pencil, desperate to just write *something*, anything, to pass. Make a mark. Secure your future. Start a career. 

But the pencil has gone entirely limp, bending like the stem of a wilted dandelion. When you press the eraser to the paper, it leaves a trail of crushed mint leaves. Suddenly, you understand the prompt—the way you only suddenly understand things in dreams. You don't need to answer the questions; you need to fold them.

With clumsy, heavy fingers, you begin folding the soggy exam paper. The clock at the front of the room—which has no numbers, only phases of the moon—starts spinning wildly. *Tick, tick, tick,* loud as a heartbeat. Sweat beads on your forehead as you crease the corners, folding the water-questions inward until you are holding a crisp, glowing paper boat.

"Pencils down," whispers the forest. 

The floor beneath your desk dissolves into a warm, dark ocean, rising quickly past your ankles, your knees, your waist. Instead of drowning, you find the paper boat has grown large enough to hold you. You climb inside just as your desk drifts away into the current. 

Lying back on the giant folded exam, looking up as the redwood branches part to reveal a sky full of unfamiliar purple constellations, the anxiety completely vanishes. You passed. You don't remember what career this was supposed to be for, anyway—drifting into the starlight feels like a much better job.
