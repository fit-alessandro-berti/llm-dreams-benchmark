You find yourself in a post-apocalyptic world, with resources scarce and dangers everywhere. Despite the hardships, you decide to open a library.

Not a real one. You know that. But there is a building on the corner of what used to be Halsey Street with most of its roof still on, and someone before you had already dragged in shelves, and the shelves are full of things that are not books but that you shelve anyway: a bicycle wheel, a jar of buttons, a wedding dress folded into a square the size of a Bible, a stack of empty picture frames. You give each of them a number in pencil on a strip of masking tape. You keep a ledger. You do not know why this feels so urgent, only that when you finish a shelf you can breathe.

The sky is the color of a dirty spoon. It has been that color since you arrived, and you have the sense that you arrived a long time ago, though you cannot remember the arriving. In the mornings ash falls, soft as flour, and you sweep it into a dustpan and put the dustpan on the shelf too, number 341. Nobody comes for weeks. Then a woman comes.

She is wearing three coats and carrying a small dog that does not move. She stands in the doorway and looks at your shelves and says, "Do you have anything on how to fix a radio."

You do not. You tell her you do not. But you go along the rows anyway, running your fingers over the tape numbers, and you stop at 117, which is a shoebox, and inside the shoebox is a radio, and inside the radio, when you open the back, there is a folded note that says, in your own handwriting, THE BATTERY IS IN THE DOG.

You look at the woman. She looks at the dog. The dog looks at nothing, because it is made of felt and wire, and when she turns it over there is a zipper along its belly and inside is a battery the size of a brick, still warm.

"How did you know," she says.

"I catalogued it," you say, which is not an answer, but she nods as if it is.

The radio works. It picks up one station. The station is a man reading numbers very slowly, and then between the numbers he says a name, and the name is yours. You turn the radio off. The woman does not ask. She leaves the dog on the counter as payment, and you give it a number, 342, and you write in the ledger: dog, felt, hollow, purpose unknown.

After that people come every day.

They come in pairs and in dozens, with their faces wrapped, with their hands out. They bring you things and they take things and it stops mattering which is which. A boy brings a photograph of a house and asks if you know where it is. You look at the photograph and you know the house, you know it the way you know your own teeth, and you take him up onto the roof and point, and the house is there, at the end of the street, standing whole in the middle of the rubble like a tooth in a broken jaw. He runs toward it. You never see him again, but that night a light comes on in one of its windows, and stays on.

You begin to understand that the shelves are not for keeping things. They are for finding them.

You go down into the basement, which you had not known was there. The stairs are wet. The basement is bigger than the building above it, much bigger, and the shelves down here are already numbered and the numbers go into the tens of thousands, and the handwriting on the tape is yours, older, more tired, the pencil pressed harder. Shelf 8,004: a set of house keys. Shelf 8,005: a key ring with no keys. Shelf 8,006: a door.

It is only the door, leaned against the shelf like a painting. You open it anyway. Behind it is your kitchen. Not a kitchen. Yours. The kettle is on. Someone has left the radio playing, and the man is still reading numbers, and it is only now, in the warm yellow light with the smell of toast, that you understand the numbers are dates, and the dates are going backward, and he is almost done.

You do not go in. You cannot say why. You close the door and you write on a fresh piece of tape, 8,006, KITCHEN, DO NOT LEND, and you press it to the wood so hard the pencil snaps.

Upstairs the woman with the three coats is back. She has brought others. They are standing very quietly in the aisles, touching nothing, and when you come up the stairs they all turn to look at you the way the dog looked at nothing.

"We heard our names," she says. "On the radio. We came to check them out."

You open the ledger. The pages are full. Every line is a name, and every name has a number, and beside each number there is a small mark you do not recognize, a hook, or a check, or a bent nail. You run your finger down the column until you find your own, and beside it there is no mark yet. The space is blank. The pencil is broken in your pocket.

"I'm sorry," you say. "The library is closing."

Nobody moves. The ash comes down through the hole in the roof and settles on their shoulders, and on the shelves, and on the open page, softly, patiently, filling the blank space the way snow fills a footprint, and you stand behind the counter and wait to see what shape it will take.
