The green light beneath the canopy turns the color of old silver. Leaves lift their pale undersides toward the sky, though there is no wind yet, and every fern along the path bends in the same direction, pointing deeper into the woods.

You know you should turn back. You can remember arriving at the forest, but not from where, and when you look behind you the path has already narrowed to a dark seam between the trees. Ahead, something flashes soundlessly. For an instant every trunk is perfectly white, and you see thousands of fine threads stretched from branch to branch, as if the whole forest has been stitched together.

Then the thunder comes up through the ground.

It shakes your teeth. Acorns leap in the moss. Somewhere nearby, a tree groans like a ship, and the first drop of rain strikes your hand, warm and red. You rub it away, startled, but the next drops are ordinary water, huge and cold. Within seconds the rain is falling in ropes. You run beneath the trees while the path dissolves under your feet, yet the storm seems to be herding you rather than chasing you. Wind closes every turning behind you with curtains of branches. Only the way ahead remains open.

Through the rain you see a yellow window.

A small house stands in a clearing where you are certain no house stood before. Its roof is tiled with overlapping pieces of bark, and its chimney twists upward like a black finger. Warm light spills across the flooded grass. You reach the door and knock, but your knuckles make no sound. The door swings inward anyway.

Inside is not a house but a long railway carriage. Lamps sway above rows of empty velvet seats. Rain streams across the windows, although the carriage is moving now, carrying you at impossible speed through the forest. At the far end sits a child in a bright blue coat, holding a glass jar against their chest. Lightning flickers inside it, folding and unfolding like a captive insect.

“You’re late,” the child says.

You ask where the train is going.

“To the place the storm began.”

The child lifts the jar. With each pulse of the trapped lightning, you recognize their face differently: your closest friend, your mother, yourself at seven years old. You try to move nearer, but the aisle lengthens beneath your steps. Outside, the trees run beside the train on their roots. They keep pace easily, leaning close to the windows, their knotted faces turning to watch you pass.

The carriage jolts. The jar slips from the child’s hands and shatters without touching the floor.

White light pours through the aisle. It becomes a flock of tiny luminous birds, beating against the ceiling and your shoulders. The windows burst open, and the storm enters all at once—not rain and wind, but lost things. A red mitten strikes your cheek. Pages torn from schoolbooks whirl around you. A key you searched for years ago spins past, followed by the smell of a bedroom that no longer exists. Voices call from every direction, repeating words you had forgotten anyone ever said to you.

The child seizes your wrist. “Don’t listen to all of them,” they shout. “Choose one.”

Among the voices you hear your own, very faint, singing from outside the train.

You climb through the broken window.

For a moment you are falling through rain. Then you land lightly on the forest floor, and the train rushes onward without you, shrinking into a single yellow window before vanishing between two trees. The storm circles overhead like an enormous wheel. The song leads you through bracken and over black roots until you reach the clearing where your walk began.

At its center is a vast tree split open by lightning. There is no fire inside the wound. Instead, a spiral staircase descends through its hollow trunk, and far below, beneath the earth, a blue sky is shining.

You go down.

With every step the thunder grows softer. Roots pierce the walls like sleeping arms. The staircase ends on the ceiling of an upside-down field. You step from it and do not fall. Grass rises around your ankles toward the ground above, while clouds drift beneath your feet. In the distance, rain is flowing upward from a dark lake into the storm.

Someone is standing on the lake.

It is the child again, though now they are very old. Their blue coat hangs from their shoulders in tatters. They hold out one empty hand, and you understand that the storm is not angry. It is searching for the lightning that escaped the jar, searching through every forest and every forgotten room for the bright, dangerous piece that belongs at its center.

You feel a warmth beneath your ribs.

When you look down, light is shining through your clothes. The luminous birds have gathered inside your chest, wing against wing, making your heart flicker. You are suddenly afraid that giving them back will leave you hollow. The old child seems to hear the thought.

“Only the cage will be empty,” they say.

You open your mouth, and the song comes out. The birds stream with it, rising in a brilliant spiral through the inverted sky. They cross the lake, become a single bolt of lightning, and shoot upward into the earth.

The world turns over.

You are standing once more beneath the split tree. Rain falls gently through sunlit leaves. The storm has moved beyond the hills, muttering to itself, and the forest is full of birdsong. In your palm lies a small glass key, warm from being held, though you do not remember picking it up.

Behind you, somewhere among the trees, a lock quietly opens.
