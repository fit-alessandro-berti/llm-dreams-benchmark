# The Competition

You are participating in a competition where the grand prize is your dream job. As the competition progresses, you face various challenges, and each one takes a little more of you than the last.

---

The atrium is impossible, of course — it is dream architecture, and it knows it. The ceiling vaults upward like a held breath. Twenty doors line the curved walls, each numbered, each breathing faintly out warm light. Somewhere far above, a clock is running backwards, and every so often a chime goes the wrong direction, so that the sound arrives before the strike.

There are forty-one other contestants. You recognize some of them immediately — people from the margins of your life, from the version of you that existed at eleven, from the hallway of the apartment where you grew up. None of them speak. All of them are waiting for you to go first, which is itself the first test.

A woman made of folded paper extends a program. Your name on it is spelled correctly, which is somehow the most alarming thing yet.

"Rounds," she says, and her pages rustle like applause. "Three. Only two of you will reach the final room, and only one of you will be offered the work."

"What if I don't want it anymore?" you ask.

The paper woman smiles without a mouth. "Then you will still finish. That is the cruelty and the mercy of it. You can't stop. You can only arrive differently."

---

**Round One: The Written Test.**

The exam hall smells like a library after rain. Sixty questions on a desk made of skin. You uncap your pen and the questions begin to move.

Question four is *What is the job, honestly?* and you write three paragraphs of careful poetry about it. The paper drinks the ink. Then, underneath, in handwriting identical to your own, it writes: *That's the version you'd tell people.*

Question nine is *Why do you deserve this?* and the honest answer is *I don't, but I want it so badly it has changed the shape of my hands,* and you almost write that, except you look up and see the other contestants at their desks, all writing with the same face, the same frown, the same scratching pen.

You write something safer. You write something true but armored.

The paper does not accept it. It blurs. Somewhere in the hall a woman begins to cry, softly, the sound of someone discovering they have been applying for a job they already hold.

---

**Round Two: The Interview.**

The panel is not human. It is you.

Five versions of you sit behind a long table: the eleven-year-old who first knew what they wanted; the version who got everything they asked for and was miserable; the version who refused; the version who is, in every visible way, exactly where you are now and radiantly, unbearably happy; and one chair that is empty, with a coat on the back of it.

They ask you questions in unison, and the questions are the ones you have spent years not asking.

"Do you still want it," they say, "or do you just want to have wanted it?"

And here is the dream's cruelty: there is no answer that holds. Want it and you'll be told you've been coveting. Refuse and you'll be told you've been afraid. Say you want it and the empty chair's coat stirs, and the eleven-year-old version of you begins, silently, to fade out of the room like a photograph left in the sun.

So you say the only true thing left: "I want it. And I want to be the person who survives having it. I don't know if those are the same person, and I'm not going to find out by standing here."

The version of you behind the table with the miserable face — the one who got everything — nods slowly, and something in their expression cracks, and they murmur: "*That's the answer that took me eleven years.*"

They don't tell you which one of you it was.

---

**Round Three: The Final Room.**

You climb a staircase that goes up but feels like descent. The door at the top has no handle. It opens anyway, because you lean into it — dream logic, or grace, or both.

The room is small and ordinary and it breaks your heart. A desk. A window with a view of a street you know. A person waiting on the other side of the desk who is *you*, older, quieter, wearing the job.

"You came," they say.

"I came."

"And now you have to decide what to say, because I am going to say what you say, and if I say it wrong we will both be wrong forever."

The dream hangs. Outside, the backwards clock strikes something that sounds like a beginning.

And you understand, with a clarity that tastes like metal, that the prize was never the job. The prize was the *answer* — the sentence that explains the whole shape of your life, the one you've been drafting and crossing out since you were eleven. The job is only the envelope it comes in. And once you say it out loud, you cannot unsay it, and you will have to live inside whatever it makes of you.

The older you waits. The street outside the window waits. The coat on the empty chair in the room below, you somehow know, has begun to move.

---

**What do you say?**

**A.** Name the job precisely — the title, the hours, the town, the salary. Make it small and real so it can't be taken away. *Give me the practical thing and I'll be grateful.*

**B.** Name the *work* — not the title, but the thing itself: what you actually make, what problem you solve, who you serve. *Tell me what I do, not where I work.*

**C.** Say nothing yet. Ask the older version a question first — the one you've been afraid of. *Before I answer, tell me what it cost you.*
