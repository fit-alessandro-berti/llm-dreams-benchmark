…begin to speak, but the words come out not as sound, but as light.

Each sentence spills from your lips like glowing threads, weaving together in the air above the crowd. You watch them form a vast, shimmering tapestry—a map of every story you’ve ever wanted to tell. The audience gasps, then laughs, then cries, all at once. Some reach up to touch the floating letters; the words dissolve into warm rain on their fingertips.

You weren’t nervous anymore. You realize you’ve been here before, in this exact dream, many times. The stage is not a stage—it’s the back of a whale, drifting through a sky made of ink. The crowd are not strangers—they’re every version of yourself you’ve ever been, and every version you’ll ever become, all watching you with patient, forgiving eyes.

You forget the speech. You forget why you were afraid. The microphone has turned into a seashell, and when you bring it to your lips, it doesn’t amplify your voice—it amplifies your heartbeat, booming across the auditorium like a drum.

And then you realize: the dream isn’t about the speech. The dream is about the moment before the speech—the eternal, suspended moment where anything is possible, where the crowd still believes you have something worth saying.

You look out at the sea of faces, and the sea of faces looks back at you. The silence is golden. The silence is the speech.

And then, just as you open your mouth to finally speak, you wake up—with the feeling that you’ve already said everything that mattered, in a language you’ve never learned, to an audience that has always known you.

You smile in the dark. The dream is over. But the applause, somehow, continues—faint, distant, like rain on a roof far away.

And you realize: you are still dreaming.

But now, you’re the one holding the microphone.
