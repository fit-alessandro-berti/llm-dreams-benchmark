One of the strangers, a woman in a yellow raincoat, takes a tangerine from her pocket and begins to peel it. “Well,” she says, “what would you be doing if you were there?”

The answer comes at once. “Drying the dishes.”

Nobody laughs. A boy cups a hand to his ear. You listen too. Beneath the buses sighing at the curb and the rattle of a shop shutter, you hear the small, particular clink of a spoon falling into a sink.

You follow the sound down a narrow street, sharing pieces of tangerine as you walk. At the end is a kitchen door, its paint worn away at exactly the height where your knee wants to push it. Inside, water runs over a stack of blue plates. A striped towel hangs on the oven handle.

Behind you, the strangers start to say goodbye. But the boy has orange juice on his chin, and the woman’s coat is dripping, and there are six chairs around the table.

“Wait,” you say, holding the door with your knee. “Has anybody eaten?”
