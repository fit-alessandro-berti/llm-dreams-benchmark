All around me, the landscape is restless. The ground beneath my feet ripples from soft, mossy hexagons into sheets of warm, liquid amber. Overhead, the sky folds in on itself like origami, shifting from a buzzing chartreuse to a deep, bruised indigo. Even my own hands seem slightly out of focus, their edges shimmering like heat haze. 

Yet the canvas—perched on an easel that flickers between driftwood and translucent glass—is fiercely, stubbornly still. Its stark white surface hums with a quiet gravity, pulling me closer.

There is no palette and no brush, only a strange, quiet certainty that I am supposed to fill it. I reach up and pinch a drifting ribbon of neon coral right out of the air. It feels cool and surprisingly heavy between my fingers, like wet silk. When I drag it across the linen, it lets out a low, musical sigh and stays put. For the first time since arriving here, a color holds its shape.

Emboldened, I scoop up a handful of silver triangles tumbling past my knees and press them into the upper corner, where they flatten into sharp, gleaming stars. I catch a droplet of humming cerulean from a melting arch nearby and sweep it across the bottom, watching it settle into a calm, unmoving sea. 

With every stroke, the chaotic world behind me grows dimmer, as if I am siphoning its restless energy into the frame. And the canvas itself is no longer flat. The painted ocean begins to gently lap against the wooden border; the silver stars pull back into an endless, deep velvet sky. A breeze smelling faintly of rain and cedarwood drifts out from the painting, brushing against my face.

I realize I haven't been painting a picture—I've been carving out a doorway. 

Behind me, the shifting shapes begin to dissolve into soft static, whispering like distant wind chimes. I place my palms against the edges of the frame, lift one foot over the wooden lip, and step forward into the cool, steady stillness of the canvas, letting the wet paint swallow me whole just as the world begins to wake.
