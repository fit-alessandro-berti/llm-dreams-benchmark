You wake up in a completely different era, with no modern technology around. As you explore your new surroundings, you realize you need to return a paper umbrella before the rain starts again.

The street is wet cedar and packed gravel. Temple bells stagger through the fog, never quite on the same beat. Your sandals are a half-size too small; gravel bites through the thong. Shop fronts of dark wood and paper lean in. There are no engines, no glass, no hum in the air — only charcoal smoke, river water, and a loom knocking somewhere like a second heart.

A woman selling charcoal looks up as if she has been waiting, and says your childhood nickname like it is the most ordinary word in the language. She does not ask where you came from. Nobody does. They keep answering questions you have not asked yet.

In your pocket there is still one modern thing: a house key, warm, as if it has just left someone else’s hand. Tomorrow’s date is scratched into the metal in your own handwriting.

A boy runs past, then doubles back and presses the umbrella into your palms. “It rains again at the hour of the sheep,” he says. “If you don’t return it before then, you stay.”

The air tastes like incense and wet ash. You look down. You are already holding it. This is not the first morning. It is the one where you finally have to choose whether to give the umbrella back.
