The horizon is no longer a line but a repeated wound; waves slap the hull, buckle the timber, and snatch the railings away like spoons from a sinking table.

Below deck, the crew mutters in Cantonese, Portuguese, and numbers—names for winds. You climb down at once, not to shout but to listen. A young sailor asks if you’re afraid. You almost lie. Instead you say, “Yes,” and then, “So we’ll be honest with each other. That’s the ship’s only lantern.”

You divide the labor: hands for the lines, eyes for the clouds, a voice for the ones afraid to speak. You don’t promise safety; you promise attention. The storm keeps escalating—thunder like a freight train inside the mast, rain like thrown glass—but something shifts when the crew stops staring at the weather and starts watching one another. Someone laughs. Someone else cries and then begins rigging a strap.

At the zenith, a single clear patch burns—maybe an opening, maybe a mirage—and through it you spot a faint thread of light not far ahead: a navigation light, a reflection, a rumor of land. You steer toward the thread, not the promise of it. The ship groans, the deck tilts, and the crew pulls together in a rhythm that becomes a song.

By morning, no one is unchanged. The crew stands blinking in gray sunlight, hair wild, sea still furious around them, and the last word hangs in the air: you didn’t tame the storm—you became part of its path.
