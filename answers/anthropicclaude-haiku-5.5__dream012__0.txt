...and you realize that the sea is not really pulling at the ship. The waves rise and fall in slow, deliberate patterns, almost like breathing, and the rain falls upward for a moment before it strikes the deck.

"Hold the wheel steady," you say, but your voice comes out quieter than you intended, and the wind swallows it. Still, the first mate, a broad woman with silver braids you don't remember her having, nods and grips the rail beside you. Behind her, the crew moves through the lantern light, hauling ropes that hum like plucked strings.

You step to the bow. The storm clouds part for a heartbeat, and through the gap you see a lighthouse standing on the water itself, its beam sweeping not across the waves but down into them, as though it is searching for something sunk long ago.

"Captain," says a boy from the rigging, his face pale and bright. "There's a bell ringing under the hull."

You know, the way one knows things in dreams, that the bell is the ship's own. It was lost in a harbor you have never visited, and it has been waiting for you to come back for it.

"Bring the sails about," you tell them. "Not toward the light. Toward the bell."

The crew hesitates, then obeys. The ship groans, turns, and the sea parts beneath it like a curtain. Far below, through clear green water, you glimpse rows of old lanterns burning on the seabed, each one marking a sailor who never came home. Your crew sees them too, and instead of fear, a hush falls over them, and one by one they begin to sing, low and steady, a melody that calms the swells.

The storm does not vanish. But it begins to sound like music, and you find you are no longer afraid of it. You take a breath of salt air, grip the wheel, and steer your ship straight into the heart of the tempest, toward the bell that is calling you home.
