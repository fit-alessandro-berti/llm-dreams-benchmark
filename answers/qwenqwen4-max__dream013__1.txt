…begin to notice that the puzzles know your name.

The early ones were honest: brass tiles you had to cross in prime order, a river with a wolf and a goat and a third thing you couldn't look at directly, which also had to be gotten across. You solved them with the clean pleasure of a key turning. Chalk on your fingers. Somewhere above, a light like an exam hall.

Deeper in, the questions change. A door asks you to prove you've forgiven someone. A stone basin holds water that will only go still once you stop wanting it to. You do these things badly, and they open anyway, which frightens you more than failing would.

Then you notice the walls.

They're made of solutions. Every answer you've given has been laid down behind you as corridor — your reasoning, hardened into brick, extending the maze in precisely the direction you were trying to go. You haven't been finding your way through. You've been building it, at speed, out of the only material you had.

You run. Running is also a kind of answer.

At the center is a small room, and it is your grandmother's kitchen, though you never had one like it. A table. A window with an ordinary evening outside. And a door, standing open, with nothing on it. No lock. No riddle. No brass.

You stand before it for what feels like a season, looking for the question. You check the frame for inscriptions. You say numbers aloud. You offer it things — the wolf, the goat, the third thing, everything you've solved. You wait for the click that means you've earned it.

The door is just a door. That's the puzzle. It was always the puzzle, and every corridor you built was a way of not walking through it.

You put your hand on the frame. The wood is warm, like something that has been sitting in sun.

And you wake — not frightened, exactly, but with the particular grief of a word you almost had, and chalk on fingers that aren't there, and the sense that somewhere behind you, patiently, a new corridor is being added to the maze, built from an answer you haven't given yet.
