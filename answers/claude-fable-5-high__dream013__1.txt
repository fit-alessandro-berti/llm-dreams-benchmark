You are in a maze filled with complex puzzles. Each puzzle requires intense concentration and logical thinking to solve, and as you move deeper into the maze, you begin to notice that the puzzles are changing you.

The first one was simple enough: a door with seven brass dials, each engraved with symbols that rearranged themselves when you looked away. You solved it by refusing to look away, holding all seven in your mind at once, and when the door swung open you felt something click behind your eyes, as though a room in your head had been unlocked along with it.

The corridors are made of chalkboard. Equations you don't remember writing trail along the walls at shoulder height, and you realize with a slow, dreamlike certainty that they are your own thoughts, leaking out of you and drying on the slate. When you solve a puzzle, the writing grows neater. When you hesitate, it smears.

Deeper in, the puzzles stop pretending to be locks and doors. One room contains only a chair and a mirror, and the puzzle is to figure out which one of you is sitting down. Another room is a vast library where every book is the same book, and the answer is hidden in the one copy with a misprint — you find it not by reading but by listening, walking the aisles until you hear a single volume humming slightly out of tune.

You become aware that the maze is grading you. Not with numbers, but with space: each solution opens the walls a little wider, raises the ceilings, lets in more of that pale light that comes from nowhere. Failure is never punished. The maze simply waits, patient as chalk dust, and you understand that it has been waiting a very long time.

At the center — and you know it is the center the way you know things in dreams, without evidence — there is a table, and on the table is the final puzzle. It is a small wooden box with no seams, no hinges, no lid. You turn it over in your hands for what might be hours. You press it, weigh it, hold it to your ear.

And then you understand. The box is not the puzzle. The box is the answer. The puzzle was the maze, and the maze was you: every corridor a doubt, every locked door a question you were afraid to ask yourself. The box is smooth and seamless because there is nothing left inside it to get out.

You set it down gently. The walls of the maze fold back like paper, page after page after page, and beyond them is an ordinary morning — a window, a bed, the sound of someone making coffee downstairs. As you rise toward waking, you reach for a pencil to write down what you solved, and your hand comes away covered in chalk.
