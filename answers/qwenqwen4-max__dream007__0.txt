...you decide to say the true thing instead of the winning thing.

It comes out of your mouth wrong. Not sound — an object. A small brass key, warm from being carried, lands on the table between you with a click that seems far too loud. You both look at it. Your friend keeps arguing but their voice arrives a half-second late, like a badly dubbed film, and you realize you can read the words on their lips before you hear them: *you never, you always, you never.*

The kitchen has become the kitchen of a house you lived in when you were nine. The linoleum is that same yellow. Outside the window it is snowing, but the snow falls upward, and neither of you finds this worth mentioning because the argument is still the most important thing in the room.

You try to explain what the key opens. Every time you begin, the sentence rearranges itself into a different sentence, one you didn't mean, one that sounds like an accusation. Your friend's face keeps changing — now they are twenty, now they are the age they are now, now they are someone else entirely who is nevertheless, unmistakably, them. Their hands are their hands. You would know those hands anywhere.

And then the strange mercy that dreams sometimes allow: you understand that the thing you were arguing about was never the thing. It was a coat hung over the thing. The thing underneath is small and old and embarrassing, something like *I was afraid you'd leave first,* and it has been sitting in this kitchen the whole time, patient as a dog.

You reach across the table. Your arm is too long — it goes on reaching, past the key, past the salt shaker, past a stack of letters you suddenly know you never sent. Your friend takes your hand. Their palm is cold from the upward snow.

"You could have just said that," they tell you, and they are laughing, or crying, and in the dream these are the same weather.

You want to ask what the key is for. But the room is already folding itself up the way rooms do, and the last thing you notice is that the argument is still lying there on the table, unfinished, and neither of you needs to pick it back up.

You wake with your jaw unclenched for the first time in weeks, and the sentence you never managed to say still sitting behind your teeth, waiting to be said in a room with normal snow.
