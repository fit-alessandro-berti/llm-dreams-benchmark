...written in a script that seems to be your own handwriting, except every word is a question you’ve never actually asked yourself aloud.

The first page isn’t a multiple-choice exam—it’s a photograph of a door you never walked through, and the instructions say *Explain why you didn’t open it.* You reach for your pen, but it’s made of glass, and when you try to write, your hand passes through the paper, leaving only a smudge of yesterday’s rain. The clock on the wall is melting into a puddle of warm, silver light, and the proctor—who has the face of your future self, but younger—doesn’t say anything; they just hold up a mirror instead of a grading rubric.

Then the room shifts. The exam hall is no longer a hall. The desks fold into the branches of a tree you climbed as a child, and the “final exam” is actually a conversation with every version of you that ever decided you had to choose one career, one life, one correct answer. The questions don’t ask for formulas; they ask for permission. *What would you do if failure were empty of shame? What is the taste of a life you actually want?*

You realize you don’t need to pass this exam—you need to stop taking it. You stand up, but the chair dissolves into mist, and you find yourself half-sitting, half-floating in a space between your bedroom and an open sky. The paper in your hands becomes a page of your own breathing, and you finally understand: the exam was never about your career. It was about whether you’d let the fear of the wrong answer keep you from writing the right one.

You exhale. The alarm clock in your real room rings—not with urgency, but with a kind of gentle permission. You wake up with the taste of silver light still on your tongue, and for a moment, you don’t remember the questions. You only remember the feeling of having finally answered them by deciding not to finish.

But when you turn to your desk, there’s a blank page there, and your pen is warm. The dream hasn’t ended; it’s just asking you to begin.
