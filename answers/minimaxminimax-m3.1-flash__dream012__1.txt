…and you realize, with a lurch that belongs not to the ship but to you, that the storm is not coming *at* you. It is coming *from* you. You have been making weather for weeks, and you did not notice, and now your crew stands knee-deep in it, looking up at your face for the part of you that knows what to do.

The deck pitches hard. Salt spray lashes the rail like flung sand. Fifteen souls huddle behind the breakwater of the mainmast, and each of them is an argument you have been having with yourself for years: the boy with the lantern, who is terrified of the dark and so tends the light like a wound; the bosun, whose hands shake so badly he can no longer hold a rope, only tie himself to it; the old woman in the stern who is not praying but counting, over and over, some number she has never once been able to say aloud.

You grip the wheel. It is driftwood and rope, and it turns against you, and the compass in the binnacle spins like a small panicked heart.

"Captain!" the first mate shouts, and his voice cracks on the word, which tells you everything. "Do we run for the islands?"

You look at the chart. The island is there, inked in a navigator's careful hand — and it has moved. You have watched it drift for three nights now, patient as a thought.

"Tell me something," you call out instead, over the wind. "Not a report. Not a course. Tell me what you are afraid of."

No one answers. The lightning forks, and in its white bloom you see their faces turn toward you — not for orders, exactly. For permission. They have been waiting, it seems, for someone to say the word *afraid* out loud in front of the sea.

So you do it.

"I'm afraid," you say, and the wind *drops*. Not all at once. It goes out of the storm the way a held breath goes out of a chest: uneven, enormous, almost sighing. "I have been afraid since the water took my brother, and I have stood at this wheel every day since and made myself into a wall, and a wall is no use to anyone in a storm."

The sea does not calm. It quiets. The waves come up long and slow and grey, the way grief comes up slow and grey when it finally stops arguing and starts carrying you somewhere.

"Listen to me," you say. "We are not sailing *through* this. We are the weather now. So we will do what weather does: we will move, and we will turn, and we will not insist on a heading."

And your crew — that beautiful, salt-crusted, shaking crew — begin to breathe. In, and out, and in, and with the breathing the deck steadies, and the spinning compass slows, and at last it points, trembling, at the small moving island.

The boy with the lantern laughs once, out of pure surprise, and it sounds like a bird getting its wings back.

You steer by something other than the wind.

*And far below, in a quiet room where a lamp burns and a page lies open, someone turns it — and the sea goes on holding its breath, waiting to see what you will do next.*
