You start to run.

Not because anyone told you to — because your legs decided, and your lungs agreed — but the sound of your own footsteps against wet stone is the only thing in the city that makes sense right now. Everything else is a sort of beautiful nonsense.

The street is wide and beige and warm. Bread somewhere, coal somewhere, rain falling upward from a drainpipe into a sky the color of a nickel. There are no street signs. There are, instead, small brass plaques on the corners, blank, polished smooth as coins.

Your phone has 41% battery and one notification, which reads:

**MEETING — 60 min**
**The place you have already been.**

You wait for an address to appear. The screen dims. When you tap it awake, the words have changed.

**The place you have already been.**

You look up. A woman in a coat too heavy for the weather is coming toward you, and she has a cat tucked under one arm like a loaf of bread.

"You're looking for the tenth door," she says, before you've said anything. "Everyone looks for the tenth door."

"I don't know what I'm looking for."

"Oh, but you do. That's the difficulty." She sets the cat down; it immediately climbs a lamppost. "Follow the smells first. The building you want smells like the thing it wants to remind you of."

"What is it supposed to remind me of?"

She considers this seriously. "Ask it," she says. "It's been waiting longer than you have."

And she's gone, and the cat is gone, and the lamppost is only a lamppost.

So you follow the smells. Past a doorway leaking the green, wet breath of a garden that shouldn't exist on this street. Past a bakery where the loaves are stamped with dates — 1998, 2003, 2011 — all of them past, all of them in order. Past a busker playing a song in a minor key that makes you feel homesick for a place you've never lived.

At some point the city folds. An alley becomes a courtyard with a fountain. The courtyard becomes a stairway. The stairway becomes a street identical to the first one, and there, sitting on a bench, is a version of you — older, or tired, or just more settled — who is also looking at a phone.

"You took the bakery," he says. "I took the fountain. It's fine. We all get there around the same time, eventually." He stands. "I'll tell you what I know. The map is a piece of paper you lost before you woke up. It's in your pocket."

It is. You pull out a scrap of paper, damp at one corner, covered in handwriting that is unmistakably your own — hurried, unlooped, the pen pressed too hard. It is a map. It is very good. It shows streets that don't exist and one building with a single heavy door and, beside the door, a small notation:

*Knock in the rhythm of a name you used to call someone.*

And you know — not remember, *know* — the rhythm. Three knocks, pause, two knocks, pause, three. You could do it in the dark. You could do it in your sleep.

You walk for what feels like forty minutes and no time at all. The paper changes in your hand; the map redraws itself to keep pace, and at last the heavy door is in front of you, and it is warm, and it has a small brass plaque, and the plaque is not blank.

It says your name. Not your current one.

You knock. Three, pause, two, pause, three.

The door opens inward on a room with a long table, and lamps, and a great many people sitting there — all of them slightly wrong, all of them with your hands, your posture, your particular way of holding a cup.

At the far end, in the chair that would be the head of the table, is someone who looks exactly like you did an hour ago, before any of this, when you still knew what you were doing.

"You found us," they say, and there's a kind of enormous relief in it. "Sit down. We've been waiting."

"Do you know what the meeting's about?"

"They never tell us. That's how we learn to find each other." They gesture at the table, at the rows of near-you faces, all of them patient, all of them faintly lit. "Everyone in this city arrived lost and frightened and in a hurry. And now look at us. We found the door. Every lost thing in this place is inside this room, drinking something warm, telling each other the story of how it got here."

"And then?"

"And then you go back out." The one at the head of the table smiles, and it's your smile, the one you make when you're about to say something you've decided not to. "And the city will be different. Not easier. Just *familiar*. You'll know which smells to follow."

You sit. Someone pushes a cup of something hot and bitter toward you. Across the table, the older you from the bench raises a hand in greeting, and you realize you don't remember meeting him, and yet you do, and the not-remembering is a kind of tenderness.

Outside, the rain falls upward. The bread smells like your childhood. Somewhere in the city, a cat is climbing a lamppost it has climbed a thousand times, waiting for someone to come and be told the truth.

And when you finally step back out through the heavy door — a little later, or much later, or exactly an hour after you woke up in a stranger's bed with your heart going like a bird in a box — the street is waiting, and it is the street you know, and you know the way.
