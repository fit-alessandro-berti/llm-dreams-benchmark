You find yourself in a vast, unfamiliar city, and you realize you have an important meeting in an hour but no idea where it is. You start to ask a woman selling newspapers for directions, but every paper on her stand is printed with a photograph of you asking her for directions. In one photograph you are wearing a red coat, in another you are much older, and in the last you are only a pale blur beside an empty pair of shoes.

The woman studies your face and folds her hands over the newspapers. “You’re early,” she says.

You check your watch. It has no hands, only a tiny revolving door beneath the glass. Each time it turns, someone minuscule steps through carrying a briefcase and disappears beneath your cuff.

“Early for what?” you ask, but the woman has already pulled down the shutters of her kiosk. Painted across them is a map of the city. A silver star pulses at its center, though when you lean closer you see that the streets forming the map are slowly rearranging themselves. The star slips from a square to a bridge, from the bridge to the roof of a black tower, then crawls off the edge of the shutter and onto the pavement like an insect.

You follow it.

The streets are crowded with people walking backward. They move expertly, never colliding, while you stumble against shoulders and suitcases. Every face seems almost familiar. A schoolteacher you have forgotten, a neighbor from a house where you never lived, a child with your father’s eyes. They are all speaking into telephones, repeating the same sentence in different languages: “Tell them I’m on my way.”

The silver star leads you into a subway entrance. There are no stairs, only a steep corridor carpeted in blue velvet. You slide down it, faster and faster, clutching at brass door handles set uselessly into the walls. Behind each door you hear a different sound: applause, rain, cutlery at a family dinner, someone quietly calling your name. You manage to catch one handle and wrench the door open.

For an instant you are standing in your childhood kitchen. Sunlight lies across the table. A kettle is beginning to whistle, and someone whose back is turned to you is slicing a pear. You know that if they turn around, you will miss the meeting forever. The knowledge fills you with such grief that you let go.

You drop onto a railway platform.

A train waits with its doors open. Instead of destinations, the sign above it lists times: 8:13, LAST TUESDAY, WHEN YOU WERE READY, TOO LATE. You enter the carriage marked NOW. It is empty except for a man asleep beneath an umbrella. Although the carriage is underground, rain falls steadily inside it, drumming on the seats and running in silver streams along the floor.

The train begins to move. Buildings pass outside the windows instead of tunnels. You see whole offices cut open like dollhouses, people gathered around conference tables. In every room there is one empty chair. As the train speeds up, the people turn toward you in perfect unison. Some point to the empty chair. Others raise their wrists and tap their watches.

You try to ask the sleeping man which stop you need, but when you touch his shoulder the umbrella collapses. Beneath it is a marble statue of yourself, eyes closed, rainwater pouring from its mouth. A small brass plaque on its chest reads: TEMPORARY REPLACEMENT.

The train brakes sharply. Your briefcase—though you do not remember carrying one—falls open. Hundreds of white moths spill out, each with a word written across its wings. Agenda. Apology. Evidence. Birthday. Promise. One moth lands on your finger. Its wings say THE THING YOU CAME TO SAY.

You cup it carefully in your hands and step off the train.

The station opens directly onto the roof of the black tower from the map. The city spreads below you without end, domes and chimneys and glass avenues folding over one another to the horizon. The tower’s revolving door stands alone in the center of the roof. Above it, suspended in the sky, an enormous clock counts backward from one minute.

You push through the door and enter a waiting room.

It is impossibly small. There is a fish tank, a row of green chairs, and a receptionist seated behind a desk. Fish drift through the air outside the tank while tiny clouds float within it. The receptionist asks for your name.

You tell her, but it comes out as the sound of a key turning in a lock.

She nods as if this is correct. “They’ve been expecting you for years.”

“Who are they?”

She points to the only other door. On it, in gold letters, are the words IMPORTANT MEETING. Below them, someone has taped a child’s drawing of a house. Smoke rises from its crooked chimney. Four figures stand in the garden, but one has been erased so fiercely that the paper is nearly torn through.

The clock in the sky reaches ten seconds. You hurry to the door, still holding the moth between your palms. Nine. The handle is warm and seems to be breathing. Eight. You suddenly cannot remember what the meeting is about. Seven. You cannot remember what job you have, or whether you have one. Six. From the other side comes the soft scrape of chairs and the murmur of many voices settling into silence. Five.

You open your hands to look at the moth, but the words have vanished from its wings.

Four.

It flies into your mouth.

Three.

You swallow.

Two.

You open the door.

Inside is a vast room filled with everyone you have ever been. They sit around a table that stretches farther than you can see: yourself as a child with scraped knees, yourself lying convincingly at sixteen, yourself on mornings you have forgotten, yourself in futures that will never happen. The oldest version of you sits at the head of the table beside the empty chair. No one looks angry. This is somehow worse.

The clock reaches one and stops.

“Well?” the oldest you asks gently. “Did you bring it?”

Your throat flutters. The moth is alive inside you, beating its wings against your ribs. You understand that you are supposed to speak, and that every version of you has come to hear the answer. You walk to the empty chair, but instead of sitting, you climb onto it. From there the table resembles a long road. The people around it become buildings, the water glasses become lakes, and the lights overhead become a chain of moons.

“I’m on my way,” you say.

The words emerge as moths. Thousands of them pour from your mouth, flooding the room in white wings. The younger versions of you laugh and reach up to catch them. The older ones close their eyes. The far wall dissolves, revealing the childhood kitchen, still bright with afternoon sun. The person slicing the pear finally turns around.

It is you, exactly as you are now.

For a moment you face each other across the distance. Then the kettle whistles, so loudly that the city shakes. Towers fold into suitcases. Streets coil like ribbons. The meeting table tilts, and everyone slides laughing toward the open kitchen door.

You wake with one hand held to your throat and the feeling that you have just said something essential. Outside, rain taps the window. On the bedside table your watch is ticking normally, but beside it lies a single white moth wing, bearing one small silver word: HERE.
