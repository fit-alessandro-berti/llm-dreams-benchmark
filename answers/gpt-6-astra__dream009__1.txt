The street is all mud and cart tracks. Smoke rises from thatched roofs, and somewhere beyond the houses a bell is ringing. You reach instinctively for your phone, but your pocket holds only a small brass key.

“Late again,” says a woman carrying a basket of onions.

“Late for what?”

She looks at you more carefully. Then she lowers her voice.

“For waking the king.”

You follow her gaze to a castle on the hill. Every window is shuttered except one.

“I don’t know the king,” you say.

“That’s what you said yesterday.”

Before you can ask what she means, she takes your wrist and turns your hand palm-up. A sentence has been written there in faded ink:

**Don’t let him tell you his dream.**

The bell stops.

Around you, people abandon their work. A baker leaves a loaf half-cut. A boy sets down two buckets without spilling a drop. Everyone begins walking toward the castle.

You go with them because standing still suddenly seems dangerous.

At the gate, a guard steps aside.

“We’ve kept your room ready,” he says.

He leads you up a narrow staircase to the unshuttered window. Inside is a plain bed, a washbasin, and a wooden chest with a brass lock. The key fits.

Under a folded shirt lies your phone.

Its screen lights up before you touch it.

There are hundreds of photographs of the room, taken from where you are standing. In each one, the bed is a little closer to the chest.

Behind you, the floorboards creak.

“You’re awake,” someone says with relief. “Good. I’ve had the strangest dream.”

You close your hand over the warning, trying to remember whether the voice is yours.
