written in your own handwriting, though you do not remember writing them.

The first one is simple:  
**What will you do with the life you’ve been keeping as a backup?**

You pick up the pencil. It is warm, as if someone else has just put it down. Around you, the exam hall is longer than the building should allow. Rows of desks stretch into a gray corridor. Other candidates write quietly, but their papers are blank, and every time they pause, you hear the sound of a door closing somewhere far away.

The second question says:  
**Name the version of yourself you are most afraid of becoming.**

Before you can answer, the words rearrange themselves.

**Name the version of yourself you are most afraid of never becoming.**

Your hand moves before you decide it should. You write: *The one who finally says no.*

The paper shivers.

A proctor walks down the aisle, carrying a red pen that drips slowly through the air like smoke. They do not look at your name. They look at your answer.

“That,” they say, “was the only one I hoped you would choose.”

The third question is not a question at all. It is a list of doors:

A. The career that would make your parents proud  
B. The career that would make your friends understand you  
C. The career that would make you forget how lonely you are  
D. The career that would make you disappear entirely  
E. The one you were too frightened to write down

Your pencil hovers. In the dream, you know with absolute certainty that E is the real answer, but you also know that if you choose it, the exam will not end. It will simply become the rest of your life.

You write nothing.

The proctor tilts their head. “You may still answer,” they say, “by leaving it out.”

You do not know what that means, but you understand it.

When you finally look at the top of the page, your name has changed. It is no longer your legal name. It is the name you used to call yourself when you were small and not yet certain what you were.

The clock on the wall stops moving.

The other candidates stand up, but they are not holding exams. They are holding doors. One by one, they open them and step through, and each door closes behind them with a sound like a breath.

You are the only one still seated.

The final question appears, written in large letters across the entire page:

**Are you ready to become the person who will have to live with this answer?**

Your hand begins to tremble. The pencil feels heavier now, as if it is made of the same thing as consequence.

You write: “Not yet.”

The paper accepts it.

The proctor nods, as if you have earned something no one else saw.

“Then you may continue,” they say.

The lights go out.

When the room brightens again, the exam hall is gone. You are sitting at a small desk by a window, and the afternoon sun is warm on your face. The paper in front of you is blank except for a single line at the top:

**Final Exam: Introduction to Being Yourself**

Below it, in your handwriting, are three words:

**Try again.**

You blink. The pencil is in your hand.

Outside the window, a car horn sounds, distant and ordinary, and for a moment you cannot tell whether you have finished the dream or only just begun the next one.
