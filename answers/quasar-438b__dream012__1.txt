You are the captain of a ship in the middle of a turbulent sea. Your crew looks to you for guidance as the storm worsens, and you grip the helm with white-knuckled hands, squinting through salt-crusted glass at a horizon that won't stay still.

Below, your crew waits. Their faces are pale from weeks below decks, lit by lanterns that swing wild on their hooks. The cabin walls groan.

A sailor near the rails—Marsters, maybe—shouts that the stores have slipped. A crate of hardtack has come loose, now rolling across the wet floor with every surge, smashing the raised rails flat. You order him to secure it. He tries. The floor bucks him sideways before he can brace.

Your ship is losing the thread of itself. The hull is a held breath too long in the lungs, and now the hull is forgetting how.

You press the crew to quiet. Not with a yell, but with the flatness of a voice held under command—*say nothing now, do nothing now, just hold the line and wait for the ship to tell us what it wants next.* They obey, not because they trust you, but because there is nothing else left to try.

The windward rail dips. You feel it in your soles first, the slant, and then in your chest, where the deficit of air makes itself known. You are starting to serve the ship's lean instead of fighting it.

And then the dream gives you one check:

A light, low on the horizon, steady. Not a star. Not a ship. A shore. It doesn't flash, doesn't drift. It stays.

You turn the helm east into it. The crew feels the shift—the slant slows, then stops. The hull stops groaning. The ship lifts its head, just a degree, and the sea flattens beneath it, as if the water had only ever been a held argument, waiting to be released.

You don't celebrate. You stand at the rail and watch the light grow. The crew drifts back to their posts. Nobody speaks.

The dream ends as the bow touches gravel you cannot see, but can hear—the first small chored sound, unmistakable, of hull meeting land.
