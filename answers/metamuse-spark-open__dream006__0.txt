And you begin to realize you've forgotten every word you were supposed to say.

Your mouth opens, but no sound comes out at first. The microphone in front of you is absurdly tall, or maybe you're suddenly much shorter. The expectant faces in the crowd blur together — hundreds of eyes reflecting the stage light — and your hands feel strangely empty. No notes. No podium. You pat your pockets and find only a small, smooth stone, warm to the touch.

A quiet panic rises in your chest, that familiar dream-weight in your legs, as if the stage is tilting slightly beneath you.

Then, someone in the front row smiles. And another person nods, encouragingly.

You take a breath, and when you finally speak, your voice doesn't sound like your own — it's calm, clear, and it fills the entire hall without needing the microphone at all.

"I had a whole speech prepared," you hear yourself say, and the crowd gives a soft, knowing laugh, as if they've all had this exact dream before. "But I left it on my pillow when I woke up."

The tension breaks. The stone in your hand begins to glow faintly.

"So instead, I wanted to tell you what I saw on the way here."

And suddenly you remember — you flew to this stage. Not walked. You flew over a city made of libraries and rivers of light, over streets where it was gently raining upward. You describe it, and as you speak, the auditorium ceiling dissolves and everyone can see it too — the night sky you flew through, vast and violet, full of slow-moving lanterns drifting like jellyfish.

The audience gasps, not in judgment, but in wonder. They had forgotten they could see it too.

You didn't need a speech after all. You just needed to remind them they were dreaming together.
