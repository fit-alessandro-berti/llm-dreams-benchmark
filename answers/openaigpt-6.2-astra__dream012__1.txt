“Shorten sail!” you call. “Nobody goes forward alone.”

The crew moves. So do you. There is comfort in work that needs both hands. Only when the last reef is tied do you notice the drops of water lifting from your sleeves. All around you, rain is falling upward.

“Captain,” says the youngest sailor. She points over the rail. Far below the keel, a lighthouse turns its lamp. With each sweep you see a little more of the drowned town: a clockmaker’s window, an empty square, your father standing on the steps of the house where you were born. He has come out without his coat. Even after all these years, that is what troubles you.

The wheel begins to turn toward him.

You could let it. You are tired, and you know exactly how the latch on that door would feel beneath your thumb.

“Captain?” the sailor asks again.

You take her hand and set it beside yours on the wheel. Together you hold the course. Below you, your father raises one arm. This time you understand that he is not asking you to come home. He is waving you on.

By dawn the rain is falling the ordinary way, cold against your cheeks. The youngest sailor is still at the helm. You start to tell her how to meet the next swell, then feel the deck rise gently beneath your boots.

She already knows.
