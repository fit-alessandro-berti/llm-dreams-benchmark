are standing at the helm, hands firm on the wheel, staring into the gray wall of storm rolling toward you. The spray lashes the cockpit glass. Below deck, your crew waits—silent, afraid, watching your silhouette for some sign, any sign, that you know what to do.

You feel the hull groan beneath your boots. The timber protests. A rigging chain snaps somewhere in the rigging, and you hear it go, and you hold the wheel tighter because holding things is what you know, when nothing else can be known.

"Bring her about," you say, low. Not a command, almost a thought said aloud. The watch responds as if it might help. They begin to move.

Salt blows horizontal and the bow begins a slow, painful turn. You feel the wind break against the hull's new angle and know, without numbers, that the keel will hold for another hour at least. You don't say this. You say, "Steady." They steady.

A deckhand looks at you, salt-whitened, and you see he is scared not of the sea but of you, of what you might ask him to die. You put a hand on his shoulder and tell him nothing. He stays.

The bow points ten degrees off the wind. You hold.
