You are at a large social gathering where you only know one person. As you try to find them, you encounter various groups of people and each group seems to have been waiting for you specifically.

The first group is standing by a table of glasses that nobody is drinking from. A woman with a very long neck turns as you pass and says, "There you are. We were just talking about your house." You have never met her. You want to say so, but the sentence comes out as "Yes, the house," and the whole group nods with the satisfaction of people who have finished a long argument. One of them, a man in a jacket the color of wet sand, hands you a glass. The glass is empty, but it is heavy, as if the emptiness weighs something. You carry it with you. You don't know how to put it down.

The room is bigger than it should be. You remember arriving through a front door, an ordinary door with a brass knocker, and yet the ceiling is now high enough that the chandeliers hang like distant weather. People move in slow currents. You keep seeing the back of a head that could be your friend's head, the same dark hair, the same way of tilting slightly to the left when listening, and each time you get close the person turns and it's someone else, someone who smiles at you as if you've made a small charming mistake.

The second group is younger and sitting on the floor in a circle, though there is nothing in the middle of the circle. They are all wearing the same shoes. "Do you know the rule?" one of them asks you. You say you don't. "That's good," she says. "You're the only one who doesn't." They make room for you and you sit down. For a while nothing happens, and then you realize the nothing is the game, and you're losing at it, and you don't know how. You stand up. Your knees are older than they were. The glass is still in your hand.

Somewhere a piano is being played, but every time you locate the direction of the sound it moves, the way a cricket moves in a dark room. You think: my friend is near the piano. You think this with total certainty, and the certainty has a taste, faintly metallic, like a coin held in the mouth as a child.

You go down a hallway lined with coats. Far more coats than people. They hang two and three deep, sleeves brushing you as you pass, and some of them are warm, as if only just taken off. Halfway down the hallway you find a coat that is yours. You didn't wear a coat tonight. You check the pockets and find a folded note in your own handwriting that says: You already found them. You read it three times. It doesn't become clearer. You put it back and you feel, obscurely, that you've been rude to yourself.

The third group is in a kitchen that is also somehow the outside. There's a stove and there's grass, a refrigerator with a wind blowing past it. These people are older and speak quietly, and they don't turn toward you at all, which is a relief so big you nearly cry. You stand among them and listen. They are talking about someone who left a party years ago and was never seen again, and everyone at the party still remembers exactly what they were wearing. "Blue," one says. "No, green," says another. "It was the kind of blue that people call green." You want to ask who they mean. You know who they mean. You become aware that you are the one person here that only one person knows, and that this is a kind of thinness, that you could slip between two of these coats and not be missed.

You go looking harder now. You move through the party with your empty glass held out in front of you like a lantern. The party is now several floors. There are staircases you take that deposit you on the same floor you left. In one room a film is being projected onto a wall, and the film is of this party, and in the film you can see yourself going down a hallway lined with coats, and in the film there is someone behind you, following at a distance, with dark hair, tilting slightly to the left. In the film you never turn around.

You turn around.

The room behind you is empty except for the long-necked woman, who says, kindly, "They're upstairs. They've been upstairs the whole time." You ask her how she knows. She says, "You told us. At the beginning. Don't you remember? You came in and you said, I only know one person here, and I'm going to find them, and you handed me your coat."

You go upstairs. The stairs are quiet. The piano is louder here and then, on the landing, it stops mid-phrase, and in the silence you hear your name spoken once from a room at the end of the hall, in a voice you have known for a very long time, saying it the way it is only said by someone glad to see you.

The door is open. The room is small, a bedroom with the party's coats piled on the bed, and your friend is sitting on the edge of the bed with their own empty glass, waiting, unsurprised, as though you've only been gone a minute. "I couldn't find you," you say. They look at the glass in your hand and then at the glass in theirs and they laugh, and you realize you've been holding half of the same thing all night.

"I know," they say. "I was looking for you too. Everyone downstairs kept telling me where you'd been."

You sit down beside them on the coats. Below you the party goes on, all those people who somehow know you, murmuring, and it doesn't matter anymore. You set your glass on the floor. It makes no sound. The window is open a little and the wind from the kitchen comes in smelling like grass, and you both sit there not talking, which is the whole reason you came, and the piano starts again, very far away, finishing its phrase.
