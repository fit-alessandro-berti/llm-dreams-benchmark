"Good—" The microphone swallows the word whole. The audience leans forward. Somewhere in the back rows, someone is eating popcorn.

You try again. "Good evening." This time the words come out, but the voice isn't yours — it belongs to a teacher you had in third grade, or maybe your mother. No one seems to notice. They're all just waiting.

You look down at your notes. The page is blank. No — it's covered in your own handwriting, but you can't read a word of it. The letters slide off the page like drops of water, and you catch one as it falls. It turns into a small silver fish in your palm, gasping.

The audience is patient. Too patient. And now that you really look, every face is familiar — the cashier from the grocery store, your childhood dentist, a friend you keep meaning to call. They aren't waiting for a speech at all. You realize, with the sudden certainty that only dreams allow, that every one of them is holding a small candle, and the candles were lit before you ever arrived.

The floor tilts, gently, like the deck of a ship. The rows of seats ripple. Someone in the front row rises to applaud, and the sound of it is rain on a tin roof—

And you wake up. Except you don't. You're still on the stage, only now it's your old school cafeteria, and the clock on the wall says it's almost time for something important, though you can't remember what.

You take a breath.

You begin again.
