You glance down at your notes. The pages are blank. All of them. But when you look up, no one seems to mind. They're just waiting, patient as the tide. The spotlight hums above you like a refrigerator, and the light is warm — warmer than any spotlight should be, like afternoon sun through the window of your childhood home.

In the front row sits your fourth-grade teacher, though you haven't thought of her in twenty years. She's mouthing the words along with you, as if she wrote the speech herself. You look down and realize you have no shoes on, and this feels enormously important, the most important fact in the universe, though you couldn't say why.

The words keep arriving now, surfacing one by one like fish rising to feed, and each sentence feels truer than anything you've ever said while awake. The audience begins to rise — or is it the stage that's rising? Faces blur into a single bright mass, and you feel yourself growing weightless, opening your mouth for the final line—

—and you wake.

Dark room. Quiet. Your heart pounding. And for one strange, lingering moment, you can still feel them: hundreds of eyes, warm and patient, waiting to hear what you'll say next.
