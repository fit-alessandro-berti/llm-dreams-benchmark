Above you, the twilight sky is entirely uninterrupted by power lines or thedistant hum of traffic. Instead, the silence between footsteps is vast and deep, broken only by the creak of wooden tavern signs swinging in the wind and the murmur of voices speaking in a rhythmic, archaic dialect.

As you hurry down a narrow, winding alley, the architecture seems to lean inward. Merchants in velvet caps are packing away bolts of dyed silk and crates of speckled apples, lighting tallow candles that cast long, dancing shadows against plaster walls. You try to ask a passing lamplighter for directions, waving your long pole toward the center of town, but when you open your mouth, no sound comes out—only a tiny puff of silver mist. 

Unbothered, the lamplighter simply smiles, taps his temple, and points his glowing wick toward a crooked spire rising above the rooftops at the edge of the market square. 

Your feet feel suddenly weightless, carrying you effortlessly over slick stones in long, gliding strides. You reach a heavy oak door carved with phases of the moon. It swings open before your knuckles even brush the wood. Inside, there are no digital screens or glowing bulbs—only hundreds of pendulums swinging wildly out of sync, hourglasses pouring sand upward toward the ceiling, and a massive, unfinished astrolabe hovering an inch off the floorboards. 

At the center of the room stands an old woman wearing spectacles with four lenses layered over each eye. She doesn't ask where you came from; she simply holds out a gloved hand. 

You pull the pulsing brass gear from your pocket. The moment you drop it into her palm, every pendulum in the room freezes at once. A single, impossibly deep bell chimes—so loud and resonant that the wooden walls begin to ripple like water, the candlelight stretches into blinding beams of morning sun, and the chime softens into the distant, familiar sound of birds singing outside your bedroom window.
