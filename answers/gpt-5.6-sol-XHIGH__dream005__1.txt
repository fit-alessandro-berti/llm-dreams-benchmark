You ask a woman in a silver dress whether she has seen your friend. She says, “Of course,” with such confidence that relief floods through you. Then she points toward three different doors at once. Her arms multiply delicately from her shoulders, one hand indicating the kitchen, one the garden, and one a narrow blue door you are certain was not there a moment ago.

“Which one?” you ask.

She looks surprised. “They all lead to the same place.”

You choose the kitchen. Inside, dozens of people are preparing a meal with the solemn concentration of surgeons. A man slices a loaf of bread, and each slice contains a tiny furnished room. In one, a family is eating dinner; in another, it is raining onto an unmade bed. Nobody seems to find this unusual. You search the faces for the one familiar face, but whenever you think you recognize it, the person turns around and becomes someone else.

At the far end of the kitchen, your friend’s laugh rises above the clatter. You hurry toward it, squeezing between tables, apologizing as sleeves catch on chair backs and glasses tip without spilling. The room lengthens. The faster you walk, the farther away the laugh becomes, until it is coming from inside a dumbwaiter in the wall.

You pull open its little brass door. Instead of shelves, there is a crowded elevator. Everyone inside is wearing a coat and holding a damp umbrella. They shuffle backward to let you in.

“Going up?” the operator asks.

You say the name of your friend.

The operator nods as though this is a floor. The doors close, and the elevator begins moving sideways.

Through a glass panel you watch the party pass by in cross-section: rooms stacked beside rooms, each containing another gathering. In one, everyone is dancing in complete silence. In another, guests are seated around a table waiting for a cake covered in lit candles, though they are all very old. In a third, you see yourself standing alone beneath a paper lantern, looking into the elevator as it slides past.

You wave to yourself. The other you does not wave back. Instead, they point urgently behind you.

When you turn, the elevator is empty except for an umbrella dripping seawater onto the carpet. The doors open onto the garden.

It is night outside, though through the windows the party is still full of afternoon sun. Long tables stretch across the lawn, covered with place cards. You find one bearing your name, but every other card bears it too. Hundreds of versions of your name flutter in the breeze. At the head of the nearest table sits your friend, back turned, wearing the red jacket you remember.

You call out. The figure stands and begins walking away between hedges.

You follow into a maze. Music from the party grows muffled, then changes into the sound of a train moving over tracks. The hedges become taller and lean inward. Between their leaves you glimpse moments that do not belong in a garden: your childhood bedroom, an airport baggage carousel, a beach under a green sky. Once, you see your friend on the other side of the hedge, keeping pace with you. You push your hands through the branches, but they close around your wrists like fingers.

“Wait,” you say.

“I have been waiting,” your friend answers, though the voice comes from somewhere ahead.

The maze opens at last onto a small railway platform. All the guests are there now, lined up along the edge, still holding their drinks. A train waits with its windows glowing. Above it, the station clock has no hands. Your friend stands in an open carriage doorway.

You try to reach them, but the guests begin asking you questions. How have you been? Are you still living there? Did you bring the thing you promised? Each question makes your pockets heavier. You put your hands into them and find keys, invitations, smooth stones, a child’s mitten, and a folded map drawn in your own handwriting. None of it is the thing they want.

The train whistle blows. You force your way through the crowd, spilling apologies. At the door, your friend extends a hand. Their face is in shadow, but you know them by the small scar across one knuckle.

Just before you take hold, you remember that you do not know whose hand this is.

The train begins to move. You run beside it, reaching. Your friend leans farther out, and for one moment the lantern light crosses their face. It is your face, older and exhausted, looking back at you with immense tenderness.

“You found me,” they say.

Then the platform drops away. You are running across the dark surface of a lake, the train gliding beside you without tracks. Beneath the water, the party continues upside down. Guests raise their glasses toward you from chandeliers growing out of the depths. Paper lanterns float below like moons.

You stop running. The train disappears soundlessly into the night, but the hand remains in yours.

When you look down, you are holding only the folded map. It is blank except for a small red dot that moves slowly toward the place where your heart would be. As it reaches the center, every lantern in the water goes out, and from very far away you hear someone at the party calling your name as if they have only just arrived.
