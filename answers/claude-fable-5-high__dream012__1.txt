You are the captain of a ship in the middle of a turbulent sea. Your crew looks to you for guidance as the storm worsens, and you open your mouth to give the order — but what comes out is not your voice. It is the sound of rain on a tin roof, a sound you have not heard since childhood, and the crew nods as though they understand it perfectly.

"Hard to port," the rain says through you, and the ship turns.

The waves are enormous now, but you notice they are rising and falling in slow motion, each one holding its crest a moment too long, like a held breath. When you look closely at the water, you see it is full of pages — thousands of handwritten pages, dissolving and reforming, and you understand without being told that the sea is made of every letter that was never sent.

Your first mate approaches. She has your mother's face, though you are certain she didn't a moment ago. "Captain," she says, "the compass is asking for you."

Below deck, the compass sits in the navigation room, grown to the size of a dinner table. Its needle does not point north. It points at you, and follows you as you circle the room, the way the eyes of certain portraits do. Etched around its rim, where the degrees should be, are the names of everyone you have ever forgotten.

"Where do we sail?" the compass asks, in the voice of the rain.

"Away from the storm," you say.

"There is no away," it says, gently, the way one corrects a child. "The storm is the part of the sea that remembers being sky. It is only trying to go home."

You climb back up. The crew has lashed themselves to the masts, but they are singing — a song you wrote when you were nine years old and never showed anyone. All of them know the words. The waves have grown taller than the ship, arching overhead until the sea closes above you like hands in prayer, and inside that green cathedral everything goes quiet.

The ship is sailing underwater now, or the water has become air; it is impossible to say which. Fish drift past the rigging carrying small lit lanterns in their mouths. Far below, on the sea floor, you see other ships — hundreds of them, whole and upright, their sails full of current instead of wind, their captains standing calm at their wheels. They wave to you, unhurried, the way neighbors wave.

Your first mate stands beside you. "We were never in danger," she says. "You knew that. You were only afraid of giving the order."

"Which order?"

"To stop steering."

You take your hands off the wheel. The ship does not falter. It glides, certain as a thing returning, down toward the lantern light, and the storm far above softens into a sound like rain on a tin roof, and you realize you are small again, lying in your bed, listening to it, and the ship was the bed, and the crew was every person you would someday have to guide, and the sea —

the sea is still deciding what it will be when you wake.
