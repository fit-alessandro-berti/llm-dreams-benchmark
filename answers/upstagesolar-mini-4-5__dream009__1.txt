You stand and look around. You're in a courtyard of a stone house, roof tiles sagging in the middle. Across from you, an old woman is stirring a pot over a fire that smolders more than it burns. She doesn't startle at your arrival. She just keeps stirring.

You need to—you need to figure out when and where you are, and whether there's any way home. But before you can ask, she sets down the pot and says, in a language that's somehow familiar and entirely unfamiliar at once, "You're late. The river floods at dusk, and you've walked a long way to arrive in the rain."

Behind her, you notice the door to the house is open, and inside, on a rough wooden table, there's a map—not paper, but something woven, its lines frayed and dark with age. It marks places you know and places you don't, and in the corner, a symbol you've never seen: a spiral with three dots inside it.

You need to—you need to ask her about it. But first, she points to a bowl of water and a rag. "You'll want to wash the road off your face before you speak to anyone. Even strangers in strange centuries notice dirt."

You need to—you need to start somewhere. Maybe with the water. Maybe with her question. Maybe with the spiral.
