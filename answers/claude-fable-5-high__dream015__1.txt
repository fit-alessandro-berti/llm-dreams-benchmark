You find yourself in a post-apocalyptic world, with resources scarce and dangers everywhere. Despite the hardships, you decide to plant a garden.

It seems absurd, even in the dream it seems absurd, but your hands are already moving, clearing rubble from a square of earth behind what used to be a library. The soil underneath is dark and soft, impossibly soft, as if it had been waiting under the concrete the whole time. You dig with a bent street sign, and the digging makes no sound at all.

The seeds come from your pockets. You don't remember putting them there, but there they are, an endless supply — some are ordinary, apple and squash and sunflower, and some are small glass beads with tiny lights inside them, and you plant those too, because in the dream you understand that light is something that can grow.

A woman appears at the edge of the garden. She has been there for a while, you realize, maybe always. Her coat is stitched from old maps, and when the wind moves it you can see roads shifting across her shoulders, highways rearranging themselves, cities trading places. She doesn't speak. She kneels down beside you and begins to plant as well, and where her fingers touch the ground, green comes up immediately — not sprouts but full stalks, unfurling in seconds like time-lapse film, and this feels correct, because she has clearly been doing this longer than you.

"The trick," she finally says, "is that it was never about the food."

You want to ask what it was about, but the sun is setting in the wrong direction, rising and setting at once from two ends of the sky, and the light crossing over the garden makes everything translucent. You can see through the sunflower stems to their slow green pulse. You can see through your own hands to something bright and unhurried moving inside them.

Others come. That's how it works — first one, then three, then a dozen, people stepping out from behind broken walls where there was no room for anyone to hide. A boy carries water in a car hubcap. An old man brings a chair with three legs and sits on it anyway, perfectly balanced, and tells stories about the world before, except the stories keep changing, and nobody minds, because in each version the ending is different and in each version somebody survives.

The garden grows faster than gardens grow. By what should be nightfall it is a field, and the glass-bead seeds have come up as slender trees with lanterns for fruit, glowing amber and warm, and the whole ruined city is lit at its center like a body with a heartbeat again.

You climb the library steps to look at it all. From up there you can see the darkness beyond the light — the real dark, the dangerous dark, the shapes that move in it. They are still there. The dream does not pretend they aren't. But they stay at the edge of the lantern-light, circling, and one of them, wolf-like and thin, steps just close enough for you to see its eyes, and its eyes are not hungry. They are only tired. You leave a squash on the bottom step, and in the morning — because suddenly it is morning — the squash is gone, and in its place is a single seed you don't recognize, black and heavy as iron.

You plant it, of course. You know better, and you plant it anyway, because that is what you have decided to be now: the kind of person who plants things.

What comes up is a door.

It stands alone in the middle of the field, frame and hinges and all, wrapped in vines that bloom as you watch. The woman in the map-coat nods at you from across the garden, and the roads on her shoulders all straighten at once, every highway pointing the same direction, toward the door.

You open it. On the other side there is grass, and the smell of rain, and a world that was never ruined at all — or this world, healed, it's impossible to tell — and just before you step through, you look back at the garden, at the lantern trees, at the people eating together in the light they made, and you understand that you can carry all of it with you, that it fits in your pockets, that it was always going to fit.

You step through, and the door closes softly behind you, and the sound it makes is the sound of someone you love saying your name, and you wake with dirt under your fingernails and the certainty, fading but real, that somewhere something you planted is still growing.
