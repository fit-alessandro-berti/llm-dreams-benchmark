speak—but instead of words, a small blue bird flies from your mouth.

It circles the microphone, lands on your shoulder, and waits.

The audience leans forward.

You try again. Another bird emerges, this one carrying a thread of gold. Then another, and another, until the air above the stage is stitched with bright, impossible patterns.

You search the front row for someone who can explain what is happening. Your childhood teacher sits beside a woman made of rain. At the end of the row, your younger self swings their feet above the floor.

“What was I supposed to say?” you whisper.

Your younger self shrugs. “We came to see what you’d do.”

You look down at your notes. The pages have become windows. Through one, you see the kitchen of a house you no longer remember. Someone has left a cup on the table for you.

The blue bird nudges your cheek.

So you set the pages aside and hold out your hands.

The audience does the same.

Overhead, the golden threads gather into a door. It opens just wide enough for the smell of warm bread to drift through, and suddenly you remember the first line of your speech.

But now everyone is standing on their chairs, trying to reach the handle.
