You are given a mysterious locked box with no key in sight. As you examine it closely, you notice intricate patterns and tiny animals worked into the dark wood: foxes chasing their own tails, moths carrying lanterns, fish swimming upward through the grain. The longer you look, the more certain you become that they are moving. Not quickly, and never while you watch one directly, but each time your gaze shifts, the creatures have crept a little farther around the box.

The person who gave it to you has already vanished. You are standing alone in a railway station whose roof is so high that clouds have formed beneath it. Trains wait at every platform, their windows glowing, though there are no tracks. Instead, long strips of black water run away into the fog. A voice from the loudspeaker announces departures in a language you almost remember from childhood.

The box is warm in your hands. When you hold it to your ear, you hear a room on the other side: a clock ticking, curtains breathing in an open window, someone turning the pages of a book. Then three soft knocks come from inside.

You knock back.

At once, every clock in the station strikes a different hour. The people you had not noticed before rise from the benches. They all wear your face, though at different ages. One is a child with scraped knees. One is very old and carries a bouquet of dry weeds. One looks exactly as you did yesterday. None of them seems surprised to see you. They point, together, toward the farthest platform.

There a white train is arriving over the water without making a ripple. Its doors open before it stops. You climb aboard because the box has begun to pull you toward it, gently but insistently, like a compass needle finding north.

Inside, the carriage is furnished as a narrow house. A kitchen passes into a bedroom, the bedroom into a garden, and the garden into a corridor lined with doors. Through one door you glimpse a birthday party at which everyone sits perfectly still around an unlit cake. Through another, snow falls upward into a summer sky. Behind a third, someone is singing the tune your mother used to hum, but all the notes are in the wrong order.

At the end of the corridor stands a little table with a key upon it.

The key is made of clear glass. You reach for it, and your hand passes through. You try again, more slowly. This time the key closes around your fingers rather than your fingers around the key. It is cold and pulses like a small heart.

The box wriggles beneath your arm.

When you bring the key near the lock, the carved animals flee to the edges of the lid. The moths extinguish their lanterns. The fish hide beneath the hinges. Only one tiny fox remains, standing beside the keyhole and looking up at you with an expression of warning.

“If I do not open it,” you say, though you are not sure who you are speaking to, “I will always wonder.”

The fox bows its head.

The key turns without touching the lock. The lid opens, and inside is the station, impossibly small and perfectly detailed. You see the clouded roof, the water tracks, the waiting crowds. You see the white train moving through its toy landscape. Peering closer, you see yourself standing in the train and holding a box. Inside that smaller box is another station, another train, another you.

You lean too far. The tiny station rushes upward, widening around you, and suddenly you are falling through one box after another. In every one, a different version of you opens the lid. In one you are wearing a crown of feathers. In another you have no shadow. In another you are the child with scraped knees, laughing as you fall past yourself. Each reaches out, but their hands slide through yours like reflections on water.

At last you land softly in the room you heard inside the box.

The curtains breathe at the open window. The clock ticks backward. A book lies open on the floor, and its pages turn by themselves, blank at first, then filling with handwriting as you watch. It is your handwriting. The words describe the station, the train, the key, and your arrival in this room. The final line says: Do not look beneath the bed.

You kneel immediately.

Beneath the bed is the night sky. It stretches away in every direction, dense with stars, and suspended among them hangs a brass key much larger than the moon. Far below—or above—you can see the locked box drifting alone. You understand then that the room is inside the box, the box is inside the station, and the station is underneath the bed. The arrangement is absurd, but in the dream it feels less like a puzzle than a memory you have finally recovered.

Three knocks sound at the bedroom door.

“Who is it?” you ask.

Your own voice answers, muffled by the wood. “I brought you the box.”

You open the door. No one is there, only the oldest version of you walking away down the corridor, dropping dry weeds one by one. Wherever a weed touches the floor, it takes root and blossoms into a blue flower. You follow the trail until the corridor opens onto the station again.

All the trains are leaving now. Your other selves wave from the glowing windows as they glide away across the black water. The child presses both palms to the glass. Yesterday's you looks down, as if ashamed. The old one stands at the final door and gives you the bouquet, now fresh and blue.

“What was in it?” you ask.

The old one smiles. “You were.”

Then the station begins to fold. The platforms close like the covers of a book, the clouded roof creases down the middle, and the water rises in shining walls. You clutch the flowers and feel yourself becoming smaller, small enough to fit inside a carved wooden box, small enough to curl between a fox and a sleeping moth.

Just before the lid closes, you throw the glass key through the narrowing gap.

It falls onto the floor of another room, where someone you cannot see picks it up. For one bright instant you look through their eyes and see the box resting in their hands. You try to warn them. You try to say that there is no need to open it, that every locked thing is also a door looking for the person it already contains.

But all that comes out are three soft knocks.
