Around you, hundreds of other students turn their pages at once. The sound is enormous, like a flock of birds lifting from a field. You look up, but the examination hall has no ceiling. Rows of desks rise in terraces toward a white sky, and at every desk sits someone you recognize: your childhood dentist, the woman who once returned your lost wallet, a teacher whose name you have forgotten, your mother as a young girl. They are all writing quickly.

The clock above the blackboard has thirteen hands. None of them move, yet you can hear it ticking inside your chest.

You choose “tomorrow.” The instant your pencil fills the circle, a door appears beside your desk. Through its little window you see yourself in a laboratory coat, accepting an award while cameras flash. You feel a surge of relief—until that future version of you turns toward the window. Their smile vanishes. They press one palm against the glass and silently mouth, Don’t choose this.

You try to erase the answer, but the eraser leaves a hole in the paper. Through the hole you can see another room far below, where you are standing behind a bakery counter dusted with flour, laughing with people you do not know. The smell of warm bread rises through the desk. For a moment you want to crawl through, but the hole closes when the examiner walks past.

The examiner is very tall and wears a graduation gown that trails the length of the aisle. Where a face should be, there is a small round mirror. As they lean over you, you see yourself reflected at several ages at once: a frightened child, your present face, an old person with kind, exhausted eyes.

“No looking at yourself,” the examiner says, and draws a red line across your page.

The next question reads, “Show all work.” Beneath it is your entire life written as an equation. Birth is an opening parenthesis. Every friendship is a fraction. Several years have been crossed out. At the end, after an equals sign, there is only an empty box.

You begin calculating, but your pencil has become a thermometer. The mercury climbs whenever you remember something embarrassing and falls whenever you think of home. All around you, students are beginning to disappear. Each time someone finishes, their desk folds neatly shut around them and sinks through the floor. The others do not react. They write faster.

You turn the page. The third question is a map of a city you have never visited, except every street bears the name of a decision you postponed. Regret Avenue curves back into itself. Practical Lane ends at a cliff. A tiny gold star marks “You are here,” but it keeps moving whenever you look away.

“Five minutes remaining,” announces a voice from the sky.

Panic rushes through you. There are still hundreds of pages. Some are made of tracing paper, some of leaves, one of thin ice. The questions grow less comprehensible: “Defend the color blue.” “Translate your earliest fear into architecture.” “If success is traveling north at sixty kilometers per hour and happiness leaves the station ten years late, where will they meet?”

You know there must be correct answers. Everyone else seems to know them. Your mother, still a girl, has reached the final page. She looks across the aisle at you and quietly tears a corner from her exam booklet. She folds it into a tiny boat and sets it on the floor.

Water is running beneath the desks now, shallow at first, then rising over your shoes. The paper boat floats toward you. Written along its side are the words: The exam is not asking what you know.

The water climbs to your knees. Ink loosens from the pages and swims around the hall in dark ribbons. Students shout as their carefully written answers dissolve. The doors behind their desks swing open one by one, revealing offices, operating rooms, courtrooms, stages, cockpits—bright little futures filling rapidly with black water.

The examiner stands at the front, untouched by the flood. In the mirror of their face, your reflection is drowning.

“One minute remaining.”

You reach the final page. It contains a single sentence: “State, in your own words, what you are willing to lose.”

Your pencil is a pencil again. You think of the award room, the bakery, all the doors you have not opened and all the people waiting behind them. You think of the years crossed out in the equation. The water reaches your throat.

Then you write: The future I was promised.

For the first time, the clock stops ticking.

The examiner lifts both hands and removes the mirror as though it were a mask. There is no face beneath it, only a round opening filled with daylight. Wind tears through the hall. Pages whirl upward, carrying equations, maps, grades, and names into the white sky. The desks unfold into birds. Your mother’s paper boat grows large enough to stand in, and you climb aboard just as the floor drops away.

You sail through the opening in the examiner’s face and emerge onto a quiet lake at dawn. On the far shore, many roads begin, but none have signs. Your blank exam booklet lies in your lap. On its cover, where your name should be, someone has written, “Take your time.”

Behind you, a bell rings.

You wake with your hand clenched as if it is still holding a pencil. For several seconds you can hear wings beating above your bed, and there is a faint smell of warm bread in the room.
