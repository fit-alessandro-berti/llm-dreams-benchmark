The rain arrives from below. It rises out of the moss in slow warm threads and gathers overhead, and I understand, with the flat certainty dreams allow, that the storm has been looking for me for a long time and is relieved to have caught up.

I am carrying something. I have always been carrying it. When I look down it's a lantern with a candle inside that burns underwater-green, and I know I must deliver it before the thunder finishes counting. The thunder is at four. It has been at four for years.

There's a door in the hillside that wasn't there and also has never not been there — a kitchen door, chipped white paint, the latch that sticks unless you lift as you push. I lift as I push. Inside is my grandmother's house, except the house belongs to a heron now, and the heron is standing at the sink washing a teacup with tremendous care, and it says without turning around, *You're late, but the soup doesn't mind.*

There's a place set at the table. There's a bowl of something steaming that smells like the inside of a piano. I set the lantern down and the candle goes out and it doesn't matter at all — the storm has come inside and sat down across from me and taken off its coat, and it has my father's hands, and it is tired, and it wants to tell me about the drive over.

Outside, the forest exhales. The thunder reaches five and stops, satisfied.

I sit down. I listen. The rain goes back into the ground where it lives.
