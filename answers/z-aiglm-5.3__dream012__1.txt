...and you open your mouth to give the order, but no sound comes out. Or rather, a sound comes — just not the one you meant. It's your mother's voice, calling you in to dinner. The crew doesn't seem to notice. They keep staring at you, salt running down their faces, and you realize with a jolt that you know every one of them. The tall one at the bow taught you to ride a bike. The woman clinging to the rigging was your third-grade teacher. Every face you've ever loved is on this ship, and every one of them is afraid.

So you do the only thing that makes sense. You take the wheel.

It's warm — warm the way a hand is warm, as if someone just let go of it. The moment you grip it, the deck steadies, just a little. Lightning splits the sky, and in the white flash you see the wave coming. It's enormous. Taller than the sky. At the crest of it, impossibly, a gull rides the curl, calm as anything, and you understand — the way you understand things in dreams, completely and without evidence — that the wave isn't coming *at* you.

It's coming *for* you. There's a difference.

The storm isn't trying to sink the ship. It's trying to tell you something.

You steer straight into it.

The instant the bow meets the wall of water, everything goes still. Not calm — *still*. The wave has frozen mid-crash around the hull, holding the ship like a hand, and in the green-glass light of the suspended water you can see everything: fish hanging like ornaments, the street you grew up on lying somewhere in the deep, a bicycle wheel turning slowly, slowly, far below. Your crew is gone, but you're not alone. You can hear them — every voice you've ever known, humming one low note. The note a hull makes. The note the sea makes. The note you've been hearing your whole life without knowing it.

And then you understand the last thing, the thing the storm came all this way to say:

You were never the captain.

You're the ship.

The storm isn't happening to you. It's happening *through* you — every wave a breath, every thunderclap a heartbeat — and when you finally let go of the wheel, nothing changes, because the wheel was never what was holding you steady.

You were.

You wake with salt on your lips and the sheets bunched around you like water, and for one long moment you can still hear it — that low hum, fading, like a ship passing far offshore in the dark.
