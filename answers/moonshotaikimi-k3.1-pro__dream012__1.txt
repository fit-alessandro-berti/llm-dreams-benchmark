and you notice the rain is falling upward.

It rises from the deck in silver threads, past the rigging, into the black belly of the clouds. No one else seems surprised until you point. Then the entire crew falls silent.

The compass spins wildly, stops, and points straight down.

“Hard to port,” you say.

The helmsman stares at the mountainous wave ahead. “Captain?”

“Toward it.”

One by one, the crew take their stations. The ship turns. The wave bends over you like a closing hand—and breaks without a sound.

For a moment, there is no sea.

Then the ship slips through the surface and emerges beneath it, sailing across a vast, star-filled sky. The storm glimmers far above, small and harmless as a sheet of crumpled foil. Below the keel, constellations drift like luminous fish.

The crew begin to laugh. You laugh too, though you cannot remember giving the order to breathe underwater.

Ahead, an upside-down lighthouse floats among the stars. Its keeper leans from the door as you approach. He has your face, only much older.

“You brought them far enough,” he says.

Behind you, the crew fade one by one, not with fear, but with the relieved expressions of people waking from a long journey.

“Where have they gone?”

“Home.”

“And the ship?”

“Ships are only for storms.”

The deck softens beneath your boots. The masts become bare trees; the sails fold themselves into white birds and fly away. You try to hold the wheel, but it is already smooth driftwood in your hands.

The keeper offers you the compass. Its needle now points toward a tiny golden door at the base of the lighthouse.

“Must I go through?” you ask.

He smiles. “Only when you hear the bell.”

Far away, a bell rings once.

You wake before dawn. For a moment, your bedroom seems to tilt beneath you. Your palm aches as though it has been gripping a wheel, and when you open it, there is a faint circle of salt across the skin.

Outside, rain taps the window.

You watch it for a long time, just to make certain it is falling down.
