You are about to take the final exam for a course that determines your future career. As you sit down to start the exam, you notice the questions are written in your own handwriting.

Not a handwriting like yours. Yours. The little hook you put on the lowercase g, the way your capital T leans as if it is about to fall over. Question one asks: "Describe the room you are sitting in now, but do not use the word 'room.'" You look up. The hall is enormous, longer than it is wide, and the desks go on in rows until they blur into a kind of fog near the far wall. There is no clock. There is a proctor at the front, but the proctor is facing away from everyone, writing on a blackboard that already has too much on it.

You pick up the pencil. It is warm, as though someone has just put it down. You write: "A hall of desks. Fluorescent light that hums in a key I cannot name. Somebody two rows ahead is crying quietly and pretending it is a cough." As soon as you finish the sentence, the question on the page changes. Question one is now crossed out, neatly, in your handwriting, and beneath it says: "Good. Now say what you left out."

You left out that the person crying is your brother, who is not in this course, who never took this course, who is eleven years old in this hall even though he is thirty-four in every other place. You write that. The page accepts it.

Question two: "Which career is being determined? Show your work."

You realize you do not know. You knew when you walked in. You knew when you registered, when you paid, when you stayed up for years memorizing whatever the course was about. You turn the exam booklet over to look for the course code and the back cover is a mirror, a cheap plastic one, the kind glued into a child's pencil case, and it shows your face but the face is a few years older, and it is listening to you.

You write: "The career of the person who answers these questions." You feel this is a cheat. The page does not seem to mind. A small mark appears in the margin, a check, but the check is trembling, as if the hand that made it was not sure.

Around you people are finishing. You can tell because they stand up, walk to the front, and hand the proctor their booklets, and the proctor takes each one without turning around, with a hand that reaches backward over the shoulder, and then the student walks through the blackboard and is gone. Nobody seems alarmed by this. The blackboard takes them like water takes a stone.

Question three is blank. You wait. Nothing appears. You understand, slowly, that you are supposed to write the question, not the answer. This is worse. You sit for a long time. The hum of the lights changes key. Your brother has stopped crying and is now folding his exam into a boat.

You write: "What did I want before I wanted this?"

And under it, immediately, in the same hand, an answer arrives that you did not write, though the letters are yours: "You wanted to be outside. You wanted a window. You built a whole life so that one day you could earn the window, and you forgot the window was the point."

You look for a window. There are none. There are the shapes of windows, tall rectangles of a slightly lighter paint, where windows had been considered and then decided against.

The exam is now many pages thick. It was four pages when you sat down. You flip through and every page is full, front and back, in your handwriting, and it is all true, and you have not written any of it. There are grocery lists. There is an apology to someone whose name you have not thought about in twelve years. There is a very precise drawing of the inside of your left knee. There is a page that just says "no" four hundred times, and at the bottom, small, "yes."

The proctor turns around.

The proctor is you, as well, but that is not what makes you sit back in your chair. What makes you sit back is that the proctor is bored. Not cruel, not judging. Bored, the way a lifeguard is bored on a day with no waves. The proctor says, without raising their voice, and the voice carries the whole length of the hall: "Time."

You have not finished. You have not started, in some sense. You stand up with the booklet, which is now heavy, book-heavy, and walk toward the front, and the walk is long, and the rows of desks on either side are empty except for your brother, who holds up his paper boat as you pass so that you can see he has written your name on the sail.

At the front you hold out the booklet. The proctor does not take it. The proctor says: "You keep that one."

"Then what do I hand in?"

The proctor points at the pencil.

You look at it. It has been getting shorter. It is now barely the length of your thumb, and you understand that it was never the exam that was being graded. You put the pencil into the proctor's hand, and it is warm, and the proctor closes their fist around it as though it were something that could get away.

"Did I pass?"

"It's not that kind of course," says the proctor, and turns back to the blackboard, and you see now that the blackboard is not full of formulas or notes. It is full of names, in many handwritings, and one of them is yours, and beside it there is no grade, only a small drawing of a window, and through the window, in chalk, somebody has drawn weather.

You step into it. The chalk is cold and smells like rain. Behind you, very far away, you hear a paper boat being set down on a floor, and a small boy saying, to no one, "He'll be back for this."
