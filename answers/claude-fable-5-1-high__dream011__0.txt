You are about to take the final exam for a course that determines your future career. As you sit down to start the exam, you notice the questions are written in your own handwriting.

Not a handwriting like yours. Yours. The little hook you put on the lowercase g, the way your capital T leans as if it is being pushed from the left, the ink smudge you always leave at the end of a line because you never learned to lift the pen in time. Question one is in blue ballpoint and the paper around it is slightly dented, the way paper gets when you press too hard because you are nervous.

Question 1. What did you say to her in the kitchen, the second time?

You look up. The hall is enormous, longer than it is wide, and the rows of desks run toward a stage so far away that the proctor standing on it is just a pale vertical mark, like a candle. Nobody else is looking up. Hundreds of heads bent, hundreds of pens moving, and the sound of the pens is a kind of rain.

You look back down. There is a second question underneath the first, and you are certain it was not there a moment ago.

Question 2. Why did you stop?

You know what the exam is for. You have always known, in the way you know things in dreams, which is to say the knowledge arrives already old. This is the final for the course, and the course was everything up to now, and the grade decides what you are permitted to become. You spent the night before revising. You remember revising. You remember the desk lamp, the highlighter, the cold tea. But you cannot remember what the course was called, and the textbook you studied has become, in memory, a book with no words in it, only page after page of your own face printed in halftone dots.

You uncap your pen. The pen is also yours, the one you lost in the second year, the one with the bite marks. You write under question one, and your hand writes the answer before you decide what it is.

I said I would call. I said it the way people say it when they mean the opposite.

That is correct. You know it is correct because the paper warms slightly under your palm, like something alive that has been reassured.

The clock on the wall does not have numbers. It has a single hand and the hand is a strand of hair, and it moves not in a circle but back and forth, like someone shaking their head slowly, no, no, no. You have no idea how much time is left. Nobody does. This is, you realize, part of the exam.

Question 3. Draw the house.

There is a large blank space below it. You draw the house. You draw it from the outside first, and it is easy, the two windows, the door, the crooked chimney every child draws, and then your hand keeps going and draws the inside without your permission, cutting away the front wall like a dollhouse, and there are the stairs with the third step that creaks and there is the bathroom with the pink tile and there in the kitchen is a small figure with its back turned, and you do not want to draw its face but the pen is moving toward it and you lift the pen. You lift the pen off the paper for the first time in your life at the right moment and there is no smudge.

The figure stays faceless. The paper goes cold.

Someone two rows ahead of you begins to cry. Not loudly. The proctor does not move. The rain of pens does not falter. The crying person is a man in a grey jumper and he is not writing anymore, he is holding the paper up in front of his face and reading it and his shoulders are going, and you understand that his questions are in his handwriting too, and that everyone's are, and that the hall is full of people being asked the only questions they have been avoiding, and that this is why the room is so quiet. It is not concentration. It is the sound of several hundred people being caught.

You turn the page.

The next page is a multiple choice section and you are relieved, the relief of the drowning man who finds a rung. But the choices are as follows:

Question 4. When you left, you were
(a) right
(b) right
(c) right
(d) afraid

You circle (d). It costs you something. You feel it leave you, a small weight, like a coin out of a pocket with a hole in it, and you hear it hit the floor under the desk and roll away, and you do not go after it.

Question 5. Name three things you were good at before you decided what you were good at.

You write: climbing trees. You write: listening. Then you sit for a long time with the pen above the third line, and the strand of hair on the clock shakes its head, and finally you write, in letters smaller than the others: being wrong out loud.

The proctor has come down from the stage. You did not see this happen. She is walking down the centre aisle, and she is not checking on anyone, she is just walking, and as she passes each row the people in it stop writing and put their pens down and sit very straight with their hands flat on the desk, the way you were taught to. She is closer now and you can see that she is wearing your mother's coat, the brown one with the missing button, but the face above it is nobody's, is a face left blank the way you left the figure in the kitchen blank, and this does not frighten you as much as it ought to. It seems fair. It seems like the exam being honest about what it is.

She stops at your row. She looks at your paper. You have the sensation of being read, of the sentences in your handwriting being lifted off the page and turned over like stones to see what is living underneath.

"You have one question left," she says, in no voice at all, and she puts a finger on the paper, and where she touches it the page turns itself.

Question 6.

There is nothing after it. Just the number and the full stop and the blank field below, ruled in faint blue lines, waiting.

"Where is the question," you say.

"You write the question," she says. "That is the question."

You look at the blank space. You think about the coin rolling away under the desk. You think about the faceless figure standing at the kitchen sink, and the man in the grey jumper, who has stopped crying now and is writing again, quickly, as if he has finally remembered something. You think about the course that was everything up to now and the career it decides, and you understand, with the old dream-knowledge, that there is no career. There was never a career. There is only the question of what you will be allowed to carry out of this room, and the answer is: whatever you are willing to write down.

You put the pen to the paper. You write the question you have never asked, the one you have arranged your whole life around not asking, and you cannot read it as you write it, the letters are appearing and dissolving like breath on glass, but your hand knows the shape and it does not smudge and it does not shake.

Then, below it, you begin to answer.

The hall gets brighter. Not from the windows, there are no windows, but from the paper itself, from all the papers, hundreds of small white lights in the long dark room, and the rain of pens becomes a different sound, softer, like the sound of many people at once letting out a breath they have been holding since a specific afternoon years ago. The proctor walks on. The strand of hair on the clock stops shaking its head and hangs straight down, still, at what you decide to call six o'clock, and you decide that means there is time.

You are still writing when you wake. Your hand is closed around nothing. On the inside of your wrist, in blue ballpoint, in your own handwriting, there is a single word you do not remember writing, and in the morning light it takes you a moment to read it, and when you read it you are not sure whether it is the question or the answer, and you find you do not need to know.
