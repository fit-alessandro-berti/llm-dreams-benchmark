Except no words come out. Instead, a slow, shimmering ribbon of golden light spills from your mouth, curling into the air like smoke and smelling faintly of rain and old books. 

A wave of panic hits your chest. You look down at your cue cards to find your place, only to realize you are holding a handful of dry autumn leaves. The wooden podium beneath your hands is softening, sprouting damp green moss, and the sleek black microphone has quietly bloomed into a heavy white lily. 

You look back up to apologize, bracing for laughter, but the auditorium has lost its walls. The rows of red velvet seats now stretch infinitely across a shallow, mirror-still ocean under a bruised purple twilight. And the audience isn’t made of strangers anymore—it’s a patchwork of your entire life. Your third-grade teacher sits beside the barista you saw yesterday; your childhood dog is in the front row wearing a neatly pressed suit, nodding encouragingly. 

None of them seem to mind the silence. In fact, they lean forward as if you are making a brilliant point.

Taking a deep breath, you try again. This time, the resonant, low hum of a cello vibrates straight from your chest, echoing across the water. With the sound, gravity loosens its grip. Your shoes lift an inch off the floorboards, then a foot. 

The crowd rises to give you a standing ovation, though the sound of their applause is indistinguishable from waves breaking against a distant shore—or maybe rain tapping against a bedroom window. You float effortlessly upward toward a sky crowded with three pale moons, entirely forgetting why you were ever nervous at all.
