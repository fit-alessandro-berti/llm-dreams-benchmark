You wake up in a completely different era, with no modern technology around. As you explore your new surroundings, you realize you need to find water before anything else, because your mouth is full of the taste of chalk and the light coming through the shutters is already the color of late afternoon.

The room is small and smells of tallow and wet wool. There is a bed that is not a bed but a wooden shelf with straw sewn into a sack, and you have been sleeping on it long enough that the straw has taken the shape of your back. On the wall hangs a bunch of dried herbs tied with red thread. You reach for your pocket out of habit and there is no pocket. There is a tunic, rough, dyed the brown of river mud, and a belt with a small knife on it that you do not remember owning but which fits your hand exactly.

Outside, the street is unpaved and slopes downward. You follow the slope because water is always downhill. People pass you carrying things: a woman with a basket of eels still moving, a boy leading a goat with a bell that does not ring, two men carrying a long beam between them who say something to you in a language you understand only after they have already gone past. You realize you answered them. You said, "The well is dry, try the one by the chapel," and you have no idea how you knew that.

The well by the chapel is not dry. It has a wooden cover and a rope that has been mended in four places. You lower the bucket and it takes a very long time to reach the bottom, longer than any well should be, and while you wait you notice that the chapel bell has no clapper and that the shadows of the people in the square are pointing in different directions, as if each of them had their own private sun.

The water, when it comes up, is cold and tastes of iron and of something else, something like the smell of a book that has been closed for a hundred years. You drink and drink. A girl of about nine watches you from the chapel steps. She has a slate in her lap and she is drawing on it with a piece of charcoal, and when you go closer you see she has drawn a rectangle with rounded corners, and inside the rectangle, small squares in rows.

"What is that?" you ask.

"It's the thing you were looking for when you came," she says. "You kept touching your leg for it. I thought I'd make you one so you'd stop."

You want to explain that it doesn't work that way, that the drawing is not the thing, but you are not sure anymore that this is true here. You take the slate. It is warm. When you press one of the small squares with your thumb the charcoal smudges and, somewhere very far off, a bell rings once, the bell that has no clapper.

"Now you've done it," the girl says, not unkindly, and goes inside.

You understand then that you need to find out what time it is. Not the hour, the year. You go into the chapel after her and it is much larger inside than outside, the way churches are in dreams, and the floor is covered in flagstones with names cut into them, and every name is yours. Different spellings. Different dates. Some of the dates are in the future. You walk across your own names toward the altar, where a man in a grey robe is copying something from one book into another with a feather.

"Which one is today?" you ask him, and gesture at the floor.

He does not look up. "The one you're standing on."

You look down. The stone under your feet is blank. It has not been cut yet. The feather in his hand is moving and you realize, with the slow horror that in dreams arrives as something closer to curiosity, that he is not copying. He is writing. And the book he is writing into is the floor, and the floor is going to have your name on it, and the date will be whatever date he decides it is.

"Wait," you say.

He waits. The feather stops. For the first time he looks at you, and his face is the face of the boy with the goat, and the woman with the eels, and the two men with the beam, all of them at once, arranged into one face the way you can arrange the same set of features into any stranger.

"You're the one who was going to tell us how it turns out," he says. "You came from there. That was the arrangement."

You open your mouth to tell him. You know so much. You know about the plague that will come by sea, the printing press, the way the sky will one day fill with lines of white from machines nobody here could name. You know how it all turns out. And you find that you cannot say any of it, not because your voice fails but because every fact, as you reach for it, has turned in your hand into something else: a smell, a color, the taste of the iron water, the weight of the girl's slate.

"I forgot," you say. "I'm sorry. I drank from the well."

He nods as if this is the answer he expected, perhaps the answer everyone gives. He dips the feather. He begins to write, and the letters appearing on the stone under your feet are not in any alphabet, they are the small squares in rows, the girl's drawing, a keyboard being pressed by no one.

You need to leave before he finishes. You know this the way you knew about the well. You back away across the floor of names and the flagstones lift slightly under each step like keys, and each one makes a note, and the notes are making a tune you half know, something from a car radio, something from a kitchen, something from a life that is on the other side of a door you cannot find.

At the chapel entrance the girl is waiting with a lantern, though it is not yet dark. "You'll want this," she says. "It gets dark early here. It gets dark and then it stays."

You take the lantern. You step outside. The square is empty and the shadows have all agreed at last on a single direction, and it is pointing at you. The street slopes upward now. Water is always downhill and you have already had your water, so you climb.

Halfway up you look back, and the chapel is gone, and the well is gone, and there is only the girl very small at the bottom of the hill, holding up her slate so you can see it. She has erased the rectangle. In its place she has drawn a bed, a proper one, with a pillow, and a window with light coming through it, and a small shape on the table beside the bed that could be anything, a stone, a book, a thing with rounded corners that will wake you.

You keep climbing. The lantern burns without oil. Behind you the bell with no clapper begins to ring the hour, and it rings and rings, more times than any hour has, and you count them going up the hill, and you are somewhere past forty when you realize you are counting the years, and you are almost there.
