You are about to take the final exam for a course that determines your future career. As you sit down to start the exam, you notice the questions are **not written on the page at all.**  

Instead, the paper is blank—soft, pale cream, like the inside of a seashell. And beside it, resting where a textbook would be, is a small, smooth stone you don’t recognize but feel you’ve held before. The examiner—someone you know vaguely from a past class, maybe your old TA, maybe just *a version of yourself from last year*—doesn’t wear a suit. They sit in a simple chair, hands folded, eyes calm, no clipboard. They don’t speak first.  

The first “question” appears not in ink, but as a gentle shift in the air: *a single, clear sound*—like a robin starting to sing outside a window you haven’t opened in months. Below it, in small, clear script that seems to grow as you read it:  
> *“Where, right now, in this room, do you feel the quietest kind of wanting? Not ‘should,’ not ‘must,’ just the soft pull—like a root finding a spot of soil. Describe it without naming the thing you think you’re supposed to want.”*  

You blink. No formulas. No timeline. No right answer written down. You feel your breath slow—no panic, just a small exhale you didn’t know you were holding. You glance at the stone in your peripheral vision. It’s cool, slightly rough where your thumb would touch it. You pick it up without thinking. It fits.  

The second “question” comes as a faint scent—wet earth after the first spring rain, mixed with the smell of old paper and warm tea. The text:  
> *“Tell me what you would notice if you sat with that quiet wanting for exactly five minutes—no fixing, no planning, just noticing. What does the light do on the floor? What sound does the chair make when you shift? What does your hand do with the stone?”*  

You sit. You don’t write fast. You write slowly, in simple sentences: *“The light is pale gold near the window, but softer by the wall. The chair creaks just once, low, when I lean forward a little. My thumb traces the smooth edge of the stone; it doesn’t change.”* You don’t try to make it profound. It feels true.  

The third “question” arrives as a small, warm spot on your wrist—like sunlight through glass—and the words:  
> *“If this wanting could choose one small, ordinary thing to keep doing—every week, for no reason other than it feels right—what would it be? (It cannot be ‘study harder’ or ‘get the job.’)”*  

You think: *Planting tiny basil seeds in recycled jars on the sill. Just once a week. Watching them grow.* You write: *“Put three basil seeds in a little jar. Water lightly. Look at them for two minutes when I pass the windowsill.”* No justification. No ‘why it matters.’ Just the action.  

Then the paper stays blank for a moment. No fourth question. No timer appears. The examiner nods—just once—and says, softly, *“That is the exam.”* They don’t grade it. They don’t read it aloud. They simply place a single dried lavender sprig beside your paper and the stone.  

You look up. The examiner’s face isn’t serious or distant; it’s the same warm, slightly tired ease you’ve seen in your old TA’s smile when they were explaining photosynthesis on a rainy Tuesday. They say, *“The course wasn’t about picking the career. It was about noticing where you already tend. The career will find the tenderness, if you keep tending it quietly.”*  

You don’t feel relief like a wave. You feel a quiet settling—like dust settling after wind stops. No sudden clarity about “the job.” Just a clear, soft knowing: *I already know what I would do, small and ordinary, if I stopped waiting for the right answer on the page.*  

You pick up the lavender sprig. It smells faintly of calm. You tuck it into the pocket of your chair. You pick up your pen—not to rush—and write one final line, not for the exam, but because it feels true:  
> *“I will put the basil seeds in the jar this week.”*  

The blank page doesn’t need more. The exam is over. The room stays soft light. Outside, the robin finishes its song. You don’t wake up. You just sit, breathing, holding the stone, and the quiet wanting feels like home.
