Then the rain doesn't fall—it arrives in a single, breathless rush. It drums against leaves, hammers the bark, and turns the path into a shallow, rushing stream. You tilt your head back, letting the cold wash over your face, and for a strange, dream-logic moment, the downpour doesn't drench you so much as it hums. Each drop sounds like a syllable, each flash of lightning stitches a silver thread across the dark. 

The storm pulls you forward, not with force, but with gravity. You step past a moss-draped stone you've never seen before, and the wind parts like a curtain. Beyond it, the forest opens into a clearing where the trees bow in perfect concentric rings. At the center rests something that shouldn't be there: a shallow glass basin filled not with water, but with slow-drifting ash and light, catching the storm's glow like a mirror suspended in the dark.

You kneel. The wind settles. The rain thins to a fine, silver mist. In the basin, the ash slows, then arranges itself into a shape you almost recognize—a door, a face, a map, a question. The storm holds its breath. And in that suspended quiet, you realize you've been walking toward this exact moment since before you stepped into the trees.
