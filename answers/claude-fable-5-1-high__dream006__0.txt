You are about to give a speech to a large audience. As you step onto the stage, you see the crowd looking expectantly at you, and you begin to notice that the lectern is much farther away than it should be. You walk toward it. The stage is polished wood, very dark, and it reflects the ceiling lights as long white bars that slide ahead of you as you move. You walk for what feels like a full minute and the lectern does not get closer, and no one in the audience seems to find this strange. They wait. Some of them have programs open on their laps. You can hear the paper.

Then you are at the lectern, without having arrived, the way you are always suddenly at places here. You put your hands on either side of it. There are no notes. You knew there would be no notes, you remember now, because you had decided to speak from memory, and you feel a small proud warmth about this decision even as you realize you cannot remember what the speech is about.

You look down at the wood of the lectern and there is something written on it, carved shallowly, the way people carve into desks in school. It says YOUR NAME. Not your name. The words YOUR NAME. You understand this is a placeholder that somebody forgot to fill in.

The microphone is a thin black stalk and when you lean toward it, it leans away from you slightly, like a plant bending from a hand. You lean again and it bends again. You stop leaning. Fine. You will speak without it. The hall is very large, tiered, with balconies going up into a dimness where you can only see the pale ovals of faces, hundreds of them, and above the top balcony there is no ceiling, just more faces, fainter and fainter, until they become the texture of the dark.

You open your mouth and say, "Thank you all for coming."

Your voice is enormous. It goes out into the hall and comes back to you from all sides, and it is not quite your voice, it is deeper, it has a radio quality, a voice that has been trained. The audience relaxes visibly. Some of them nod. A woman in the third row writes something down. You feel encouraged, and then you feel afraid, because now you have to say the next thing.

"I want to begin," you say, "with a story."

You do not have a story. But the voice keeps going, and you listen to it the way you would listen to someone else.

"When I was a child," the voice says, "there was a room in our house that we did not use."

This is true. You had forgotten it entirely until the voice said it, but it is true. There was a room. It was off the upstairs hallway and the door was always closed and it was not forbidden, exactly, nobody ever told you not to go in, it was just that no one did. You remember the door now with tremendous clarity: painted white, with a brass knob that had gone brown, and a small hook-and-eye latch too high for you to reach.

"Every house has one," the voice says, and the audience laughs, warmly, a laugh of recognition. You did not intend it as a joke. You are not sure it was one.

You look out at them while the voice continues without you. You are looking for someone you know. You have the feeling that there is someone you know out there, that this is the reason you agreed to speak, and if you can find them the rest will be easy. You scan the rows. The faces are all attentive and all unfamiliar and all, you begin to notice, slightly the same. Not identical. But they have the same quality of listening. They all tilt their heads at the same angle. When one blinks, the ones near it blink a fraction of a second later, so that a blink travels across the hall in a slow ripple, like wind over a field.

"So one day I opened the door," the voice says.

You did. You remember this. You were nine, or eleven, some odd number, and it was a summer afternoon and the house was empty in the way houses are empty at three in the afternoon, and you stood on a chair and undid the hook.

The voice stops.

You wait for it to go on, and it does not. The hall is completely silent. Not even paper. You realize the voice has gone as far as your memory goes, that it was never speaking from anywhere but you, and that you do not remember what was in the room.

The pause stretches. It is not yet a bad pause. An audience will give you about four seconds. You use two of them looking at the woman in the third row, who has stopped writing and is looking back at you with her pen lifted, and you notice for the first time that her program, open on her lap, is blank. All of the programs are blank. Every person in the hall is holding a folded sheet of clean white paper.

"And inside," you say, in your own voice now, small and unamplified, and you hear how small it is, how it barely makes it past the first row, "inside there was —"

Someone in the balcony coughs. The cough goes up and up through the tiers of faces.

You try to see the room. You try to walk into it, in your mind, to stand on the threshold with the chair behind you and the hook swinging on its eye and look in. And you can see the light, a very yellow light, afternoon light coming through a window with the blind half down, and you can see dust in the light, and the floor, bare boards, and a rectangle on the floor where a rug used to be, paler than the wood around it.

"There was a lot of light," you say.

The audience waits. It is not enough. You know it is not enough. A room full of light is not a story, a story is what is in the light.

And then you understand, with the clarity that only comes here, that the reason you cannot remember what was in the room is that you are in it. That the hall is the room. That the tiers of faces going up into the dark are the dust rising in the yellow light, and the polished floor of the stage is the pale rectangle where the rug used to be, and you are standing exactly where you stood when you were nine or eleven, and you have never left, and the door behind you with the hook swinging is the door you came through onto the stage. You have been giving this speech since three in the afternoon of a summer day a long time ago. That is why they all have the same face. That is why the programs are blank. There was never anything written for this.

You turn around to look at the door and it is there, white, with the brown brass knob, standing by itself in the middle of the stage with no wall around it. It is closed. The hook is latched from this side.

You turn back to the audience. They are still waiting. They are very patient. They will wait, you realize, as long as you need, and this is worse than if they had begun to leave.

"I'm sorry," you say. "I've lost my place."

And the woman in the third row nods, as though this were the speech, as though this were exactly the line she had been waiting to write down, and she writes it, and you watch her pen move across the blank page, and the whole hall begins to applaud, softly, thousands of hands, a sound like rain starting on a roof, and it goes on, and on, and it does not get louder, and it does not stop, and you stand at the lectern with your hands on the carved words and wait for it to end so that you can finish, though you know now that you will not be finishing, that the applause is the end, and it has been going on the whole time, underneath everything, and you are only now able to hear it.
