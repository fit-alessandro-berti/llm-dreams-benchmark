Not because you have nothing left to say, but because a small blue fish has begun swimming between you, suspended in the air. Every time either of you raises your voice, it grows larger.

Your friend notices it too. Their anger falters.

“That wasn’t here before,” they say.

“It might have been,” you answer.

The fish circles the kitchen light. Inside its translucent body, you see flickers of the afternoon you met, the terrible apartment you helped each other paint, a message you never answered. Soon it is too large for the room. Its tail brushes the cupboards, shaking loose a soft rain of teaspoons.

You want to explain why you’re right. Instead, you ask, “What did you think I meant?”

Your friend looks down at their hands.

“That I didn’t matter enough to ask.”

The kitchen wall folds outward, revealing a dark shoreline. You can still feel the argument in your chest, stubborn and hot, but beside it is the memory of making a decision alone because you were afraid they would say no.

“I should have asked,” you say. “I still don’t know if I could have done what you wanted.”

“I know.”

Together, you carry the fish toward the water. It is lighter than you expect and difficult to hold. Your friend loses their grip once; you nearly drop it laughing, then stop, unsure whether laughter is allowed yet.

But they are laughing too.

At the shore, you lower your hands. The fish slips away beneath the surface, briefly lighting the stones under your feet.

You stand beside your friend in the cold shallows. There is still something you need to tell them. As you search for the words, they reach down and retrieve one of the teaspoons from your shoe.
