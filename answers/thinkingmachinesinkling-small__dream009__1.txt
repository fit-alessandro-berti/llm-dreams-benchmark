You wake up in a completely different era, with no modern technology around. As you explore your new surroundings, you realize you need to **find water and shelter before nightfall—because your phone is just a cold, dead rectangle in your pocket, and the sky is turning a strange, un-phone-lit shade of amber.**

The air smells like woodsmoke, wet wool, and something sweet that might be bread or honey. You’re standing in a village that feels like a painting that’s been slightly smudged: half-timbered houses with crooked roofs, mud streets instead of asphalt, and horses instead of cars. The clothes people wear are thick and rough—linen, leather, fur. When they notice you, they stare not at your face, but at your sneakers and your synthetic shirt. You are an anachronism walking through them.

You try to ask for directions, but your voice sounds too loud, too sharp, like a radio broadcast in a quiet room. They don’t understand you. You realize the language has shifted too—words feel almost right, but the grammar is fluid, strange.

So you do what your modern brain tells you: you look for a map, a GPS signal, anything. Nothing. The only “signal” is your thirst. You are hungry, dehydrated, and the sun is already low.

You enter the nearest building that smells like warmth—a tavern, though it has no electric lights, just oil lamps flickering gold against stone walls. Inside, people sit on wooden benches, drinking from clay mugs. A woman with sun-weathered skin notices your empty hands. She offers you a wooden bowl of cool water and a dense, dark bread. You accept. For a moment, the dream-logic softens: you don’t need to translate your hunger into data. You just need to eat.

But then you see it. On the wall, a clock—an ornate brass one, ticking with a mechanical heart—shows a time that doesn’t exist in your memory. The hands move backward, slowly, and when they align with the moon rising outside, the room seems to breathe. The people freeze, turning into shadows, and the floor beneath you shifts like water.

You realize the true need: **you don’t just need to survive. You need to find the clock’s chime.** It’s the only doorway back.

You take the bread, not out of hunger, but as an offering or a key. You step back into the cool night air. The stars are wrong—too close, arranged in patterns your phone once used to unlock itself. You follow the sound of that ticking to the village square, where a stone tower stands like a needle sewn into the earth.

As you climb, your modern thoughts dissolve into the dream’s language. You are not lost; you are translating yourself. At the top, the clock strikes. It doesn’t sound like time. It sounds like your alarm clock from this morning, but slowed down, made of bells and wind.

And when it stops—

You don’t wake up in your bed.

You open your eyes in the digital glow of your phone’s charging screen, heart hammering, the taste of woodsmoke and dark bread still on your tongue. For a second, you aren’t sure which era is real.

You look outside your window. The streets are still asphalt. The cars are still humming.

But the clock on your phone is blinking. And in the dream’s afterglow, you realize you have what you need now: not the technology, but the memory of how to survive without it.

You get up, make coffee with real fire or electricity—whatever this era offers—and you carry one thing back with you: the certainty that you can adapt, even when the signals go dead.

**The dream ends not with a return, but with a choice: do you chase the clock back into the past, or do you stay, bringing the old wisdom with you?**

*(And if you do choose to go back, the bread is still warm, and the tower is waiting for your heartbeat rather than your password.)*
