At first, we are sitting in a sterile, fluorescent-lit waiting room, clutching resumes printed on heavy cardstock. Beside me is a man in a pinstriped suit made entirely of folded newspapers, and a woman whose shadow keeps pacing nervously even though she is sitting perfectly still. When my name is called—though it doesn’t sound like my name, it sounds like the chime of a grandfather clock—the doors open not into an office, but onto a vast, floating archipelago of polished mahogany desks drifting through a twilight sky.

The first challenge is waiting on the nearest desk: a typewriter with no keys, only small pools of liquid color, and a prompt hovering in the air that reads, *Translate the silence before a thunderstorm.* 

In the strange, unquestioning logic of dreams, I know exactly what to do. I dip my fingers into the indigo and silver pools, and as I touch the paper, the page ripples like water. Words don't appear; instead, the scent of ozone and damp earth rises from the parchment, and a gentle static makes my hair stand on end. Around me, other competitors are failing—the newspaper man is desperately trying to staple gusts of wind together, and he slowly dissolves into a flurry of confetti, drifting harmlessly down into the clouds below. 

Before I can catch my breath, the mahogany desk stretches and hardens into a narrow glass bridge. We are in the semi-finals now. The judges appear for the first time, towering silhouettes made of constellations and ticking brass gears. Their voices overlap like an echo in a canyon: *To claim the role, you must organize the Archive of Forgotten Things.*

Suddenly, I am knee-deep in a sea of lost moments. There are half-remembered melodies humming like luminescent fireflies, the misplaced keys of a thousand strangers, and the bittersweet ache of childhood summers trapped inside dusty glass marbles. Panic sets in—how can I possibly sort infinity? My feet feel encased in lead, that classic dream-heaviness threatening to pull me under the clutter. 

Then, I stop fighting the chaos. Instead of building shelves, I begin weaving the melodies and the marbles together, realizing they don't want to be separated—they want to be a story. As I connect a lost lullaby to a faded photograph, the entire room illuminates. The heavy lead feeling vanishes, replaced by weightlessness.

Suddenly, everyone else is gone. The constellations lean down, so close I can hear the hum of their starlight. In their hands, they hold a golden key that is also, somehow, a compass. 

*Are you ready to begin?* they ask. 

It hits me all at once what my dream job actually is. I am not meant to sit in an office, write code, or manage spreadsheets. I am applying to be the Architect of Horizons—the one who paints the sky just before people wake up, and leaves trails of wonder tucked into the corners of the world. 

I reach out to take the compass-key. The moment the warm metal touches my palm, a door made of pure morning light swings open in the middle of the sky. I take a breath, step across the threshold to start my first day, and—
