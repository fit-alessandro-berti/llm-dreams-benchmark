...written in a language you've never seen before, yet somehow you can almost read it. The letters shift when you look directly at them, rearranging themselves like ants avoiding a footstep. Question one seems to say *"Explain the color of the sound your mother makes when she's proud of you."* Or maybe it says *"Calculate the weight of a Tuesday."* You're not sure. You're sure you studied for this.

You look up for help. The proctor at the front of the room is your third-grade teacher, except she's also somehow a lighthouse. She sweeps her beam across the rows of desks, and wherever the light touches, the students turn briefly into birds and then back again. No one seems to mind. You raise your hand, but your hand is holding a pencil that has grown to the length of a fishing rod, and it bends heavily toward the floor.

"You have forty minutes," the lighthouse announces. The clock on the wall says 11:73.

You decide to just start writing. The moment the pencil touches paper, the words flow out easily, beautifully, and in your own handwriting, but they aren't answers. They're a letter to someone you haven't spoken to in years. *I'm sorry I never called back,* you write. *I kept meaning to.* You try to stop, to get back to the exam, but the pencil keeps going, and the page keeps getting longer, unrolling off the desk and across the floor like a scroll.

The student beside you leans over. It's you, but older, maybe sixty, with kind eyes and paint on their fingers. "You know this isn't the real test, right?" they whisper.

"Then what is?"

They shrug and point toward the window. Outside, the parking lot has become an ocean, and there's a small wooden boat tied to the bike rack. It has your name painted on the side, spelled slightly wrong.

You look back at your exam. The questions have changed. Now there's only one, and it's in perfectly clear English:

**"What did you actually want to be?"**

The lighthouse-teacher sweeps her beam toward you. The room goes quiet. Every bird-student turns to look. You open your mouth to answer, and what comes out is the sound of a bell, low and warm, ringing somewhere far off across the water.

The bell keeps ringing.

It becomes your alarm.

You wake up with the answer still on your tongue, fading fast, like the taste of something sweet. You lie there for a moment trying to hold onto it, and even though the words are gone, the feeling stays with you all morning: the strange, calm certainty that you already know.
