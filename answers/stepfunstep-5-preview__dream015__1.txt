Despite the hardships, you decide to plant the seeds anyway.

You kneel on the shoulder of Route 9, where the asphalt has split open like a wound healing wrong. The world around you is a graveyard of steel and glass—skeletal skyscrapers leaning against each other like exhausted giants, the air thick with ash that never quite settles. Your canteen is half-empty. Your boots are held together with tape and prayer. Three days ago, you watched a pack of feral dogs—or something that had once been dogs—tear apart a man in a hazmat suit two blocks over.

But in your pocket, wrapped in cloth, are three seeds. You don't know what they'll grow into. Maybe nothing. Maybe wheat. Maybe poison. In a world like this, the difference doesn't always matter.

You use your fingers to dig. The soil is unexpectedly soft, almost warm, and when you bury the seeds, the earth seems to sigh and close over them like an eyelid. You pour the last of your water over the patch, watching it darken the dust.

That's when you notice the silence.

Usually, the wind carries sounds through the ruins—metal creaking, distant coughing, the scuttle of things that shouldn't scuttle. Now, there's nothing. You turn.

Standing ten feet away is a deer. It shouldn't exist. Not here. Its coat is too clean, too white, and between its antlers grows a small tree—no older than a sapling, with leaves that shimmer like coins. The deer looks at you with eyes the color of summer, and somehow you know it isn't dangerous. Not to you. Not now.

You feel a vibration in your chest. Looking down, you see green shoots pushing up through the cracks in the road, not just where you planted, but everywhere—unfurling with impossible speed, weaving through the rebar, blooming with flowers that glow faintly in the twilight. The post-apocalyptic world begins to fold in on itself, not ending, but transforming. The ruins are becoming trellises. The tanks are becoming ponds. The sky shifts from gray to amber.

You sit back on your heels and laugh, because you understand, in the way you only understand things in dreams, that the world didn't need to be saved by heroes or rebuilt by armies. It just needed someone stubborn enough to plant something in the rubble and refuse to leave.

Then the wind picks up, carrying the smell of rain—real rain, not ash—and as the first drops hit your face, you feel yourself becoming lighter, rising above the greening world, until the road, the deer, and the three seeds shrink to a single point of light.

And you wake.
