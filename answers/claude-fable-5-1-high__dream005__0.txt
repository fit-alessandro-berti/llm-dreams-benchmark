You are at a large social gathering where you only know one person. As you try to find them, you encounter various groups of people and each group seems to already know you, or to know someone who is almost you.

The first group is standing around a table that has no legs. It floats at the height of a table anyway, because that is where tables belong. A woman in a green dress hands you a glass and says, "You came. We didn't think you'd come after what happened." You do not know what happened. You say, "Of course I came," and they all laugh warmly, and the laughter goes on a little too long, and you realize they are waiting for you to say something else, something you were supposed to have prepared. You excuse yourself. You say you're looking for someone. "Aren't we all," says a man whose face you can only see in profile, no matter which side of him you walk to.

The house is bigger on the inside than it looked from the road, but you can't remember the road. You remember arriving. You remember a coat, and giving the coat to someone, and now you don't have it and you feel the absence of it like a missing tooth. You keep touching your shoulders where the coat would be.

The second group is in a kitchen. They are all sitting on the counters, legs dangling, like children. One of them is your third-grade teacher, but she is your age now, and she is drinking wine out of a measuring cup. "You never turned in the map," she says, and it's true, you remember it now, the map of a country you were supposed to invent, and you feel a hot rush of shame decades late. "I'm still working on it," you tell her. She nods as though this is an acceptable answer and points down the hallway. "He went that way," she says, and you didn't tell her who you were looking for, but she's right, it is a he, and he did go that way.

The hallway is lined with doors and every door has a party behind it. You can hear them, the way you can hear the ocean in a shell, a general roar with individual voices surfacing and sinking. You open one door and it's a room full of people all looking at their phones, and every phone shows the same photograph, and the photograph is of this room, taken from where you are standing, and you are in it, opening the door, looking in. You close the door quietly, like closing it on a sleeping child.

You find a staircase. On the staircase there is a group of people sitting on the steps the way people sit on steps at parties, knees up, drinks balanced, blocking the way but friendly about it. They shift to let you pass and as you go up between them they say things to you, low and kind, things like "You're doing fine" and "Nobody noticed" and "It wasn't your fault about the dog," and you don't know what dog and you are suddenly afraid to know. One of them touches your ankle as you go by and says, "He's on the roof," and you keep climbing.

The stairs go up for a long time. Longer than the house is tall. You pass a landing where a string quartet is playing a song you knew as a child, but slowed down so much that each note becomes a room you have to walk through. You pass a window and outside it is daytime, and then you pass another window and outside it is night, and through a third window you see the party itself, from above, the whole lit house in the dark like a ship, and you understand you are somehow both inside and outside it.

At the top there's a door that opens onto the roof, and the roof is a lawn. Of course it is. There are people on the lawn too, lying on their backs looking up, and the sky above them is very close, close enough that you feel you could reach up and touch its underside, and it has a texture, like the ceiling of a tent.

And there he is. Your one person. He's standing at the edge of the roof with his back to you, hands in his pockets, looking out. You feel a relief so enormous it is almost grief. You walk toward him across the grass and you say his name, and he turns around.

He looks at you politely. The way you look at a stranger who has said your name in a crowd.

"I'm sorry," he says. "Do we know each other?"

And you open your mouth to say yes, to say of course, to say all the things you know about him, the year you met, the apartment with the broken radiator, the joke about the lighthouse, but the words are gone. Not forgotten. Gone, the way the coat is gone, given to someone at the door. You stand there with your mouth open and the wind moving in your hair and you realize that everyone here knows you, everyone here has been kind to you, everyone here has forgiven you for things you don't remember doing, and the only person you came for is the only one who has never seen you before in his life.

"That's all right," he says gently, as if he's the one comforting you. "It's a big party. It happens." He gestures at the lawn, the lying-down people, the low tented sky. "Stay anyway. You're already here."

Down below, through the floor of the roof, you can hear them laughing, the warm laugh, the one that goes on a little too long. Somewhere a woman in green is telling a story about you. Somewhere your teacher is waiting for the map.

You sit down on the grass beside him. You look up. The sky is very close. You realize you could stay here, that this is a place you could stay, that no one would ask you to leave, that they would go on knowing you and forgiving you for as long as you were willing to be known.

You are still trying to remember the joke about the lighthouse when you wake up, and for a moment, lying in the dark, you almost have it.
