The first question asks you to calculate the exact weight of a decision you made in elementary school, and the multiple-choice answers are printed in different fonts: **A:** 3 lbs. **B:** the color blue. **C:** your mother’s phone number. **D:** all of the above. When you hover your pencil over the paper to answer, you realize the desk is made of thin ice, and beneath it stretches a very still, very dark ocean.

Your number two pencil has become an osprey feather.

You look up to ask the proctor for clarification, but the proctor is wearing your face—only older, with the kind of exhaustion that comes from decades of a job you have not started yet. They do not speak. They simply tap their wrist, where a watch should be, but instead there is a small window, and through it you can see weather you have not yet experienced.

The exam booklet is warm. It heats up when you are afraid. Question four instructs you to draw a map to a house you have never lived in, but as soon as you sketch the roof, the lines cut into the desk, and the room begins to tilt. The other students are not people you know; they are versions of yourself at different ages, all chewing on identical pens, all sweating the same sweat. No one looks up. The clock on the wall has no numbers—just photographs of doors.

You decide to answer honestly. For the final question—*“Who will you be when the world changes shape?”*—you write the truth: that you are still here, still holding this instrument, still sitting. 

When you finish, the ink does not dry. It lifts off the page like smoke, or birds, or a swarm of very organized ants, and forms a shape in the air above the exam. The shape looks something like a door. Something like a lung. Something like an answer.

The proctor—your future self—nods once. Not in approval, but in recognition. They turn the page of their own booklet.

“Begin,” they say, though you already have.
