…written in a language that keeps shifting between your native tongue and symbols that feel like déjà vu made visible. The first question asks you to calculate the exact velocity at which your childhood innocence fell away, measured in the sound of a door closing. The second instructs you to list, in order of importance, all the versions of yourself that you abandoned in hallways you’ve never actually walked.

You try to write an answer, but your pen begins to hum, vibrating with a frequency that makes your teeth ache. The proctor—a figure wearing your own face, but younger, with eyes that haven’t yet learned to doubt—leans over your shoulder and whispers, *"The correct answer is already inside you, but you have to forget where you left it."*

The other students around you are writing furiously, but their papers are blank. They’re not writing answers; they’re drawing maps of places that don’t exist on any globe, routes to futures they haven’t decided to want yet. The clock on the wall has no hands, only a single tear-shaped droplet of mercury that slides slowly toward twelve, though you can’t remember if you’re counting up to the exam or away from it.

You look down at your own paper. The questions have rearranged themselves into a single sentence, written in your own handwriting but smudged as if underwater: *What will you be when you stop pretending to know?*
